## A.1 Behavioural Emergencies — Introduction — Tutorial 1

### Question 1

**"Evaluate the role of psychological assessment in emergency medical contexts, including how mental status examinations (MSE) and cognitive screening inform acute management and disposition decisions."**

Psychological assessment in the emergency setting serves to identify immediately dangerous states (suicidality, homicidality, acute psychosis, delirium, intoxication and withdrawal), to distinguish organic from functional causes of abnormal behaviour, to assess decision-making capacity, and to determine a safe disposition (Boland, Verduin and Ruiz, 2021). The Mental State Examination provides a structured, objective description of appearance, behaviour, speech, mood, affect, thought form and content (including suicidal and homicidal ideation), perception, cognition, insight and judgement at a single point in time (Outcome 40.1). Cognitive screening tools such as the MMSE (Folstein, Folstein and McHugh, 1975) and the MoCA (Nasreddine et al., 2005) quantify orientation, attention, memory, language and visuospatial function, and impaired orientation and attention point towards delirium, dementia or intoxication rather than a primary psychiatric illness, which in turn directs the clinician towards urgent medical investigation (glucose, oxygenation, head injury, infection, toxins). Delirium specifically requires a dedicated tool such as the CAM, because a single MMSE cannot capture acute onset, fluctuation and inattention (Inouye et al., 1990). These assessments inform acute management (for example whether sedation is needed, whether the patient needs medical rather than psychiatric care, and whether the patient may refuse treatment) and disposition (emergency centre versus psychiatric facility; voluntary versus involuntary care under the MHCA; safe discharge with follow-up). Their limitations in the emergency setting are that they require a cooperative patient, take time, are influenced by language, education and culture (a major limitation in South Africa), and cannot replace a decision-specific capacity assessment or a suicide risk assessment (Outcome 40). In prehospital practice, the CDST requires the provider never to assume a psychiatric cause and to check glucose, oxygen saturation and blood pressure in every agitated patient (HPCSA PBEC, 2025, p. 47), and Stander, Hodkinson and Dippenaar (2021) caution against using the GCS as a substitute for capacity assessment.

### Question 2

**"Discuss the phenomenology and clinical significance of Specific Phobia, Social Anxiety Disorder, and OCD as part of the anxiety spectrum. How would you differentiate these conditions diagnostically and what are the implications for management in emergency medical care settings?"**

**Specific phobia** is marked fear or anxiety about a specific object or situation (for example needles, blood, heights, animals) that is out of proportion to the actual danger, is actively avoided or endured with intense distress, and persists for six months or longer; the blood-injection-injury subtype is clinically significant because it can cause vasovagal syncope during procedures (APA, 2022). **Social anxiety disorder** is marked fear of social situations in which the person may be scrutinised by others, with fear of acting in a way that will be negatively evaluated (humiliation, embarrassment, rejection). **Obsessive-compulsive disorder** — which DSM-5-TR now places in a separate chapter of obsessive-compulsive and related disorders rather than among the anxiety disorders — consists of obsessions (recurrent, intrusive, unwanted thoughts, urges or images that cause anxiety and that the person tries to neutralise) and/or compulsions (repetitive behaviours or mental acts performed to reduce the anxiety or according to rigid rules), which take more than an hour a day or cause significant impairment, with insight that may be good, poor or absent (APA, 2022). **Differentiation** rests on the focus of the fear: a circumscribed object or situation (specific phobia), social evaluation (social anxiety disorder), or intrusive thoughts with neutralising rituals that are not limited to one feared stimulus (OCD). All three must be distinguished from substance- and medical-induced anxiety (for example stimulant use, hyperthyroidism). **Emergency implications**: specific phobias may cause panic or syncope during injections, cannulation or confinement in an ambulance, so procedures should be explained, the patient allowed to lie down, and distraction used; social anxiety may limit the history the patient will give in front of others, so a private setting is helpful; OCD rituals (for example handwashing, checking) may delay cooperation, and these can usually be accommodated if safe while maintaining infection control. Comorbid depression and suicidality must be screened for. Long-term management consists of CBT with exposure (phobias and OCD, with exposure and response prevention for OCD) and SSRIs (OCD and social anxiety disorder); benzodiazepines are rarely appropriate and only for short-term, acute anxiolysis (Craske and Stein, 2016).

### Question 3

**"Critically assess the interplay between OCD symptomatology and stigma in clinical and community settings. How can healthcare professionals counteract stigma to improve engagement and adherence to treatment?"**

OCD symptoms are often secret, embarrassing and ego-dystonic (for example intrusive violent, sexual or blasphemous thoughts), which makes people fearful of being judged as dangerous or "mad", while the trivialisation of OCD in popular culture ("I'm so OCD about neatness") minimises the suffering involved. Stigma operates as **public stigma** (prejudice and discrimination from others), **self-stigma** (internalised shame) and **anticipated stigma** (fear of discrimination), and results in concealment of symptoms, delayed help-seeking (often for many years), reduced adherence to treatment, social withdrawal and worse outcomes (Tutorial memo). In clinical settings, stigma may also arise from staff who are impatient with rituals or who misinterpret intrusive thoughts as intent. In the South African context, mental illness stigma is reinforced by limited mental health literacy and resources, and negative attitudes have been documented even among prehospital providers towards patients with mental health crises (Evans, Geduld and Stassen, 2018). Health professionals can counteract stigma by adopting a compassionate, non-judgemental approach; using person-first, non-stigmatising language; providing psychoeducation that explains OCD as a common, treatable neurobiological and cognitive condition and that intrusive thoughts do not reflect a desire to act; normalising and explaining evidence-based treatments (exposure and response prevention, SSRIs) and their side effects; involving families; providing culturally sensitive care; linking patients with peer support; and advocating for mental health parity and public awareness. In emergency care, respectful accommodation of rituals where safe, and avoidance of dismissive comments, preserve trust and future engagement.

### Question 4

**"Evaluate pharmacological and psychotherapeutic treatment options for Generalised Anxiety Disorder (GAD) and Panic Disorder, including first-line medications, psychotherapy approaches, and considerations for comorbidity and safety."**

First-line pharmacological treatments for both GAD and panic disorder are **SSRIs** (for example sertraline, escitalopram, paroxetine) and **SNRIs** (for example venlafaxine, duloxetine), which take several weeks to work, may initially increase anxiety (so low starting doses are used), and should be continued for months after remission (Craske and Stein, 2016). Pregabalin and buspirone are alternatives for GAD. **Benzodiazepines** provide rapid anxiolysis but are limited to short-term use because of tolerance, dependence, withdrawal, cognitive impairment, falls in older people, and dangerous synergy with alcohol and opioids. The best-supported psychological treatment for both disorders is **cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT)** — cognitive restructuring of catastrophic thoughts, with interoceptive and in vivo **exposure** being particularly important in panic disorder — and mindfulness-based and applied relaxation therapies are additional options; psychotherapy and medication are comparably effective, and combination may be used in severe cases. **Comorbidity** is common (depression, other anxiety disorders, substance and alcohol use), and treatment should be integrated; **safety** considerations include screening for suicidality (anxiety disorders increase suicide risk, especially with comorbid depression), avoiding benzodiazepines in patients with substance use disorders, monitoring for serotonin syndrome with combinations of serotonergic drugs (60.28), and remembering that SSRIs can increase suicidal thinking in young people early in treatment. In emergency practice, a "panic attack" is a diagnosis of exclusion: the ECP must exclude ACS, PE, arrhythmias, asthma, hypoglycaemia, thyrotoxicosis and stimulant toxicity with vital signs, SpO₂, glucose and a 12-lead ECG before attributing symptoms to anxiety (Outcome 42).

### Question 5

**"Explain the DSM-5-TR diagnostic criteria for bipolar I disorder and contrast it with bipolar II disorder, emphasising the clinical implications for treatment and prognosis."**

Bipolar I disorder requires **at least one lifetime manic episode** — a distinct period of abnormally and persistently elevated, expansive or irritable mood **and** increased goal-directed activity or energy, lasting **at least seven days** (or any duration if hospitalisation is necessary), present most of the day nearly every day, with **three or more** of the following (**four** if the mood is only irritable): inflated self-esteem or grandiosity, decreased need for sleep, pressured speech, flight of ideas or racing thoughts, distractibility, increased goal-directed activity or psychomotor agitation, and excessive involvement in risky activities; the episode causes marked impairment, requires hospitalisation or has psychotic features, and is not due to a substance or medical condition (APA, 2022). Major depressive episodes commonly occur but are **not required** for bipolar I. **Bipolar II** requires at least one **hypomanic episode** (the same symptoms for at least **four consecutive days**, with an observable change in functioning but **without** marked impairment, hospitalisation or psychosis) **and** at least one **major depressive episode**, with **no history of mania**. Clinically, bipolar I carries a higher risk of psychosis, severe functional impairment, dangerous behaviour during mania and hospitalisation, whereas bipolar II is dominated by recurrent and often prolonged depressive episodes, frequently misdiagnosed as unipolar depression. Both carry a **high suicide risk**, particularly during depressive and mixed episodes. Treatment includes mood stabilisers (lithium, valproate, carbamazepine, lamotrigine) and atypical antipsychotics, with psychotherapy and psychoeducation; antidepressants are used cautiously and only with a mood stabiliser because of the risk of switching to mania or rapid cycling (Vieta et al., 2018). Emergency implications include managing manic agitation (CDST sedation if dangerous), exclusion of stimulant use and thyrotoxicosis, recognition of **lithium toxicity** (60.27), and suicide risk assessment (52.4).

### Question 6

**"Analyse the potential consequences of DAMA (discharge against medical advice) in psychiatric emergencies and relate to prehospital decision-making."**

Patients who leave psychiatric care against medical advice show reduced treatment benefit, worse psychiatric, medical, psychosocial and socioeconomic functioning, decreased access to outpatient services, overuse of emergency services and earlier readmission (Brook et al., 2006, cited by Evans, Geduld and Stassen, 2018), and psychiatric DAMA is a specific risk factor for **suicide** (Kuo et al., 2010, cited by Evans, Geduld and Stassen, 2018). In prehospital care, the equivalent is the **refusal of hospital treatment (RHT)** on scene. Evans, Geduld and Stassen (2018) found that South African providers frequently accepted refusals from suicidal patients, believed that patients could never be transported without consent, and believed that a signed refusal absolved them of responsibility; several had personally experienced a patient's death after leaving them on scene. The consequences of an inappropriate prehospital refusal therefore include death or serious harm to the patient, moral distress and trauma for the provider, legal liability (negligence) and disciplinary action, and repeated calls. Prehospital decision-making must therefore include: a careful, documented **capacity assessment**; a structured **suicide and violence risk assessment**; recognition that a refusal driven by mental illness is not a valid autonomous choice; application of **MHCA s 9(1)(c)** (care without consent due to mental illness where delay may cause death, serious harm to self or others, or serious property damage) and the **CDST obligation to transport** a patient who is a danger to themselves or may be a suicide risk (HPCSA PBEC, 2025, p. 48); involvement of family, SAPS (s 40) and senior advice; use of the least restrictive means; and, where a competent patient lawfully refuses, documentation of the information given, safety-netting and referral to community resources (Outcomes 45–47 and 50).

### Question 7

**"Critically assess the ethical tensions embedded in the HPCSA Guidelines for the Withholding and Withdrawing of Treatment when applied to emergency prehospital care of suicidal patients."**

A full answer is given in Outcome 45.1. In summary, Booklet 7 was written to guide decisions about withholding or withdrawing life-prolonging treatment in patients with terminal illness or little hope of recovery, and it explicitly prohibits killing, active euthanasia and assisted suicide (HPCSA, 2023, para 2.2). Its emphasis on **respect for autonomy** — that a competent adult's refusal must be respected even if it results in death (para 8.1.2) — creates a tension with the duty of **beneficence** to prevent a suicide, but the same paragraph contains the critical exception: the refusal is not binding where the patient's "state of mind is such that he/she is incapable of taking or participating in the decision", which is frequently the case in a suicidal crisis. **Non-maleficence** is engaged because preventing suicide may require coercive measures (restraint, sedation, involuntary transport) that themselves cause harm and must therefore be proportionate. The guideline's emphasis on **consultation**, second opinions, involvement of family and proxies, and documentation (paras 3.5, 3.7, 5.2, 12) is difficult to achieve under prehospital time pressure, uncertainty and limited information, but paragraph 7.1 resolves this for emergencies by directing practitioners to start treatment that may be of benefit until a clearer assessment can be made, with the patient informed later (para 7.2). A suicide note is not a valid **advance directive**, which presupposes a competent person planning for terminal illness, and a directive cannot compel care outside acceptable standards (para 9.1.4). **Resource** considerations (para 10) can never justify withholding emergency care from a suicidal patient. The ECP should therefore treat, assess capacity and risk, transport when the MHCA or CDST criteria apply, consult and document thoroughly, and respect autonomy only where capacity is clearly present.

### Question 8

**"Explain how the Mental Health Care Act of 2002 influences prehospital decision‑making when a patient who has attempted suicide refuses transport. Include potential safeguards for both patient rights and provider safety."**

The MHCA (Act 17 of 2002) permits a health care provider — which includes the ECP — to provide care without consent when, **due to mental illness**, any delay may result in death or irreversible harm to the health of the user, the user inflicting serious harm to themselves or others, or serious damage to property (s 9(1)(c)); a written report must be made to the Review Board and care without consent may not continue beyond **24 hours** unless a Chapter V application is made (s 9(2)). The formal involuntary pathway requires a written application (usually by a relative, or by a health care provider if relatives are unwilling, incapable or unavailable), examination by two mental health care practitioners and a 72-hour assessment (ss 32–34) — which cannot be done on scene, so the prehospital role is emergency assessment and **transport for evaluation** (Evans, Geduld and Stassen, 2018). SAPS has the statutory duty to apprehend a person who, due to mental illness, is likely to inflict serious harm to themselves or others and to take them to a State health establishment (s 40). The CDST obliges the provider to transport a patient who is a danger to themselves or may be a suicide risk, and to request SAPS instruction for mechanical restraint (HPCSA PBEC, 2025, p. 48). **Safeguards for patient rights** include: a documented capacity and risk assessment; use of the least restrictive intervention (de-escalation, persuasion, family involvement, voluntary oral sedation before parenteral sedation and restraint); respect for dignity and privacy (MHCA s 8); avoidance of deception; brief, safe, proportionate restraint only to facilitate sedation; continuous monitoring; transport to an appropriate State emergency centre; clear documentation of the legal basis so that the facility can comply with s 9(2); and confidentiality. **Safeguards for provider safety** include staging until SAPS secures the scene, removal of weapons and means of self-harm, never working alone, adequate numbers for restraint (five plus one), SAPS escort in the ambulance, securing the vehicle doors, PPE, senior medical consultation, debriefing and incident reporting (Outcomes 43 and 46).

### Question 9

**"Critically appraise the tension between the duty to relieve suffering and the obligation not to prolong life when considering palliative/terminal care in the emergency setting."**

The HPCSA states that "it is not the health practitioner's duty to prolong life at all cost", but that it is their responsibility "to know when to initiate and when to cease attempts at prolonging life, while ensuring that patients receive appropriate relief from distress" (HPCSA, 2023, para 4.2), and that practitioners "shall refrain from employing unusual methods of treatment which are of no benefit to the patient" (para 2.3). The WMA Declaration on Terminal Illness, annexed to the guideline, affirms the patient's right to refuse treatment and to request palliative measures "which may have the additional effect of accelerating the dying process", while prohibiting assisted suicide (Annexure 1, para 6). The tension in emergency care arises because the ECP often meets a dying patient without knowing the prognosis, the patient's wishes or the existence of an advance directive, under time pressure and with distressed family; aggressive resuscitation may then prolong suffering without benefit, whereas withholding treatment in error may cause avoidable death. The HPCSA resolves the emergency dilemma by directing practitioners to start treatment that may be of benefit when there is uncertainty, until a clearer assessment can be made (para 7.1), while respecting valid advance refusals (para 9.2.1), consulting family to determine best interests where the patient lacks capacity (para 3.4), respecting cultural practices around death and dying (para 4.4), documenting decisions (para 12), and ensuring that withholding treatment never means withholding comfort care (para 2.3). In prehospital practice, the CDST includes a **"Palliative end-of-life care"** tool that supports symptom control (pain, breathlessness, agitation, secretions, nausea) and linkage with palliative care teams (HPCSA PBEC, 2025), and the ECP should seek senior advice and documentation (for example a valid do-not-resuscitate decision) where available. Ethical practice is therefore patient-centred, honest with families, avoids futile interventions, and prioritises dignity and the relief of suffering.

### Question 10

**"What are the key differences between de‑escalation and physical containment strategies in violent patient management, and under what circumstances should each be employed according to PHC guidelines?"**

**De-escalation** is a non-coercive, collaborative set of verbal and environmental techniques aimed at calming the patient, preserving dignity and the therapeutic relationship, and avoiding restraint; it is the **first-line and continuous** approach in every agitated patient (RCEM, 2025; Richmond et al., 2012). **Physical containment** (restraint) is a coercive intervention that restricts movement by force or devices, carries significant physical and psychological risks, and is a **last resort**, used only when there is an imminent risk of serious harm to the patient or others and less restrictive measures have failed or are unsafe (Outcome 41.1). In South African prehospital practice, the CDST (HPCSA PBEC, 2025, pp. 46–48) sets out the sequence: first try to calm the patient (approach from a safe distance, talk in a quiet area, listen, be calm and reassuring, show kindness and empathy, set clear boundaries; avoid turning your back, direct eye contact, unnecessary touch, arguing with delusions, attempting to reason, or prolonged conversation); if the patient remains severely agitated, offer **voluntary sedation**; only if the patient refuses and remains dangerous, **restrain physically with five people plus a sixth to give medication, briefly and only to facilitate sedation**, contact SAPS, and apply mechanical restraint only on SAPS instruction; give rapid sedation; then monitor every 10 minutes and check distal pulses in restrained limbs. Containment must be proportionate, performed by trained staff, never involve pressure on the neck, chest or back, be documented (indication, duration, method, people involved) and be followed by a debrief and safe disposition. (The memo's reference to "PHC guidelines" — the primary health care standard treatment guidelines — is consistent with these principles; for prehospital ECP practice, the CDST is the applicable national guidance.)

### Question 11

**"Discuss communication strategies and environmental modifications that can reduce aggression in a violent patient, including room layout, lighting, and staff positioning, with reference to best practices in South Africa."**

Communication strategies include having only **one person speak**; introducing yourself and explaining that your aim is to keep the patient safe; using a calm, quiet, slow voice and short, simple sentences; allowing time to respond; active listening with clarifying statements; identifying and validating the patient's wants and feelings; agreeing where possible or "agreeing to disagree" (fogging); offering choices, acts of kindness and oral medication; setting clear limits as matter-of-fact information rather than threats; avoiding sarcasm, accusations, arguing or challenging delusions; and using a trusted interpreter or family member where helpful (RCEM, 2025; HPCSA PBEC, 2025, p. 46). Environmental modifications include a dedicated or adapted low-stimulus area that is quiet and not too warm; adequate lighting without glare (dimmed where possible for confused older patients); **exits** positioned so that staff cannot be trapped, with doors that open outwards; removal of furniture and objects that could be used as weapons or to barricade an exit; absence of ligature points; constant observation; and an easy way to summon help (RCEM, 2025). **Staff positioning**: stay at least two arm-lengths away, at an angle rather than face-on, with hands visible, never turning your back and never allowing yourself to be cornered, with both you and the patient having an exit (HPCSA PBEC, 2025, p. 46). In South African prehospital practice, this translates into switching off sirens, radios and televisions; moving bystanders away; obtaining collateral history before approaching; requesting SAPS if the risk is significant; and securing the ambulance (attendant seated nearest the door). Staff should be trained in de-escalation, and the CDST provides the national framework.

### Question 12

**"Identify the immediate medical assessments and potential differential diagnoses to consider when a violent patient presents with altered mental status or intoxication, and outline the initial management plan."**

The immediate assessment is scene safety followed by a rapid primary survey once the patient is contained: airway, breathing (SpO₂ — give oxygen if <92%), circulation (BP — manage shock if <90/60), disability (GCS, pupils, **glucose — treat if <3.5 mmol/L**, seizures, lateralising signs), and exposure (temperature, injuries — especially head injury — track marks, signs of self-harm, toxidromes) (HPCSA PBEC, 2025, p. 47). The **differential diagnoses** include intoxication (alcohol, cannabis, methamphetamine, cocaine, sedatives, hallucinogens), withdrawal (alcohol — delirium tremens; benzodiazepines), **hypoglycaemia**, **hypoxia**, **head injury**, infection (sepsis, meningitis, encephalitis, HIV-related CNS infections), seizures and post-ictal states, metabolic disturbances (hyponatraemia, uraemia, hepatic encephalopathy), thyrotoxicosis, heat stroke, anticholinergic toxicity, serotonin syndrome, neuroleptic malignant syndrome, and acute psychiatric illness (RCEM, 2025). The initial management plan is: safety and SAPS; verbal de-escalation; voluntary or rapid sedation per the CDST if dangerous (\*midazolam 0.2 mg/kg IM, max 15 mg; 0.1 mg/kg if >60 years\*; or lorazepam 4 mg IM or ^ketamine 4 mg/kg IM^); primary survey and correction of hypoxia, hypotension and hypoglycaemia; thiamine 100 mg IV/IM in alcohol users (HPCSA PBEC, 2025); seizure management per the CDST; treatment of specific toxidromes (for example naloxone, cooling); IV access once contained; collateral history; monitoring every 10 minutes; documentation; and transport to an emergency centre. The CDST explicitly instructs: "Do not assume aggression/agitation is due to a psychiatric condition" (HPCSA PBEC, 2025, p. 47).

### Question 13

**"Outline the pharmacological considerations for managing agitation or aggression in violent patients, including restraints, if used, and the role of sedatives, analgesics, and antipsychotics under PHC guidelines."**

Medication should be used only as part of a stepwise plan after de-escalation, preferably offered voluntarily first (oral/buccal/IN), with parenteral rapid sedation reserved for patients who remain dangerous (RCEM, 2025; HPCSA PBEC, 2025). In South African prehospital practice the CDST agents are **benzodiazepines** — \*midazolam\* (7.5 mg PO, 10 mg buccal/IN, 2 mg IV voluntary; 0.2 mg/kg IM rapid sedation, max 15 mg, halved if >60 years; repeat 0.1 mg/kg IM, max 5 mg), \*lorazepam\* (2 mg PO, 4 mg IM) and diazepam (10 mg PO) — and, for ANT/ECP, ^ketamine 4 mg/kg IM^ (HPCSA PBEC, 2025, pp. 46–47). Benzodiazepines are preferred for stimulant toxicity and alcohol or sedative withdrawal, but cause respiratory depression (especially with alcohol and opioids), paradoxical agitation, and worsening delirium in older people (Desai, Kulkarni and Verma, n.d.). **Antipsychotics** (haloperidol, droperidol, olanzapine) are used in emergency departments and psychiatric settings for psychosis-related agitation; they are not in the CDST. Evidence shows that haloperidol alone causes frequent acute dystonia and is difficult to justify without an adjunct such as promethazine, while droperidol, olanzapine and IM ziprasidone are effective alternatives, and lorazepam combined with an antipsychotic enhances efficacy (Saucedo Uribe et al., 2025); antipsychotics carry risks of QT prolongation, extrapyramidal effects, hypotension and NMS, and are contraindicated in Parkinson's disease and Lewy body dementia (RCEM, 2025). **Ketamine** has the fastest onset and preserves airway reflexes and haemodynamic stability, but causes hypersalivation, laryngospasm, emergence phenomena, hypertension and tachycardia, and prehospital use is associated with higher intubation rates (RCEM, 2025). **Analgesia** should be given for pain, which often drives agitation (CDST Pain tool), but the CDST warns: "If airway unsecured, avoid high analgesic doses as these have respiratory depressant effects" (HPCSA PBEC, 2025, p. 47). **Restraint**, if used, must be brief, only to facilitate sedation, with five people plus one, never prone with weight on the torso, and mechanical restraint only on SAPS instruction. **Monitoring** after medication (BP, HR, RR, SpO₂, GCS every 10 minutes; ECG; ideally ETCO₂) and documentation of drug, dose, route, time and response are mandatory (HPCSA PBEC, 2025, p. 47; RCEM, 2025).

## A.2 Behavioural Emergencies — Vulnerable Patient Populations — Tutorial 2

### Question 1

**"Discuss the particular vulnerabilities and risks faced by children with disabilities in the South African and wider Southern African context, as articulated in Mesthrie's analysis and the Disability Rights framework. What policy measures should be strengthened or introduced to protect this group within prevention and early intervention mandates?"**

Children with disabilities face a disproportionately high risk of abuse, neglect and exploitation because of dependence on caregivers for intimate care, communication barriers that make disclosure difficult (and lead to their accounts being disbelieved), social isolation, stigma and harmful beliefs about disability, institutional care, poverty, and lack of accessible services; and they are often excluded from prevention programmes and from participation in decisions about their protection. Mesthrie (2025) situates this within extremely high levels of violence against children in South Africa (a child homicide rate nearly twice the global average; 45% of households reporting violence against children), emphasises that the National Child Care and Protection Policy itself acknowledges "lack of adequate support for children with disabilities" as a past gap, and argues that the right to protection is inseparable from the right to be heard and to participate. The disability rights framework (the UN Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities and the African Disability Protocol) requires equal protection, accessibility, reasonable accommodation and inclusion. Policy measures that should be strengthened include: explicit inclusion of disability in all prevention and early intervention norms and standards under the Children's Act; mandatory training for social workers, police, health workers (including EMS) and educators on disability-specific communication and trauma-informed practice; accessible reporting mechanisms (sign language interpretation, pictorial tools, SMS and alternative formats); safeguarding standards in schools, health facilities and residential care; family support to reduce caregiver burden and stigma (for example respite care and the care dependency grant); child participation mechanisms adapted to disability; robust disaggregated data collection; and coordinated multisectoral oversight (Tutorial memo; Mesthrie, 2025). For the ECP, this means maintaining a high index of suspicion, communicating through trusted persons and accessible methods, documenting the child's own expressions, and reporting suspected abuse (Children's Act s 110; SOA s 54; DVA ss 2A–2B).

### Question 2

**"Critically assess Article 19 of the UN Convention on the Rights of the Child (CRC) in the context of South Africa's Children's Act 38 of 2005 and the National Childcare and Protection Policy. How do prevention and early intervention strategies reflect a child-rights based approach, and where do gaps remain in aligning domestic policy with international obligations?"**

Article 19 obliges States Parties to "take all appropriate legislative, administrative, social and educational measures to protect the child from all forms of physical or mental violence, injury or abuse, neglect or negligent treatment, maltreatment or exploitation, including sexual abuse", including social programmes to support children and carers, and procedures for prevention, identification, reporting, referral, investigation, treatment and follow-up (Mesthrie, 2025). General Comment 13 interprets this through a **child rights-based approach** that treats children as rights-holders, with the best interests of the child, non-discrimination, the right to life, survival and development, and the right to be heard as guiding principles. South Africa's **Children's Act** shifted from removal of children to a developmental, family-strengthening model: chapter 7 provides for protection (including s 110 reporting and the 24-hour notification duty on police) and chapter 8 for prevention and early intervention programmes aimed at preserving families, building caregiver capacity and avoiding removal (s 144), with norms and standards (Annexure B) that require high-risk identification, developmental assessment, and child participation in assessments. The **National Child Care and Protection Policy (2021)** adopts a broad definition of children in need of care and protection, recognises vulnerability factors such as poverty and caregiver substance abuse, prioritises targeted prevention and community-based interventions such as "cash plus care", and acknowledges past gaps (Mesthrie, 2025). **Gaps** identified by Mesthrie (2025) include insufficient attention to meaningful **child participation** across the Act and Policy; the absence of an explicit **gendered** approach despite the gendered nature of violence; a narrow family-focused conception of prevention that neglects schools and communities; weak **implementation, funding and monitoring**; norms and standards whose justiciability is uncertain; and the absence of an independent child rights ombudsman or coordinating framework recommended by the UN Committee. The overall conclusion is that the legal framework largely complies with the CRC in form, but that translating it into effective practice remains the main challenge.

### Question 3

**"Describe how the narratives of street children and the social work responses in Harare illuminate the limits of purely protectionist policies. What lessons could be translated to South Africa's approach to preventing streetism and safeguarding vulnerable children?"**

The memo summarises Chikoko's work on street children in Harare, which shows that children move to the streets because of poverty, abuse, family breakdown, orphanhood and social exclusion, and that the street offers them survival strategies, peer support and a degree of agency. Purely **protectionist** responses — removing children from the streets through police round-ups or institutional placement without addressing the causes, and without the children's participation — tend to fail, because children return to the streets, may experience the interventions as punitive, and remain exposed to the same structural drivers. Effective responses are holistic and rights-based: they address basic needs (food, shelter, health care), support families and reunify children only where it is safe, provide education and livelihood opportunities, offer community-based drop-in centres and safe spaces, integrate child protection with health and education services, and involve children in decisions about interventions. For South Africa, where at least 250,000 homeless children and young people live on the streets (Mesthrie, 2025), the lessons are to prioritise prevention by reducing poverty and family stress, strengthen family support and safe reunification, invest in community-based and child-friendly services, ensure child participation consistent with the CRC and Children's Act, link services across sectors, and monitor outcomes. For EMS, street children present with injuries, substance use (including inhalants), sexual exploitation and mental health crises, and require non-judgemental care, attention to consent and confidentiality, and referral to social services and designated child protection organisations (Children's Act s 150 lists children who live or work on the street or beg as children in need of care and protection).

### Question 4

**"Discuss the role of benzodiazepines in the management of agitation in older adults, including contexts where they are appropriate and where they are discouraged, and the potential risks involved."**

Benzodiazepines have a limited but important role in older adults: they are **appropriate** for agitation due to **alcohol or benzodiazepine withdrawal**, for **seizures**, and in some **toxicological** situations (for example stimulant toxicity), where GABAergic mechanisms are central (Desai, Kulkarni and Verma, n.d.). They are **discouraged** as routine first-line agents for undifferentiated agitation, delirium or dementia-related distress, because the 2023 Beers Criteria warn that they increase the risk of cognitive impairment, delirium, falls, fractures and psychomotor impairment, and advise avoiding them in patients with or at risk of delirium (American Geriatrics Society Beers Criteria Update Expert Panel, 2023). Risks include respiratory depression and hypoventilation (particularly in frail patients and with other sedatives), worsening delirium and prolonged confusion, falls and fractures, paradoxical agitation, excessive sedation with aspiration risk, and cumulative toxicity because of reduced clearance. **Midazolam** has raised particular safety concern in older adults (Casey et al., cited by Desai, Kulkarni and Verma, n.d.; Saucedo Uribe et al., 2025). In South African prehospital practice, midazolam is nevertheless the main sedative in the CDST, which reflects this risk by **halving the dose in patients older than 60 years** (0.1 mg/kg IM rapid sedation; 0.05 mg/kg IM repeat dose) (HPCSA PBEC, 2025, p. 47). The safe approach is to use non-pharmacological measures first, identify the cause (syndrome-directed treatment), use the lowest effective dose, avoid stacking repeat doses, monitor closely (airway, SpO₂, BP, GCS every 10 minutes), hand over clearly what was given and why, and plan deprescribing after the crisis.

### Question 5

**"Explain the essential components of a cause-oriented assessment framework for acute agitation in older adults in the emergency department, and discuss how this approach informs pharmacologic decision-making."**

A cause-oriented assessment treats behaviour change in an older adult as **medically caused until proven otherwise** (Desai, Kulkarni and Verma, n.d.). Its components are layered: (1) immediate **physiology and safety** — hypoxia, hypoglycaemia, shock, acute coronary syndrome, stroke, intracranial haemorrhage, sepsis and severe pain; (2) the **baseline cognitive status and dementia subtype** (from collateral history about baseline cognition, onset, fluctuation and previous episodes — essential for distinguishing delirium from dementia); (3) **precipitating stressors** — environmental overstimulation, unfamiliar surroundings, sleep disruption, toileting needs, urinary retention, constipation, hunger, thirst, missing hearing aids or glasses; and (4) **iatrogenic contributors** — anticholinergics, opioid escalation, sedative withdrawal, akathisia from dopamine blockers, corticosteroids, polypharmacy, tethers and immobilisation. This approach informs pharmacological decisions by matching the agent to the syndrome: **benzodiazepines** for withdrawal or seizures; avoidance of **dopamine antagonists** (haloperidol, droperidol) in Parkinson's disease or Lewy body dementia, where quetiapine is preferred if an antipsychotic is needed; low-dose **olanzapine** or haloperidol for dangerous agitation due to psychosis or dementia-related distress when non-drug measures fail; **analgesia** rather than sedation when pain is the driver; **oxygen** rather than sedation when hypoxia is the driver; and treatment of the underlying illness in delirium, with medication used only as a short-term bridge for safety. It also emphasises low starting doses, delayed repeat dosing, attention to QT and respiratory risk, rapid reassessment to avoid masking the underlying illness, and discontinuation of psychotropics after the crisis (Desai, Kulkarni and Verma, n.d.).

### Question 6

**"Identify and discuss at least three common etiologies of agitation in older adults that require distinct pharmacologic considerations, with examples of appropriate therapeutic responses."**

(1) **Delirium due to acute medical illness** (for example urinary tract infection, pneumonia, hypoxia, metabolic disturbance, medication toxicity): the priority is to identify and treat the cause and to use non-pharmacological measures (reorientation, family presence, sensory aids, quiet environment); medication is reserved for dangerous behaviour, using the lowest dose of a low-risk agent, and benzodiazepines are avoided because they worsen delirium (Desai, Kulkarni and Verma, n.d.; Inouye, Westendorp and Saczynski, 2014). (2) **Dementia-related behavioural disturbance**, including **Lewy body dementia and Parkinson's disease**: non-pharmacological care (addressing pain, toileting, environment, caregiver presence) comes first; if an antipsychotic is unavoidable, low-dose olanzapine may be used in Alzheimer-type dementia, while patients with Lewy body or Parkinson's disease are extremely sensitive to dopamine antagonists, so haloperidol and droperidol are contraindicated and quetiapine is preferred (Desai, Kulkarni and Verma, n.d.; RCEM, 2025). (3) **Alcohol or benzodiazepine withdrawal**: benzodiazepines are the appropriate treatment, at the minimum effective dose with careful monitoring, together with thiamine (CDST: thiamine 100 mg IV/IM) and treatment of dehydration and electrolyte disturbances. Further examples include (4) **pain** (for example an occult hip fracture or ischaemic limb), where analgesia rather than sedation is the correct response, and (5) **medication-induced akathisia**, where the offending dopamine blocker should be stopped rather than another antipsychotic added. Understanding the syndrome therefore determines whether the right treatment is an analgesic, oxygen, a benzodiazepine, a specific antipsychotic, or no drug at all.

### Question 7

**"Critically assess the role of ketamine in the management of acute agitation and discuss why its routine use in older adults is cautioned against in the literature provided."**

Ketamine is an NMDA receptor antagonist that produces dissociative sedation with a rapid onset (minutes after IM injection), relative preservation of airway reflexes and respiratory drive, and haemodynamic stability; it is associated with shorter times to adequate sedation than benzodiazepines or antipsychotics, and the RCEM (2025) recommends IM ketamine (4 mg/kg) as a first-line agent for severe acute behavioural disturbance in emergency departments, while the CDST includes ^ketamine 4 mg/kg IM^ for aggression (ANT/ECP) when midazolam is unavailable or a more rapid onset is required (HPCSA PBEC, 2025, p. 47). Its disadvantages include hypersalivation, laryngospasm, vomiting, emergence phenomena, hypertension and tachycardia (which may be synergistic with stimulants), and high rates of subsequent intubation in prehospital series (RCEM, 2025). In older adults, Desai, Kulkarni and Verma (n.d.) caution that the evidence for ketamine comes from general (mostly younger) emergency populations, with major guidance such as the 2024 ACEP severe agitation policy excluding patients older than 65 years, and that older adults "face greater cardiovascular variability, aspiration concern, and post-sedation diagnostic complexity" — ketamine may precipitate myocardial ischaemia or arrhythmias in patients with coronary disease, may cause aspiration in frail patients with impaired swallowing, and its dissociative and emergence effects can mimic or worsen delirium, obscuring the diagnosis. Ketamine "should not be imported into routine geriatric agitation protocols simply because it performs well in younger profoundly agitated patients"; it is better reserved for exceptional cases with close monitoring and the ability to manage the airway and haemodynamic changes.

### Question 8

**"Evaluate the ethical and legal considerations involved in the use of restraint in ABD, including the Mental Capacity Act and the aim of least restrictive practice."**

*Important note*: the **Mental Capacity Act 2005** referred to in the RCEM (2025) guideline is **United Kingdom legislation**, and it does not apply in South Africa. The equivalent South African legal bases are explained below. Patients with severe acute behavioural disturbance (ABD) are likely to lack capacity to make treatment decisions, which should be formally assessed and documented, and may require emergency treatment, including restraint, under the appropriate legislation (RCEM, 2025). In South Africa, the lawful bases for restraint and treatment without consent are **MHCA s 9(1)(c)** (where, due to mental illness, delay may result in death or irreversible harm, serious harm to self or others, or serious property damage), **NHA s 7(1)(e)** (emergency treatment where delay may result in death or irreversible damage to health and the patient has not refused), the common-law defence of **necessity**, and SAPS powers under **MHCA s 40** (including the use of necessary and proportionate constraining measures, s 40(8)). Ethically, restraint must be **necessary** (imminent risk), a **last resort** after de-escalation and offers of oral sedation, **proportionate** and **least restrictive**, applied for the **shortest possible time**, performed safely by sufficient trained staff (never with weight on the chest or neck), never punitive, respectful of dignity (Constitution s 10; MHCA s 8), and fully documented (capacity assessment, indication, duration, method, police involvement, sedation and adverse events) (RCEM, 2025; HPCSA PBEC, 2025). The RCEM (2025) notes that continued exertion under restraint may worsen hyperthermia, acidosis and catecholamine excess in ABD, that there is no risk-free method of restraint, and that prolonged restraint should prompt rapid tranquillisation; the CDST similarly limits restraint to a brief period "only to facilitate sedation" and permits mechanical restraint only on SAPS instruction (HPCSA PBEC, 2025, p. 46). Police involvement is appropriate when staff are injured, when healthcare support is not available in time, or when there is a risk of serious injury (RCEM, 2025).

### Question 9

**"Discuss the importance of capnography and physiological monitoring during rapid tranquilisation and the rationale for maintaining a high-dependency environment after sedation."**

Most sedative agents used for rapid tranquillisation have been associated with apnoea, airway obstruction or the need for intubation, and respiratory adverse events are more common with benzodiazepines (RCEM, 2025). Patients with ABD are often physiologically fragile (dehydrated, acidotic, hyperthermic, catecholamine-depleted, possibly with co-ingestants or head injury), cannot report symptoms, and may deteriorate suddenly into peri-arrest. **Capnography (ETCO₂)** detects hypoventilation, apnoea and airway obstruction earlier than pulse oximetry — particularly when supplemental oxygen is being given, which delays desaturation — and the RCEM (2025) includes ETCO₂ in the routine monitoring of sedated ABD patients alongside ECG, pulse oximetry and blood pressure. Continuous monitoring also detects hypotension, arrhythmias (including QT-related arrhythmias after antipsychotics), hyperthermia, and the evolution of the underlying cause. A **high-dependency (resuscitation) environment** after sedation ensures immediate access to airway equipment, suction, oxygen, a bag-valve mask, drugs (including flumazenil where appropriate), and staff skilled in advanced airway management, and allows correction of electrolyte, glucose and acid–base abnormalities, cooling, and investigation for rhabdomyolysis and DIC (RCEM, 2025). In the prehospital setting, the CDST requires BP, HR, RR, SpO₂ and GCS every 10 minutes after sedation, with immediate reassessment if parameters deteriorate, and the RCEM notes that the practitioner giving rapid tranquillisation must be capable of managing its complications (HPCSA PBEC, 2025, p. 47; RCEM, 2025).

### Question 10

**"Describe the de-escalation framework recommended by RCEM guidelines and provide examples of practical techniques to implement verbal and environmental de-escalation."**

The RCEM (2025) recommends a ten-domain model adapted from the Project BETA consensus (Richmond et al., 2012): (1) respect personal space (identify exits, stay out of arm's reach); (2) do not be provocative (non-confrontational body language, hands visible, do not challenge, insult or argue); (3) establish verbal contact (one staff member speaks, introduce yourself, explain why you are there, reassure the patient that you aim to keep them safe); (4) be concise (short sentences, give time to respond, repeat as needed); (5) identify wants and feelings (identify expectations, empathise); (6) listen closely (clarifying statements); (7) agree, or agree to disagree (fogging — agree with the truth, in principle, or with the odds); (8) set clear limits (matter-of-fact, not threatening); (9) offer choices and optimism (acts of kindness, offer oral sedative medication); and (10) debrief the patient and staff (explain why intervention was necessary, restore the therapeutic relationship, identify improvements). **Practical verbal techniques** include a calm introduction, explaining each step before doing it, using the patient's name, offering a blanket or (if sedation is not planned) a drink, involving a calm family member, and avoiding argument about delusions. **Environmental techniques** include a dedicated low-stimulus area that is quiet and not too warm, outward-opening doors and accessible exits, removal of potential weapons and ligature points, constant observation, and an easy way to call for help (RCEM, 2025). If sedation or anaesthesia is likely, food or drink should not be offered. The CDST "Try to / Avoid" list (HPCSA PBEC, 2025, p. 46) provides the South African prehospital equivalent (Outcome 43.2).

### Question 11

**"Critically appraise the use of sedation agents beyond first-line choices, including indications, risks, and how to manage potential complications such as respiratory depression or paradoxical agitation."**

In the RCEM (2025) guideline, the first-line agents for severe ABD are IM ketamine (4 mg/kg) and IM droperidol (5–10 mg); if these are unavailable, **midazolam** (5–10 mg IM), **lorazepam** (4 mg IM) or **haloperidol** (5 mg IM, with or without lorazepam 2 mg IM) may be considered, with **olanzapine** and further benzodiazepines used for maintenance and chlorpromazine for serotonin toxicity with severe psychosis or hyperthermia. (Note that in South African prehospital practice the order is different: the CDST uses midazolam as the first-line parenteral agent, with lorazepam or ketamine as alternatives; HPCSA PBEC, 2025, p. 47.) **Midazolam** is rapid but causes more respiratory adverse events and is of particular concern in older adults; it may be preferred where sympathomimetic effects are dangerous (cocaine, ischaemic heart disease). **Lorazepam** is effective with fewer extrapyramidal effects than antipsychotics, and its combination with haloperidol improves sedation without more adverse effects (RCEM, 2025; Saucedo Uribe et al., 2025). **Haloperidol** has a slower onset, frequent acute dystonia and QT prolongation, and is contraindicated in Parkinson's disease and Lewy body dementia; adding promethazine reduces dystonia (Saucedo Uribe et al., 2025). **Olanzapine** is effective with fewer movement disorders, but serious adverse events after IM olanzapine have been reported mainly in medically ill patients who also received benzodiazepines or other antipsychotics, so combining IM olanzapine with parenteral benzodiazepines requires great caution (Desai, Kulkarni and Verma, n.d.). Selection must consider cardiovascular status, co-ingested substances (serotonin syndrome, stimulants, alcohol), age, the anticipated need for airway control, and monitoring capability. **Management of complications**: for **respiratory depression** — stimulate, position the airway (head tilt–chin lift or jaw thrust, recovery position), give oxygen, ventilate with a bag-valve mask, insert airway adjuncts, use flumazenil cautiously only in pure benzodiazepine sedation without contraindications (not in the CDST), and proceed to advanced airway management if needed; for **paradoxical agitation** with benzodiazepines — stop further benzodiazepine doses, reassess for an organic cause (hypoxia, hypoglycaemia, pain, urinary retention), and consider an alternative agent class; for **acute dystonia** — an anticholinergic (biperiden or benztropine) in hospital; for **hypotension** — fluids and position; for **QT prolongation and torsade de pointes** — magnesium and correction of potassium; and if sedation fails after full doses, escalate to critical care, review the differential diagnosis, and avoid uncontrolled dose stacking (RCEM, 2025).

## A.3 Toxicology — Patient Approach and Management — Tutorial 1

### Question 1

**"Critically discuss the major toxidromes commonly encountered in acute poisoning and outline how their recognition guides immediate management, including any specific antidotes or supportive measures. Discuss how you would distinguish between them clinically."**

A full answer, including the CDST toxidrome table, mnemonics and a comparison table, is given in Outcome 62.3. In summary, the major toxidromes are: **sympathomimetic** (agitation, hallucinations, seizures, sweating, hyperthermia, dilated pupils, tachycardia, hypertension — managed with benzodiazepines, cooling, aspirin and nitrates for stimulant chest pain, and sodium bicarbonate for widened QRS); **anticholinergic** (agitation and delirium, flushed, dry, warm skin, hyperthermia, dilated pupils, tachycardia, urinary retention, reduced bowel sounds — managed with benzodiazepines, cooling, sodium bicarbonate if QRS is widened, and physostigmine in hospital for refractory delirium); **opioid** (depressed consciousness, pinpoint pupils, respiratory depression, bradycardia, hypotension, hypothermia — managed with ventilation and titrated naloxone: CDST 0.4 mg, or 0.04 mg in known opioid abusers, escalating to a total of 10 mg; child 0.1 mg/kg); **cholinergic** (depressed consciousness, seizures, sweating, pinpoint pupils, watery eyes, bradycardia, drooling, incontinence, vomiting and diarrhoea, bronchorrhoea — managed with PPE, decontamination, ^atropine 2 mg IV^ doubled every three minutes until the chest is clear, then 20% of the total per hour, and oximes in hospital); **sedative-hypnotic** (depressed consciousness, slurred speech, respiratory depression — managed supportively, with airway and ventilatory support; flumazenil is not used prehospitally and is contraindicated in mixed overdoses); and **serotonin syndrome** (agitation, autonomic instability, clonus and hyperreflexia — benzodiazepines, cooling, cyproheptadine in hospital) (HPCSA PBEC, 2025, pp. 55–57; Hüser et al., 2025). Clinically, they are distinguished by **mental status, pupil size, heart rate, temperature, skin moisture, bowel sounds, secretions and neuromuscular signs**: pinpoint pupils suggest opioids or cholinergic agents (secretions and sweating point to cholinergic); dilated pupils suggest sympathomimetic (sweaty skin) or anticholinergic (dry skin) toxicity; clonus suggests serotonin toxicity. *Correction to the memo*: the memo lists "mydriasis suggests stimulants or antidepressant overdose"; this is correct for TCAs (anticholinergic effects), but the key differentiator between sympathomimetic and anticholinergic toxicity is sweating versus dry skin. Toxidromes have limitations in mixed ingestions, incomplete presentations and poisons with delayed toxicity (paracetamol, iron, toxic alcohols) (Hüser et al., 2025).

### Question 2

**"What are the key components and limitations of decontamination strategies in acute poisoning, and how should clinicians decide between skin/eye decontamination, gastric decontamination, and post-absorptive elimination methods? Include evidence-based time windows and contraindications."**

**Skin and eye decontamination** is simple, effective and always indicated for external exposures: protect yourself with PPE, remove clothing, wash skin with soap and water, and irrigate eyes with running water or saline for at least 20 minutes (HPCSA PBEC, 2025, p. 54); it should precede other treatment for organophosphates, corrosives and hydrofluoric acid (with calcium gel for HF). **Gastric decontamination**: **single-dose activated charcoal** (CDST 1 g/kg, adult 50–100 g) is most effective within **one hour** of ingestion and is given prehospitally only if the patient is fully conscious, the ingestion was oral, the substance is harmful, and it is adsorbable — not for household products, paraffin, petrol, corrosives, iron, lithium or alcohol (HPCSA PBEC, 2025, p. 54); the window may be longer for massive ingestions, sustained-release formulations or anticholinergic drugs (Hüser et al., 2025); it is contraindicated with an unprotected airway or reduced consciousness (aspiration risk), bowel obstruction or perforation, and corrosive ingestion. **Whole bowel irrigation** with polyethylene glycol (25–40 mL/kg/h until the effluent is clear) is used for sustained-release or enteric-coated drugs, substances not bound by charcoal (iron, lithium), and body packers, but is contraindicated in GI haemorrhage, obstruction or ileus, haemodynamic instability, and an unprotected airway (Kaswa, 2024). **Gastric lavage** is no longer routine and is reserved for exceptional, very early, life-threatening ingestions (for example high-dose colchicine or cardiotoxicants, or very early ingestions of non-adsorbed toxins), because of the risks of aspiration, hypoxia and perforation; it is contraindicated for hydrocarbons, corrosives and an unprotected airway (Hüser et al., 2025; Kaswa, 2024). **Induced emesis** (ipecac) is not recommended (Höjer et al., 2013; HPCSA PBEC, 2025). **Cathartics are not recommended**; *correction to the memo*: sorbitol is a **cathartic** (causing diarrhoea and dehydration, especially in infants), not an emetic. **Post-absorptive (enhanced) elimination** includes **MDAC** for drugs with enterohepatic or entero-enteric recirculation (carbamazepine, phenobarbital, dapsone, quinine, theophylline), **urinary alkalinisation** for weak acids (salicylates, methotrexate — urine pH ≥7.5), and **extracorporeal elimination** (haemodialysis) for small, water-soluble, poorly protein-bound toxins with a small volume of distribution (lithium, salicylates, toxic alcohols, theophylline, metformin, valproate), guided by EXTRIP recommendations (Hüser et al., 2025). **Decision-making** depends on the route, time since exposure, the toxin's properties, the expected severity, and the patient's airway and haemodynamic stability; no decontamination technique has been shown to reduce morbidity or mortality in clinical trials, so all should be used selectively and must never compromise the airway or delay resuscitation (Hüser et al., 2025).

### Question 3

**"Analyse the role of diagnostic testing in poisoning alongside clinical assessment. Which laboratory tests are most informative, and how should clinicians balance laborator y results with urgent therapeutic needs? Provide examples of how test results could alter management."**

Diagnostic testing complements, but never replaces, clinical assessment: "treat the patient first, not the laboratory results" (Kaswa, 2024). The most informative point-of-care tests are **blood glucose** (hypoglycaemia mimics poisoning and occurs with alcohol, sulphonylureas, salicylates, beta-blockers), the **12-lead ECG** (QRS >100 ms suggests sodium channel blockade and seizure risk; QTc ≥500 ms suggests a risk of torsade de pointes; bradycardia and AV block suggest beta-blockers, calcium channel blockers or digoxin) and **blood gas analysis** (acidosis, hypercapnia, methaemoglobin, carboxyhaemoglobin, lactate) (Hüser et al., 2025). Kaswa (2024) lists the standard tests for significant poisoning as full blood count, prothrombin time, electrolytes, pulse oximetry, urea and creatinine, 12-lead ECG, glucose, arterial blood gas, calcium, toxicology screen, urinalysis and liver function (in paracetamol toxicity). The **anion gap and osmolal gap** help identify toxic alcohols and other acid-producing toxins, and **quantitative drug levels** (paracetamol, salicylate, lithium, iron, digoxin, theophylline, carbamazepine, valproate) guide treatment and, when repeated every four to six hours, show ongoing absorption and guide enhanced elimination (Hüser et al., 2025). **Urine drug screens** have limited value because of false positives and negatives and their inability to detect many drugs (Z-drugs, GHB, fentanyl analogues) (Hüser et al., 2025). In unstable patients or clear toxidromes, treatment proceeds on clinical grounds while awaiting results. **Examples** of how results change management: a paracetamol level above the nomogram line at four hours mandates NAC; a widened QRS prompts sodium bicarbonate; hyperkalaemia in acute digoxin toxicity prompts digoxin Fab; a high lithium level with neurological signs prompts haemodialysis; a raised osmolal and anion gap prompts fomepizole/ethanol and haemodialysis for toxic alcohols; a salicylate level and blood gas guide urinary alkalinisation or haemodialysis; methaemoglobinaemia prompts methylene blue; and a raised CK prompts aggressive fluids for rhabdomyolysis. Normal results do not exclude poisoning, particularly early after ingestion of agents with delayed toxicity.

### Question 4

**"Critically evaluate the decision-making process surrounding the use of antidotes in acute poisoning. What factors influence the choice and timing of antidote administration, and how should clinicians reconcile antidote availability with patient urgency and potential adverse effects?"**

Antidotes exist for only a limited number of poisons, and resuscitation and supportive care remain the mainstay of treatment (Hüser et al., 2025). The decision to give an antidote depends on: (1) the **probability** that a specific toxin is responsible (history, toxidrome, ECG, levels); (2) the **severity** or expected severity of poisoning; (3) the **evidence of benefit** of the antidote; (4) the **time window** (for example NAC is most effective within eight hours of paracetamol ingestion; oximes must be given before organophosphate "ageing"); (5) the **risks** of the antidote (for example naloxone precipitating withdrawal, agitation and vomiting; flumazenil precipitating seizures in mixed overdoses or benzodiazepine dependence; physostigmine causing seizures and bradycardia, and contraindicated with QRS widening; antivenom causing anaphylaxis; sodium bicarbonate causing hypernatraemia and alkalosis); (6) the provider's **scope** and the setting (prehospital CDST antidotes are naloxone, atropine, sodium bicarbonate, calcium, glucagon, dextrose and thiamine, with adrenaline for cardiovascular collapse — HPCSA PBEC, 2025); and (7) **availability**. Timing is crucial for antidotes with narrow windows or immediate life-saving effects (naloxone for opioid respiratory depression; atropine for cholinergic crisis; sodium bicarbonate for TCA cardiotoxicity; dextrose for hypoglycaemia; hydroxocobalamin for cyanide). Antidotes should be **titrated to clinical endpoints** (for example naloxone to adequate breathing; atropine to a dry chest) rather than given as fixed large doses. *Correction to the memo*: the memo suggests administering antidotes "within the first hour when possible" as a general rule, citing Kaswa (2024); in reality the time windows are antidote-specific (for example NAC is effective up to eight hours and beyond; antivenom may be effective for coagulopathy days after a bite), and the decision should be based on the specific toxin. Where an antidote is unavailable, aggressive supportive care must continue while advice is sought from the **Poisons Information Helpline (0861 555 777)** and transfer to a facility holding the antidote is arranged; in unknown poisonings, antidotes with a favourable risk–benefit profile (dextrose, naloxone titrated, thiamine) may be given empirically, while high-risk antidotes (flumazenil, physostigmine) should be avoided.

### Question 5

**"Argue for and against the indications for gastrointestinal decontamination in pharmaceutical poisoning, with reference to activated charcoal and whole bowel irrigation, including timing and formulary considerations."**

**For activated charcoal**: it adsorbs most pharmaceuticals, reducing absorption substantially when given early (most effective within one hour, but potentially useful later for massive ingestions, sustained-release preparations and anticholinergic drugs that delay gastric emptying); it is cheap, widely available and safe in an alert patient; and MDAC can enhance the elimination of drugs with enterohepatic recirculation (carbamazepine, phenobarbital, dapsone, quinine, theophylline) (Hüser et al., 2025). The usual adult dose is 50 g (CDST 1 g/kg, adult 50–100 g), aiming for a 10:1 charcoal-to-drug ratio, with MDAC doses of 12.5 g every one to four hours in adults (Hüser et al., 2025; HPCSA PBEC, 2025). **Against activated charcoal**: no clinical trial has shown a reduction in morbidity or mortality; most patients present after one hour; it carries a risk of aspiration in patients whose consciousness may decline (TCAs, seizure-inducing drugs); it does not bind iron, lithium, potassium, alcohols, hydrocarbons, corrosives or many household products; vomiting is common; and it may delay transport or other treatment. **For whole bowel irrigation**: it is the main option for sustained-release or enteric-coated pharmaceuticals, toxins not bound by charcoal (iron, lithium), and drug packets (Kaswa, 2024; Hüser et al., 2025). **Against whole bowel irrigation**: there is no outcome evidence; it requires large volumes (25–40 mL/kg/h) via a nasogastric tube, a protected airway and a cooperative or intubated patient; and it is contraindicated in obstruction, perforation, ileus, GI haemorrhage and haemodynamic instability. Gastric lavage and cathartics are not routinely recommended (Hüser et al., 2025). **Formulary considerations** include the availability of charcoal on ambulances (the CDST lists it within all scopes), avoidance of sorbitol-containing preparations (especially in children), the availability of polyethylene glycol, and the need to consult the poison centre. **Conclusion**: gastrointestinal decontamination should be selective — reserved for alert or intubated patients with potentially serious ingestions of suitable agents within an appropriate time window — and must never compromise the airway or delay resuscitation.

### Question 6

**"Analyse the diagnostic approach to a poisoned patient in the ICU, including the reliability of history, collateral information, toxidromes, ECG interpretation, and the role of laboratory and imaging studies in guiding management."**

After ABCDE stabilisation, the diagnostic approach comprises history, examination, ECG, laboratory tests and imaging (Hüser et al., 2025). **History** is often unreliable (altered consciousness, intentional concealment, unknown street drug contents), so **collateral information** from EMS, family, friends and bystanders is essential — including containers and the scene findings that the ECP is uniquely placed to provide (Outcome 62.6). **Toxidromes** help identify the likely class and anticipate the course, but may be obscured in mixed ingestions with antagonistic effects (Hüser et al., 2025). An **ECG** should be obtained in all suspected poisonings: changes in the PR interval (beta-blockers, calcium channel blockers, digoxin), QRS (sodium channel blockers — TCAs, diphenhydramine, local anaesthetics, flecainide), QT (antipsychotics, methadone, antiemetics, diphenhydramine) and ST segment indicate the toxin and risk; QRS >100 ms and QTc ≥500 ms are associated with life-threatening arrhythmias and seizures, and serial ECGs track the course (Hüser et al., 2025). **Laboratory tests** detect differential diagnoses (hypoglycaemia) and complications (acidosis, hypercapnia, methaemoglobinaemia, organ dysfunction, rhabdomyolysis); **quantitative levels** (paracetamol, lithium, salicylate) identify toxins, assess severity and, repeated at four- to six-hour intervals, show ongoing absorption and guide enhanced elimination; urine immunoassays are of limited value; and retaining a blood sample for later analysis can be useful (Hüser et al., 2025). **Imaging** is reserved for selected cases: CT brain for altered consciousness with trauma or focal signs; abdominal X-ray, CT or ultrasound for radio-opaque tablets (iron), pharmacobezoars or drug packets, which may guide whole bowel irrigation, gastroscopy or surgery; and imaging must never delay specific treatment (for example sodium bicarbonate in salicylate poisoning) (Hüser et al., 2025). Clinicians should avoid attributing altered mental status solely to intoxication, because trauma, infection and metabolic disturbances often coexist, and should consult a poison centre early.

### Question 7

**"Explain the indications for extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO) in toxin-induced cardiogenic shock, including patient selection criteria, timing, and multidisciplinary coordination required for a successful ECMO pathway."**

Refractory cardiovascular failure is a leading cause of death in pharmaceutical poisoning, and severe cardiotoxicity develops rapidly with hypotension, high-grade AV block, asystole or pulseless ventricular arrhythmias after overdoses of calcium channel blockers, beta-blockers, membrane-stabilising agents and other cardiotoxicants (for example bupropion) (Hüser et al., 2025). **Veno-arterial ECMO (VA-ECMO)** takes over cardiac function during refractory cardiogenic shock or cardiac arrest until the toxin is cleared and myocardial function recovers, minimising myocardial work, maintaining organ perfusion, and preserving hepatic metabolism and renal and biliary elimination of the toxin; support is usually needed only briefly, until plasma concentrations fall (Hüser et al., 2025). **Indications**: persistent cardiogenic shock or cardiac arrest due to a cardiotoxicant **despite** optimal conventional treatment — fluids, vasopressors and inotropes, and antidotes (calcium, HIET, glucagon, sodium bicarbonate, lipid emulsion as appropriate). Pacing is useful only when inotropic function is preserved, and an intra-aortic balloon pump is of little use because it requires an intrinsic rhythm. **Selection criteria** include a reversible toxic cause, no contraindication to anticoagulation, the absence of irreversible organ damage or terminal comorbidity, and access to an established ECMO programme. **Timing**: VA-ECMO should be initiated **early** in refractory shock, before multi-organ failure or prolonged arrest develops, because predictors of refractoriness differ for each class of cardiotoxicant (Hüser et al., 2025). **Veno-venous ECMO** supports respiratory failure (for example aspiration or inhalational injury), with survival rates approaching 90% reported (Hüser et al., 2025). **Coordination** requires an "ECMO rescue chain" with a rapid activation pathway, involving intensivists, emergency physicians, cardiothoracic surgeons or cannulation teams, perfusionists, cardiologists, toxicologists or poison centres, nursing staff, and transport teams; ILE should be used cautiously when ECMO is anticipated because lipid can occlude the oxygenator (Hüser et al., 2025). The evidence base consists largely of retrospective case series, but supports reasonable use in selected patients. In South Africa, ECMO is available only at a small number of tertiary centres, so early recognition and early referral are essential.

### Question 8

**"Evaluate the use of antidotes in pharmaceutical poisoning, focusing on sodium bicarbonate for sodium channel blocker toxicity and digoxin-specific antibodies for digoxin-like poisoning, including dosing considerations and monitoring."**

**Sodium bicarbonate** (8.4%) is the antidote of choice for sodium channel blocker poisoning, particularly TCA overdose, and is indicated for ventricular arrhythmias, QRS prolongation >100 ms, hypotension or seizures; it works by increasing extracellular sodium (overcoming channel blockade) and by alkalinisation (target blood pH about 7.5), which reduces TCA binding to sodium channels (Hüser et al., 2025). The hospital dose is 1–2 mL/kg of 8.4% as an IV bolus, repeated as needed or followed by an infusion (20–30 mEq/h), aiming for a pH of about 7.5 rather than complete normalisation of the QRS; blood gases should be checked within an hour and every two to four hours, with monitoring for hypernatraemia, hypokalaemia and metabolic alkalosis (Hüser et al., 2025). In South African prehospital practice, the CDST dose is ^sodium bicarbonate 8.4% 100 mL IV over 3 minutes^ in adults (2 mL/kg in children), reassessed after 10 minutes and repeated once if toxicity persists, with hyperventilation (20–24 breaths/min, ETCO₂ 20–25 mmHg) if intubated (HPCSA PBEC, 2025, p. 56). In salicylate and methotrexate poisoning, more dilute bicarbonate (1.4%) is used for urinary alkalinisation (urine pH ≥7.5, avoiding blood pH >7.5). **Digoxin-specific antibody (Fab) fragments** bind free digoxin (and other cardiac glycosides, including plant glycosides from oleander and *Drimia*), forming complexes excreted by the kidneys; they are indicated for life-threatening arrhythmias (for example ventricular arrhythmias, high-grade AV block, paroxysmal atrial tachycardia with block) and hyperkalaemia in acute poisoning (Hüser et al., 2025). When the amount ingested or the serum level is unknown, two vials initially are suggested for acute poisoning or one vial for chronic poisoning, repeated as needed according to clinical response; doses can also be calculated from the amount ingested or the serum concentration (Hüser et al., 2025). Monitoring includes continuous ECG, potassium (which can fall rapidly after Fab), renal function, and clinical response; serum digoxin levels after Fab are unreliable because most assays measure both bound and free digoxin (Hüser et al., 2025). Digoxin Fab is not available prehospitally, so the ECP manages arrhythmias, avoids hypoxia and transports urgently.

### Question 9

**"Propose a structured treatment algorithm for a patient presenting with suspected pharmaceutical poisoning, integrating initial resuscitation, diagnostic steps, GI decontamination, toxicant-specific therapies, and escalation to ECMO or other rescue strategies."**

**Step 1 — Scene safety and initial resuscitation (ABCDE)**: airway (selective intubation — a conservative strategy in comatose patients without respiratory failure, shock or vomiting reduces pneumonia; Hüser et al., 2025), breathing (oxygen if SpO₂ <92%; ventilation), circulation (IV access, crystalloid 250–500 mL boluses for SBP <90; vasopressors), disability (GCS, pupils, **glucose**; benzodiazepines for seizures), exposure (temperature — cool if ≥38 °C; injuries) (HPCSA PBEC, 2025, pp. 54–57). **Step 2 — Early empirical antidotes**: naloxone (titrated) for opioid respiratory depression; dextrose for hypoglycaemia; thiamine in at-risk patients; sodium bicarbonate for widened QRS; atropine for cholinergic crisis; calcium, glucagon and adrenaline infusion for beta-blocker/calcium channel blocker shock (CDST). **Step 3 — Diagnosis**: history and collateral information, containers, toxidrome identification, 12-lead ECG, blood gas, electrolytes, renal and liver function, paracetamol and salicylate levels in all deliberate overdoses, specific drug levels, and imaging where indicated (Hüser et al., 2025). **Step 4 — Consult the Poisons Information Helpline (0861 555 777)**. **Step 5 — GI decontamination** (if indicated and safe): single-dose activated charcoal (50 g adult; CDST 1 g/kg) within about one hour (longer for sustained-release or massive ingestions) in an alert or intubated patient with an adsorbable toxin; MDAC for enterohepatic drugs; whole bowel irrigation for sustained-release preparations, iron, lithium or packets (Hüser et al., 2025). **Step 6 — Toxicant-specific therapy**: NAC (paracetamol), sodium bicarbonate (sodium channel blockers, salicylates), HIET and glucagon (calcium channel blockers and beta-blockers), digoxin Fab (glycosides), deferoxamine (iron), methylene blue (methaemoglobinaemia), physostigmine (central anticholinergic syndrome without QRS widening), fomepizole/ethanol (toxic alcohols), octreotide (sulphonylureas), and hydroxocobalamin (cyanide) (Hüser et al., 2025). **Step 7 — Enhanced elimination**: urinary alkalinisation (salicylates), extracorporeal treatment (haemodialysis) for dialysable toxins (lithium, salicylates, toxic alcohols, theophylline, metformin, valproate) or refractory acidosis and organ failure, guided by EXTRIP; haemodynamic instability caused by a dialysable toxin is not a contraindication to haemodialysis (Hüser et al., 2025). **Step 8 — Rescue strategies**: intravenous lipid emulsion for local anaesthetic systemic toxicity (first-line) and as a last resort for other lipophilic cardiotoxicants; **VA-ECMO** for refractory cardiogenic shock or arrest, initiated early through an established ECMO pathway (Hüser et al., 2025). **Step 9 — Continuous monitoring, reassessment, documentation and psychosocial assessment** before discharge in deliberate self-poisoning (Kaswa, 2024).

### Question 10

**"Describe high-dose insulin euglycaemia therapy (HIET) for calcium channel blocker and beta-blocker poisonings, including dosing, monitoring, and potential limitations."**

HIET has become a cornerstone in the management of severe calcium channel blocker and beta-blocker poisoning with cardiogenic shock, because it directly increases myocardial contractility — in shock, the stressed myocardium switches from fatty acid to carbohydrate metabolism, and insulin improves myocardial glucose uptake and use; experimental and clinical data suggest it is haemodynamically superior to catecholamines, particularly in verapamil and diltiazem overdose (Hüser et al., 2025). **Dosing**: a bolus of short-acting insulin **1 U/kg IV**, followed by an infusion starting at **1 U/kg/h**, rapidly titrated (every 15–30 minutes) up to **10 U/kg/h** according to response, together with **dextrose** — starting with about 0.5 g/kg and adjusted to maintain blood glucose **above 5.5 mmol/L (100 mg/dL)**; dextrose may be needed for several hours after insulin is stopped (Hüser et al., 2025). **Monitoring**: frequent blood glucose (initially every 15–30 minutes), **potassium** (insulin drives potassium into cells, so hypokalaemia must be monitored and corrected), phosphate and magnesium, blood pressure, cardiac output or echocardiographic function, and urine output, in an ICU. **Onset**: improvement may be delayed for 15–30 minutes, and the full effect may take several hours, so HIET should be started **early** when CCB or BB toxicity is suspected and combined with vasopressors (Hüser et al., 2025). **Limitations**: risks of hypoglycaemia, hypokalaemia and hypophosphataemia; the need for large volumes of concentrated dextrose and central access; intensive nursing and laboratory monitoring; delayed onset; variable response; and limited availability outside ICU. HIET is not part of the CDST and is a hospital intervention; prehospitally, the ECP gives fluids, calcium, glucagon (in children with likely beta-blocker toxicity) and an adrenaline infusion within scope (HPCSA PBEC, 2025, p. 57).
