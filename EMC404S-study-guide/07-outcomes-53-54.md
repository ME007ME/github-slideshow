# Outcome 53. Comprehensively discuss the approach and management of sexual assault in the emergency care setting

## Context

South Africa has one of the highest reported rates of sexual violence in the world. A rape is estimated to occur every 12 minutes (Tilley et al., 2026, citing Action Society, 2024), and one in five South African children are likely to be victims of sexual abuse (Mesthrie, 2025, citing Cloete, 2022). Sexual violence is grossly under-reported because of stigma, fear of the perpetrator, distrust of the police, and economic dependence on the perpetrator. The ECP may be the first person to whom a survivor discloses, and the quality of that first contact influences the survivor's physical recovery, psychological recovery, access to HIV post-exposure prophylaxis (PEP), and whether justice can be pursued.

Use the term **"survivor"** (or "victim" in legal contexts, as the SOA does) and avoid language that implies blame.

## 53.1 Describe the common traumatic injuries associated with sexual assault

### Genital and anal injuries

- **Female genital injuries** most commonly involve the **posterior fourchette**, **fossa navicularis**, **labia minora** and **hymen**, typically at the 3 o'clock to 9 o'clock positions (the lower half when the patient is supine). The injury types are often summarised by the acronym **TEARS**: **T**ears (lacerations), **E**cchymosis (bruising), **A**brasions, **R**edness, and **S**welling.
- Vaginal and cervical lacerations, and occasionally severe lacerations with significant bleeding (particularly in children, postmenopausal women and assaults involving objects), and rarely vaginal vault perforation with intraperitoneal injury.
- **Anal and perianal injuries**: fissures, lacerations, bruising, sphincter injury, rectal injury, in both female and male survivors.
- **Male genital injuries**: bruising, abrasions and lacerations of the penis and scrotum.
- **Children**: hymenal transections, perineal tears, and injuries that may extend to the rectum or peritoneum; but note that many sexually abused children have **normal** examinations.

**Very important**: the **absence of genital injury does not mean that rape did not occur**. Many survivors — including the majority of adult women in many series — have no visible genital injury, because of the elasticity of genital tissue, the victim's submission under threat (which is not consent — SOA s 1(3)), delays in presentation, or the use of lubricant. The ECP must never imply disbelief because injuries are absent.

### Non-genital (extragenital) injuries

Non-genital injuries are more common than genital injuries and include:

- **Bruising of the inner thighs, upper arms (grip marks or fingertip bruises), wrists and ankles** (restraint).
- **Facial injuries**: bruising, black eyes, lacerations, fractures (nose, mandible, orbit, zygoma), dental injuries.
- **Neck injuries from strangulation** — finger-pad bruises, ligature marks, abrasions from the victim's own fingernails, **petechiae** of the face, conjunctivae and mouth, voice change, dysphagia, stridor, loss of consciousness, incontinence. **Non-fatal strangulation is a major red flag**: it can cause delayed airway oedema, carotid or vertebral artery dissection with stroke, and hypoxic brain injury, and it is a strong predictor of future homicide in intimate partner violence.
- **Bite marks** (breasts, neck, face, genitals) — these are forensic evidence (DNA from saliva, dental impressions).
- **Defence injuries** on the forearms and hands.
- **Breast injuries**: bruising, bite marks.
- **Head injuries** from blows or being pushed.
- **Abrasions on the back, buttocks and knees** from being forced to the ground (with embedded soil or grass, which is evidence).
- Stab and gunshot wounds and blunt trauma in violent assaults; injuries from falls or attempts to escape.
- **Signs of drug-facilitated assault**: altered consciousness, amnesia, intoxication (see 60.14).

### Associated non-injury harms

Pregnancy, sexually transmitted infections (including HIV, hepatitis B, syphilis, gonorrhoea, chlamydia, trichomoniasis), acute stress disorder, PTSD, depression, substance use, and suicide.

## 53.2 Formulate general principles for the assessment and management of survivors of rape and sexual assault

### Principle 1 — Safety

Ensure the safety of the survivor and the crew; the perpetrator may still be present. Request SAPS. Move the survivor to a private, safe place (for example the back of the ambulance with the doors closed).

### Principle 2 — Treat life-threatening injuries first

A full primary survey takes priority over evidence preservation. Control haemorrhage, manage the airway (especially after strangulation), treat shock, manage head injuries, and assess for intoxication or drug-facilitated assault (check glucose and GCS). **Evidence preservation never takes precedence over life-saving care**; but life-saving care can usually be delivered in a way that minimises the loss of evidence.

### Principle 3 — Trauma-informed, survivor-centred care

- Introduce yourself, explain your role, and **ask permission** before any touch, examination or intervention; explain why it is needed.
- Offer a **same-gender** provider where possible, and offer to have a trusted support person present.
- Believe the survivor; use a calm, non-judgemental, empathic tone; never ask "why" questions that imply blame ("Why were you walking there?", "Why didn't you fight back?").
- Give the survivor **choice and control** — the right to decline any part of the examination, and the choice about whether to report to SAPS (an adult survivor's choice, subject to the duties to report sexual offences against vulnerable persons — see 53.4 and 54).
- Respect privacy and confidentiality; do not discuss details in front of bystanders.
- Do not leave the survivor alone.

### Principle 4 — Limit the prehospital history to what is clinically necessary

The detailed forensic history will be taken by the forensic examiner and SAPS; repeated questioning is traumatising and may introduce inconsistencies that defence attorneys can exploit. The ECP should ask only what is needed for clinical care and safety:

- time and date of the assault (critical for PEP and emergency contraception windows and for evidence collection);
- the nature of injuries and pain;
- whether there was loss of consciousness, strangulation, or suspected drugging;
- the possibility of pregnancy;
- whether the survivor has bathed, changed, urinated, vomited or douched since the assault;
- medical history, medications and allergies;
- immediate safety concerns (is the perpetrator a household member?).

Record **any spontaneous statements in the survivor's own words**, in quotation marks.

### Principle 5 — Focused examination only

Do **not** perform a genital or internal examination in the prehospital setting unless there is life-threatening haemorrhage that requires direct pressure. Inspect visible injuries, document them carefully, and cover wounds with sterile dressings.

### Principle 6 — Preserve evidence (53.3)

### Principle 7 — Time-critical medical care: transport to a designated facility

Transport the survivor promptly to a **designated health establishment**, ideally a **Thuthuzela Care Centre (TCC)** or a hospital with a designated sexual assault service, because:

- **HIV PEP must be started as soon as possible and within 72 hours** (Horak et al., 2023), and the SOA provides that a victim who lays a charge with SAPS **or** reports the incident at a designated health establishment **within 72 hours** may receive PEP at State expense (SOA, s 28(1)–(2));
- **emergency contraception** is time-dependent;
- forensic evidence degrades with time.

Even if the survivor declines to lay a charge with SAPS, they should still be encouraged to go to the TCC or hospital for medical care, PEP, emergency contraception, and the option of having evidence collected and stored should they decide to report later.

### Principle 8 — Psychological first aid and suicide risk

Provide psychological first aid (52.6.1), assess for acute stress reactions, and screen for suicidal ideation.

### Principle 9 — Special populations

- **Children**: use the parent or caregiver if not suspected to be involved; minimal questioning; mandatory reporting to SAPS (SOA s 54) and child protection (Children's Act s 110); consider the possibility of abuse by a family member and do not return the child to an unsafe environment.
- **Persons with mental disability**: mandatory reporting under SOA s 54; adapted communication.
- **Male survivors**: face particular stigma; provide the same care and evidence preservation.
- **LGBTQI+ survivors**: may fear discrimination; provide respectful, non-judgemental care.
- **Older survivors**: higher risk of injury; consider elder abuse.

## 53.3 Discuss and apply specific procedures for the preservation of evidence in cases of sexual assault

### Why evidence matters

Physical evidence (semen, saliva, blood, hair, fibres, skin cells under fingernails, soil, condoms, objects) can identify the perpetrator through DNA, corroborate the survivor's account, and support prosecution. Evidence is fragile, is easily lost through washing, urination, eating, drinking or changing clothes, and is easily contaminated. In South Africa, forensic evidence is collected by a trained medical practitioner or forensic nurse using the SAPS **Sexual Assault Evidence Collection Kit (SAECK)**, and the medical findings are recorded on the **J88 form** (the medicolegal report used in court) (National Department of Health, 2003). The ECP does not collect forensic samples, but the ECP's actions in the first hours determine whether evidence survives.

### Procedures for the ECP

**Advise the survivor (gently, explaining why) not to do the following before the forensic examination, if possible:**

- bath, shower, wash or douche;
- change clothes or wash clothing;
- urinate or defecate (if the survivor must urinate, ask them to use a clean, dry, sealable container and keep the urine, which may be tested for drugs in suspected drug-facilitated assault, and **not to wipe** — or to keep the toilet paper in a paper bag);
- eat, drink, smoke, brush teeth or rinse the mouth (especially after oral assault);
- comb hair;
- remove tampons or sanitary pads (or, if removed, keep them in a paper bag).

These are requests, not orders: if the survivor needs to urinate or cannot wait, respect their choice and preserve what can be preserved.

**Clothing and items:**

- If clothing must be removed for treatment, **do not cut through holes, tears or stains** made by the assault; cut along seams or around damaged areas.
- Place **each item of clothing in a separate paper bag** (never plastic, which retains moisture and allows bacterial and fungal degradation of biological evidence). Do not shake clothing.
- If the survivor is lying on a sheet or blanket, fold it inwards to retain trace evidence and bag it.
- Bag any items such as condoms, tampons or objects found, separately, in paper.
- Seal, label (survivor's name, date, time, item, your name) and sign each bag.

**Handling the survivor:**

- Wear gloves at all times and change them between handling different items; minimise handling.
- **Do not clean wounds or the genital area** unless necessary for haemorrhage control or life-saving treatment; if cleaning is unavoidable, document it.
- **Do not remove anything from the survivor's body** (for example from under fingernails, from the hair, or from the skin) except to save life; if hands must be protected, paper bags may be placed over them.
- Use a clean sheet on the stretcher, and keep the sheet with the survivor's belongings.
- If the survivor vomits, collect the vomitus if practicable (especially in suspected drug-facilitated assault).

**Scene preservation:**

- The location of the assault is a crime scene: minimise entry, disturb nothing unnecessarily, note anything you had to move, and hand over to SAPS.
- Do not allow bystanders to clean up.

**Chain of custody:**

- Evidence must be accounted for continuously from collection to court. Hand over bagged items directly to SAPS (preferably) or to the forensic examiner, and record on the PRF what was handed over, to whom (name, rank, SAPS number), and at what time, obtaining a signature where possible. Never leave evidence unattended.

**Time limits**: forensic evidence is best collected within 72 hours, although some evidence may still be recoverable up to about five to seven days after the assault, so a delay should never be a reason to discourage the survivor from attending the forensic examination.

## 53.4 Identify and apply key criteria for the completion of patient report forms in relation to sexual assault

The PRF is a legal document that may be subpoenaed and used in criminal proceedings; you may be called to testify. The HPCSA requires that records be accurate, made at the time or soon after, legible, clear and unambiguous, and without confusing abbreviations (HPCSA, 2023, para 12). For sexual assault, the PRF should meet the following criteria:

1. **Accurate times**: time of call, arrival, patient contact, departure and handover; and the **reported time of the assault** (critical for PEP and evidence).
2. **Objective, factual language**: describe what you saw and did. Avoid opinions and conclusions such as "the patient was raped" (rape is a legal finding for the court) — write "patient states that she was sexually assaulted", or "alleged sexual assault".
3. **The survivor's own words in quotation marks** for key statements (for example: Patient states, "He held a knife to my throat and pushed me to the ground").
4. **The identity of the alleged perpetrator only as reported** (for example "patient reports that the assailant was her uncle"), without speculation.
5. **Detailed description of injuries**: type (bruise, abrasion, laceration, bite mark, petechiae), size (in centimetres), shape, colour, location (using anatomical landmarks and body diagrams), and any pattern. Do not estimate the age of bruises.
6. **Clinical findings**: vital signs, GCS, mental state (for example "tearful, withdrawn, shaking"), signs of intoxication or drugging, and strangulation signs.
7. **Pre-arrival actions by the survivor** (bathed, changed clothes, urinated, etc.).
8. **Treatment given** and the survivor's consent to (or refusal of) each intervention.
9. **Evidence handling**: items collected, bagged and handed over, to whom and when (chain of custody).
10. **Who was present**, including SAPS (names, ranks, station, case number if available).
11. **Notifications and reporting**: SAPS notified (and the mandatory reports for children and vulnerable persons under SOA s 54 and Children's Act s 110); information given to the survivor about PEP and the 72-hour window.
12. **Destination and handover**: name of the receiving TCC or facility and the person receiving the patient.
13. **Confidentiality**: the PRF must be protected under POPIA and the NHA; do not leave it unattended or share it with anyone not entitled to it.
14. **Completion**: legible, signed, with your name, HPCSA registration number and date; corrections made by a single line through the error with initials and date — never obliterate or alter entries after the fact.

## 53.5 Describe applicable public sector protocols in relation to post-exposure prophylaxis for survivors of sexual assault and rape

*(PEP is not prehospital ECP scope in the CDST. The ECP's role is to inform the survivor about PEP, emphasise the 72-hour window, and transport promptly to a designated facility. The details below are hospital/clinic-level information for your knowledge.)*

### Legal entitlement

In terms of the SOA (s 28), a victim who has been exposed to the risk of HIV infection as a result of a sexual offence may receive **PEP for HIV at State expense** at a designated public health establishment, receive **free medical advice** about PEP before it is administered, and apply to a magistrate for an order that the alleged offender be **tested for HIV** at State expense (ss 28 and 30–31). To receive PEP under the Act, the victim must lay a charge with SAPS **or** report the incident in the prescribed manner at a designated health establishment **within 72 hours** after the alleged offence (s 28(2)). The police official, medical practitioner or nurse to whom the offence is reported must inform the victim of the importance of obtaining PEP within 72 hours, the need for medical advice about other STIs, and the available services (s 28(3)).

### HIV PEP — current South African recommendations

According to the Southern African HIV Clinicians Society 2023 PEP guideline (Horak et al., 2023), which updates the South African National 2020 PEP guidelines:

- **Start as soon as possible, and within 72 hours** of exposure; PEP is not recommended after 72 hours.
- **Baseline HIV test** (rapid test plus fourth-generation ELISA; qualitative HIV PCR in children under two years). PEP is not given if the survivor is already HIV-positive (they are linked to antiretroviral therapy instead).
- **Regimen for persons ≥10 years and ≥30 kg**: **TDF 300 mg + 3TC 300 mg + DTG 50 mg (TLD)** as a once-daily fixed-dose combination tablet **for 28 days** (with an additional dose of dolutegravir 50 mg if on rifampicin or carbamazepine). If dolutegravir is contraindicated: TDF + FTC + ATV/r, or TDF + FTC + LPV/r. Adjusted regimens are used for impaired renal function.
- **Regimen for persons <10 years or <30 kg**: **AZT + 3TC + DTG** (paediatric weight-based dosing), with AZT + 3TC + a protease inhibitor if dolutegravir is not available, and ABC + 3TC + DTG as an alternative.
- **Follow-up HIV testing** at **six weeks** and **three months**.
- Adherence counselling and management of side effects.

### Other components of post-rape care (public sector)

- **Emergency contraception** must be offered to women and girls of reproductive age (Horak et al., 2023): commonly **levonorgestrel 1.5 mg orally as a single dose**, as soon as possible (it is most effective within 72 hours and may be used up to 120 hours), or a **copper intrauterine device** within five days; a pregnancy test is done at baseline.
- **STI prophylaxis**: should be considered for adults and adolescents, and all children with suspected penetrative injuries should receive STI prophylaxis, according to the details in the National 2020 PEP guidelines (Horak et al., 2023). South African treatment guidelines have commonly used a single-dose combination covering gonorrhoea, chlamydia and trichomoniasis (for example ceftriaxone IM, azithromycin PO and metronidazole PO); confirm the current regimen in the national guideline used at your facility.
- **Hepatitis B**: vaccination and/or hepatitis B immunoglobulin according to the survivor's vaccination and immune status (Horak et al., 2023).
- **Tetanus** vaccination where there are wounds (Horak et al., 2023).
- **Syphilis** serology.
- **Forensic examination** (SAECK and J88) with consent.
- **Psychological support and counselling**, with referral for ongoing care.
- **Follow-up visits** for HIV testing, adherence, pregnancy testing, and psychological support.

### High-yield points — Outcome 53

- Absence of genital injury does not exclude rape; TEARS; strangulation is a red flag.
- Life before evidence, but preserve evidence: no washing, eating, drinking, urinating (collect if necessary), changing; paper bags, separate items; do not cut through holes; chain of custody.
- PRF: objective, survivor's own words, times, injury descriptions, evidence handover, notifications.
- PEP: within 72 hours, TLD for 28 days (≥10 years and ≥30 kg); follow-up HIV tests at 6 weeks and 3 months; free at State expense if reported to SAPS or a designated facility within 72 hours (SOA s 28).

# Outcome 54. Discuss the intersections between emergency care, health systems and law enforcement in the context of sexual assault

## 54.1 Explain the key elements of the main criminal offences in terms of the Criminal Law (Sexual Offences and Related Matters) Amendment Act 2007, including rape and sexual assault

The Criminal Law (Sexual Offences and Related Matters) Amendment Act 32 of 2007 (the SOA) repealed the common-law crimes of rape and indecent assault and replaced them with gender-neutral statutory offences. It has since been amended, including by Act 8 of 2017 and Act 13 of 2021.

### Rape (section 3)

"Any person ('A') who **unlawfully** and **intentionally** commits an **act of sexual penetration** with a complainant ('B'), **without the consent of B**, is guilty of the offence of rape" (SOA, s 3). The elements are therefore:

1. **An act of sexual penetration** — defined in s 1 as "any act which causes penetration **to any extent whatsoever** by (a) the genital organs of one person into or beyond the genital organs, anus, or mouth of another person; (b) any other part of the body of one person or, any object, including any part of the body of an animal, into or beyond the genital organs or anus of another person; or (c) the genital organs of an animal, into or beyond the mouth of another person". Important implications: penetration "to any extent whatsoever" is sufficient (full penetration and ejaculation are not required); oral and anal penetration and penetration with fingers or objects constitute rape; and the offence is **gender-neutral** — any person (male or female) can be a perpetrator or a victim, and marital rape is included.
2. **Unlawfulness** — the absence of a ground of justification.
3. **Intention** — the perpetrator acted intentionally (including knowing, or foreseeing the possibility, that B did not consent).
4. **Absence of consent**.

### Consent (section 1(2) and (3))

"**Consent**" means "**voluntary or uncoerced agreement**" (s 1(2)). Section 1(3) lists circumstances (not exhaustive) in which B does **not** voluntarily or without coercion agree, including where B submits or is subjected to the sexual act as a result of:

- (a)(i) the **use of force or intimidation** by A against B, a third person or another person, or against their property; or (ii) a **threat of harm** against them or their property;
- (b) an **abuse of power or authority** by A to the extent that B is inhibited from indicating unwillingness or resistance;
- (c) **false pretences or fraudulent means**, including where B is led to believe that A is a different person, or that the act is something other than a sexual act;
- (d) where B is **incapable in law of appreciating the nature of the sexual act**, including where B is at the time (i) **asleep**; (ii) **unconscious**; (iii) **in an altered state of consciousness, including under the influence of any medicine, drug, alcohol or other substance**, to the extent that B's consciousness or judgement is adversely affected; (iv) **a child below the age of 12 years**; or (v) **a person with a mental disability**.

This is directly relevant to the ECP: a person who is heavily intoxicated or drugged (60.14), unconscious, asleep, or mentally disabled cannot consent. Submission is not consent.

### Compelled rape (section 4)

A person who unlawfully and intentionally compels a third person (C), without C's consent, to commit an act of sexual penetration with B, without B's consent, is guilty of compelled rape.

### Sexual assault (section 5)

"A person ('A') who unlawfully and intentionally **sexually violates** a complainant ('B'), without the consent of B, is guilty of the offence of sexual assault" (s 5(1)). A person who unlawfully and intentionally **inspires the belief** in B that B will be sexually violated is also guilty of sexual assault (s 5(2)). **Sexual violation** includes any act which causes direct or indirect contact between the genital organs, anus or female breasts of one person and any part of the body of another person, or an animal, or any object; contact between the mouth of one person and the genital organs, anus or breasts of another; and similar acts **without penetration** (s 1). Sexual assault is therefore sexual violation without penetration (for example groping, forced kissing of a sexual nature, or touching of the genitals).

Related offences include **compelled sexual assault** (s 6), **compelled self-sexual assault** (s 7), and offences of exposure, display of child pornography, sexual grooming, and **sexual intimidation** (introduced by the 2021 amendment).

### Offences against children

- Children **below 12 years** are incapable of consenting (s 1(3)(d)(iv); s 57).
- **Consensual sexual penetration** with a child aged **12 to 15 years** (statutory rape, s 15) and **consensual sexual violation** of such a child (statutory sexual assault, s 16) are offences; following the Constitutional Court's judgment in *Teddy Bear Clinic for Abused Children v Minister of Justice and Constitutional Development* (2013), the Act was amended in 2015 so that consensual sexual activity between children aged 12–15, or between a child of 12–15 and a person of 16–17 who is not more than two years older, is not criminalised. The age of consent to sex is **16 years**.
- Other offences against children include sexual exploitation, grooming, exposure to pornography and child pornography.

### Duty to report (section 54)

Section 54(1) (as amended in 2021) provides that "a person who has knowledge, reasonable belief or suspicion that a sexual offence has been committed against a **person who is vulnerable** … must report such knowledge, reasonable belief or suspicion **immediately to a police official**". "Persons who are vulnerable" include children and persons with mental disability (among others defined in s 40). Failure to report is an offence punishable by a fine or imprisonment of up to **five years**, or both (s 54(2)). This applies to **every person**, including the ECP. (For adult survivors without vulnerability, the decision to report to SAPS rests with the survivor, although the ECP should encourage and facilitate reporting.)

### Other key provisions

- Services for victims, PEP and compulsory HIV testing of alleged offenders (ss 27–39; see 53.5).
- The **National Register for Sex Offenders** (Chapter 6), which prevents convicted sex offenders from working with children and persons with mental disability.

## 54.2 Reflect on the importance of a team-based approach to the management of sexual assault cases

The needs of a survivor are medical, psychological, social, legal and safety-related, and no single professional can meet them. A **multidisciplinary, coordinated team** is essential, and this is the principle behind the **Thuthuzela Care Centres** (TCCs) — one-stop facilities, located at public hospitals, established by the **National Prosecuting Authority's Sexual Offences and Community Affairs (SOCA) Unit** with the Departments of Health, Social Development, Justice and SAPS. "Thuthuzela" is an isiXhosa word meaning "comfort". At a TCC, the survivor can receive medical care, forensic examination, PEP, counselling and assistance with reporting to SAPS in one place, and is supported through to court by case managers and prosecutors, with the aims of reducing secondary victimisation, improving conviction rates and reducing the time to finalise cases.

The team typically includes:

| Team member | Role |
|---|---|
| **EMS / ECP** | Safety, life-saving care, trauma-informed first contact, evidence preservation, information about PEP and the 72-hour window, transport to a designated facility, documentation, mandatory reporting |
| **SAPS** (including Family Violence, Child Protection and Sexual Offences (FCS) Units) | Scene control, statement taking, case number, evidence chain of custody, investigation, arrest, protection |
| **Forensic medical practitioner / forensic nurse** | Medical examination, SAECK collection, J88 completion, testimony in court |
| **Nurses and doctors** | Treatment of injuries, PEP, emergency contraception, STI prophylaxis, follow-up |
| **Social workers** | Psychosocial support, safety planning, child protection, placement of children |
| **Counsellors / psychologists / psychiatrists** | Trauma counselling, management of acute stress disorder, PTSD and depression, suicide prevention |
| **NPA prosecutors and case managers** | Court preparation, victim support through the criminal justice process |
| **NGOs** | Advocacy, shelter, long-term counselling and support |

**Why the team approach matters**:

1. **Reduces secondary victimisation** (repeated questioning, being sent from place to place, insensitive treatment).
2. **Preserves evidence and the chain of custody** through coordinated handovers.
3. **Ensures time-critical care** (PEP, emergency contraception) is delivered.
4. **Improves prosecution outcomes**.
5. **Addresses the full range of needs**, including safety, housing and mental health.
6. **Supports staff** through shared responsibility.

The ECP's contribution is critical because the first responder's attitude and actions influence whether the survivor engages with the rest of the system. Poor coordination (for example SAPS and EMS disagreeing on scene, or the survivor being taken to a facility without forensic services) can lead to lost evidence and missed PEP.

- **Thuthuzela Care Centres (TCCs)** — one-stop centres for rape survivors at public hospitals (NPA SOCA Unit).

### High-yield points — Outcome 54

- Rape (s 3): unlawful, intentional sexual penetration (to any extent) without consent; gender-neutral; includes oral, anal, digital and object penetration.
- Sexual assault (s 5): unlawful, intentional sexual violation without consent (no penetration), or inspiring the belief that one will be violated.
- Consent = voluntary or uncoerced agreement; no consent if force, threats, abuse of power, fraud, asleep, unconscious, intoxicated, <12 years, mentally disabled.
- s 54: everyone must immediately report sexual offences against vulnerable persons (children, mentally disabled) to SAPS; up to 5 years' imprisonment for failure.
- TCCs: NPA SOCA one-stop centres; multidisciplinary approach reduces secondary victimisation.
