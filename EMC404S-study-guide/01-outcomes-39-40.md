# SECTION G — BEHAVIOURAL EMERGENCIES

# Outcome 39. Discuss the identification and causes of behavioural emergencies

## 39.1 Explain what constitutes a behavioural emergency

### Definition

A **behaviour** is any observable action or response of a person to their environment. A **behavioural emergency** is a situation in which a patient's behaviour has become so abnormal, disordered or dangerous that it is unacceptable or intolerable to the patient, the family or the community, and in which there is an **immediate risk of harm** to the patient or to others, or in which the behaviour indicates an underlying condition that requires urgent intervention (NAEMT, 2021; Tintinalli et al., 2020). The key elements of the definition are therefore (1) a change in behaviour that deviates markedly from what is expected or from the person's own baseline, (2) a threat to the safety of the patient, bystanders or emergency personnel, and (3) the need for immediate intervention, whether that intervention is medical, psychiatric, social or legal.

It is important to understand that a behavioural emergency is **a presentation, not a diagnosis**. The RCEM guideline makes exactly this point about acute behavioural disturbance (ABD), stating that ABD "should not be considered a diagnosis or syndrome, but rather a clinical picture with a variety of presenting features and potential causes" (RCEM, 2025, p. 2). Similarly, Desai, Kulkarni and Verma (n.d.) emphasise that agitation in an older person "is rarely a diagnosis in itself and is more often the behavioral surface of delirium, dementia, pain, psychosis, medication toxicity, withdrawal, infection, or environmental distress". The same principle applies to every age group: the behaviour is the *surface* and the ECP's job is to look for what lies beneath it.

### What makes behaviour "abnormal"?

There is no single objective line between normal and abnormal behaviour, and judgements about behaviour are influenced by culture, religion, age and context. Behaviour is generally regarded as abnormal or disordered when it is:

1. **Statistically unusual** for the person's culture and context (for example, talking to people who are not present, or undressing in public).
2. **A marked change from the person's baseline**, as reported by family or caregivers (for example, a previously calm grandmother who has become confused and aggressive over two days, which strongly suggests delirium).
3. **Maladaptive**, in that it interferes with the person's ability to function, care for themselves, or maintain relationships.
4. **Distressing** to the person or to those around them.
5. **Dangerous** to the person or to others.

In South Africa, cultural context is especially important. Behaviour such as communication with ancestors, or the experience of *ukuthwasa* (the calling to become a traditional healer), may be culturally normative and should not automatically be labelled as psychosis (Tilley et al., 2026, citing Kirmayer, 2022). Tilley et al. (2026) note that South Africa's multicultural context "can aggravate the need for urgent western mental health care, and complicate health seeking behaviour further". The ECP should ask family members whether the behaviour is considered normal within their cultural or religious framework.

### Types of behavioural emergency encountered prehospitally

In practice, the ECP will encounter the following broad presentations:

- **Acute agitation and aggression**, including violent behaviour directed at others, property, or emergency personnel.
- **Acute behavioural disturbance (ABD)** — a severe agitated state with abnormal physiology, which may include agitation, constant physical activity, bizarre behaviour (including paranoia and hypervigilance), fear and panic, unusual strength, sustained non-compliance with police or ambulance staff, insensitivity to pain, hyperthermia and sweating, tachypnoea and tachycardia (RCEM, 2025, adapted from Gonin et al., 2018). ABD is a medical emergency because of the risk of sudden cardiac arrest from metabolic acidosis, hyperthermia and catecholamine excess.
- **Suicidal ideation, suicide attempts and deliberate self-harm.**
- **Acute psychosis** (hallucinations, delusions, disorganised behaviour).
- **Severe anxiety and panic states.**
- **Withdrawn, catatonic or mute states.**
- **Confusion and delirium**, especially in older persons.
- **Intoxication and withdrawal states.**
- **Behavioural disturbance in children and adolescents**, and in persons with intellectual disability or autism.

### The scale of the problem in South African EMS

Behavioural emergencies form a large and growing part of the South African prehospital caseload. In a census of three years (2017–2019) of Western Cape Government EMS incident records from the rural Garden Route District, Tilley et al. (2026) identified 2,976 mental-health-related incidents. Of these, 52% were overdose or deliberate self-poisoning, 7.6% suicidal ideation, 3.6% substance abuse, 3.4% death by suicide, 3% depression, 2.7% attempted suicide, 2% anxiety, 1.3% cutting self-harm, 0.7% bipolar disorder, 0.6% schizophrenia and 0.1% post-traumatic stress disorder. Tilley et al. (2026) argue that, because deinstitutionalisation under the Mental Health Care Act 17 of 2002 was not accompanied by adequate community mental health services, "EMS is de facto relied upon as a primary health care point for all poor mental health sequelae and related emergencies". This is an important point to make in an examination answer about the role of EMS: the ECP is often the first, and sometimes the only, health professional that a person in a mental health crisis will see (Evans, Geduld and Stassen, 2018).

### High-yield points — 39.1

- A behavioural emergency = abnormal behaviour + risk of harm + need for immediate intervention.
- It is a **presentation, not a diagnosis**. Always look for the cause, especially an organic (medical) cause.
- Behaviour must be judged against the person's baseline and cultural context.
- ABD (agitation, strength, pain insensitivity, hyperthermia, tachycardia) is a life-threatening medical emergency.
- In the Western Cape, 52% of EMS mental-health incidents were overdose/self-poisoning (Tilley et al., 2026).

## 39.2 Compare potential causes for behavioural emergencies, including situational causes, organic causes and psychiatric illnesses

The causes of behavioural emergencies are classically divided into three groups: **organic (biological/medical) causes**, **psychiatric (functional) causes**, and **situational (psychosocial/environmental) causes** (NAEMT, 2021; Tintinalli et al., 2020). In reality these groups overlap: a person with schizophrenia may become agitated because of a urinary tract infection, an older person with dementia may become aggressive because of pain and an unfamiliar environment, and a person who has just been assaulted may be intoxicated and have a head injury. The ECP must therefore consider all three groups in every patient and must **always exclude organic causes first**, because they are frequently life-threatening and are treatable.

### Organic (medical) causes

An organic cause is a physical disease, injury or substance that alters brain function and thereby produces abnormal behaviour. The RCEM (2025) lists the following factors that may lead to an ABD presentation, noting that substance intoxication and mental health conditions are the most common:

- substance intoxication
- sepsis
- mental health conditions
- head injury
- substance withdrawal
- anticholinergic syndrome
- serotonin syndrome
- neuroleptic malignant syndrome
- hypoxia
- thyroid storm
- hypoglycaemia
- heat stroke
- electrolyte disturbance
- seizures (including post-ictal states).

A useful way to remember the organic causes in the field is to think systematically:

| Category | Examples |
|---|---|
| **Hypoxia and hypercapnia** | Respiratory failure, pneumonia, asthma, COPD exacerbation, carbon monoxide poisoning, pulmonary embolism, shock |
| **Metabolic and endocrine** | Hypoglycaemia (the single most important reversible cause to exclude — check the glucose in every patient), hyperglycaemic states (DKA, HHS), hyponatraemia and other electrolyte disturbances, uraemia, hepatic encephalopathy, thyrotoxicosis/thyroid storm, hypothyroidism, Wernicke's encephalopathy (thiamine deficiency) |
| **Neurological** | Head injury (concussion, intracranial haemorrhage), stroke, seizures and post-ictal states, CNS infections (meningitis, encephalitis, cerebral malaria, HIV-associated infections such as cryptococcal meningitis and toxoplasmosis), brain tumours, dementia |
| **Infection and sepsis** | Urinary tract infection and pneumonia in the elderly, sepsis of any cause, HIV and its opportunistic infections, neurosyphilis |
| **Toxicological** | Intoxication with stimulants (methamphetamine "tik", cocaine), alcohol, cannabis, hallucinogens, inhalants; withdrawal from alcohol (delirium tremens) or benzodiazepines; anticholinergic toxicity (for example *Datura stramonium*); serotonin syndrome; neuroleptic malignant syndrome; medication side effects |
| **Thermal** | Heat stroke, hypothermia |
| **Cardiovascular** | Hypotension and hypoperfusion, arrhythmias, hypertensive encephalopathy |

**Clinical clues that suggest an organic cause** (sometimes called "red flags") include: (1) acute onset over hours to days; (2) first presentation of "psychiatric" symptoms after the age of about 40 years with no previous psychiatric history; (3) a fluctuating level of consciousness and disorientation to time and place; (4) visual, tactile or olfactory hallucinations (in contrast, primary psychiatric illness more typically causes auditory hallucinations); (5) abnormal vital signs (fever, tachycardia, hypotension, hypoxia, hypertension with bradycardia); (6) focal neurological signs, abnormal pupils, or a recent head injury; (7) evidence of drug or alcohol use; and (8) poor attention and impaired memory (Tintinalli et al., 2020; Inouye, Westendorp and Saczynski, 2014). Desai, Kulkarni and Verma (n.d.) summarise the single most important principle as follows: "behavior change should be treated as medically caused until a more specific explanation proves otherwise".

### Psychiatric (functional) causes

Psychiatric causes are mental disorders in which no structural or metabolic brain abnormality explains the behaviour on routine examination (the older term "functional" reflects this). The principal psychiatric causes of behavioural emergencies are:

- **psychotic disorders** (schizophrenia and its spectrum, brief psychotic disorder), which may produce agitation driven by paranoid delusions or command hallucinations;
- **bipolar disorder**, particularly mania with grandiosity, irritability, disinhibition and agitation;
- **depressive disorders**, with suicidal ideation, self-harm, psychomotor retardation, or agitated depression;
- **anxiety disorders**, including panic attacks;
- **trauma- and stressor-related disorders** such as acute stress disorder and PTSD;
- **personality disorders**, particularly borderline and antisocial personality disorders;
- **substance use disorders** (which overlap with the organic group);
- **neurodevelopmental disorders** such as intellectual disability and autism spectrum disorder, where behavioural distress is often triggered by a change in routine, sensory overload or pain that the person cannot communicate.

Psychomotor agitation occurs in approximately 11–13% of patients with mood disorders or schizophrenia, 20–30% of those with anxiety disorders, 25% of those with alcohol use disorder, 24–45% of those with dementia and 35% of those with substance use disorder (Saucedo Uribe et al., 2025). Clinical features that favour a primary psychiatric cause include a known psychiatric history, a gradual onset, a clear sensorium with orientation preserved, auditory rather than visual hallucinations, normal vital signs, and an age at first presentation of between about 15 and 35 years for schizophrenia.

### Situational (psychosocial and environmental) causes

Situational causes are external stressors that overwhelm a person's coping mechanisms and produce an acute behavioural reaction in a person who may have no underlying mental illness. Examples include:

- the sudden death of a loved one, or receiving devastating news;
- being the victim of violent crime, rape, domestic violence or a motor-vehicle collision;
- witnessing a traumatic event (an acute stress reaction);
- loss of a job, financial crisis or eviction;
- relationship breakdown;
- natural disasters and mass-casualty incidents;
- exposure to a frightening or overstimulating environment, such as a crowded emergency department or the back of an ambulance — which is a particularly important trigger in older persons with dementia and in persons with autism (Desai, Kulkarni and Verma, n.d.).

South Africa's social context produces an exceptionally heavy burden of situational stress. Tilley et al. (2026) note that 63% of South Africans live in poverty, 31.9% are unemployed, a rape is estimated to occur every 12 minutes, there are 86 murders and 595 assaults reported daily, and 18.9% of the population abuse alcohol and/or drugs. They describe these as "social antecedents for suicidality and suicide", and introduce the concept of suicide as a **syndemic** — a situation in which poverty, violence, trauma, substance use, chronic illness, stigma and mental illness interact with and amplify each other (Tilley et al., 2026, citing Caulkins, 2022).

### Comparison of the three groups

| Feature | Organic | Psychiatric (functional) | Situational |
|---|---|---|---|
| Onset | Usually acute (hours–days) | Usually gradual, may relapse acutely | Acute, linked to an identifiable event |
| Age | Any; new-onset behaviour >40 years strongly suggests organic | Schizophrenia typically 15–35 years | Any |
| Level of consciousness | Often altered or fluctuating | Usually clear | Clear (may be distressed) |
| Orientation | Often disoriented | Usually oriented | Oriented |
| Attention | Impaired | Usually intact (may be distracted) | Usually intact |
| Hallucinations | Visual, tactile, olfactory more common | Auditory most common | Usually absent |
| Vital signs | Often abnormal | Usually normal | May show sympathetic arousal |
| Physical signs | Often present (focal signs, fever, smell of alcohol, track marks, trauma) | Usually absent | Injuries if assaulted |
| Psychiatric history | Often absent | Often present | Often absent |
| Response to reassurance | Poor | Variable | Usually good |
| Priority for the ECP | Identify and treat the cause; this may be life-threatening | Safety, de-escalation, transport for psychiatric assessment | Psychological first aid, support, referral |

### High-yield points — 39.2

- Three causes: organic, psychiatric, situational. **Always exclude organic causes first.**
- Check the **blood glucose**, oxygen saturation, temperature and pupils in every behavioural emergency.
- Red flags for organic cause: acute onset, age >40 at first presentation, fluctuating consciousness, disorientation, visual hallucinations, abnormal vital signs, focal neurological signs.
- Substance intoxication and mental illness are the most common factors in ABD (RCEM, 2025).
- In South Africa, poverty, violence, unemployment and substance abuse are powerful situational drivers (Tilley et al., 2026).

## 39.3 Explain psychiatric symptoms and signs associated with disorders of consciousness, motor activity, speech, thinking, mood and affect, memory, orientation, perception and intelligence

This outcome tests your vocabulary of descriptive psychopathology — the terms used to describe what you observe and what the patient tells you. Using correct terminology on the PRF and at handover communicates precisely and protects you legally. The definitions below follow the standard descriptions in *Kaplan & Sadock's Synopsis of Psychiatry* (Boland, Verduin and Ruiz, 2021) and the DSM-5-TR (American Psychiatric Association, 2022).

### Disorders of consciousness

**Consciousness** is a state of awareness of the self and the environment. Disorders of consciousness strongly suggest an **organic** cause.

- **Clouding of consciousness**: incomplete clear-mindedness with disturbances in perception and attitudes.
- **Delirium**: an acute, fluctuating disturbance of attention and awareness with disorganised thinking, often with restlessness, disorientation and hallucinations (see 52.9.1).
- **Drowsiness, stupor and coma**: progressively reduced responsiveness. Stupor is a state in which the patient responds only to vigorous stimulation; coma is complete unresponsiveness. Note that **psychiatric stupor** (for example catatonic stupor or depressive stupor) also exists, in which the patient is unresponsive yet the eyes may be open and track, the patient may resist eye opening, and there are no neurological signs.
- **Hypervigilance**: excessive attention to and focus on all internal and external stimuli, seen in paranoid states, stimulant intoxication and PTSD.
- **Distractibility**: inability to concentrate; attention is drawn to unimportant stimuli (mania, delirium).
- **Dissociation**: a disruption in the normal integration of consciousness, memory, identity and perception (for example depersonalisation, derealisation, dissociative fugue), seen after severe trauma.

### Disorders of motor activity (behaviour)

- **Psychomotor agitation**: excessive motor and cognitive activity, usually non-productive and in response to inner tension (for example pacing, wringing of hands, pulling at clothing, inability to sit still) (Saucedo Uribe et al., 2025).
- **Psychomotor retardation**: slowing of movement, speech and thought; seen in severe depression.
- **Catatonia**: a syndrome of motor abnormalities including stupor, mutism, negativism (resistance to instructions), posturing (holding a posture against gravity), catalepsy, waxy flexibility (limbs remain where placed by the examiner), echolalia and echopraxia (mimicking speech or movements), stereotypy, mannerisms and grimacing (see 52.3).
- **Stereotypies**: repetitive, purposeless movements (for example rocking), seen in autism and schizophrenia.
- **Mannerisms**: odd but goal-directed movements.
- **Compulsions**: repetitive behaviours performed in response to an obsession (for example handwashing in OCD).
- **Tics**: sudden, rapid, recurrent non-rhythmic movements or vocalisations (Tourette's disorder).
- **Akathisia**: subjective feeling of restlessness with an inability to sit still, commonly a side effect of antipsychotics — important because it can be mistaken for worsening agitation (RCEM, 2025).
- **Dystonia**: sustained muscle contraction producing twisting, abnormal postures (for example oculogyric crisis, torticollis), an acute side effect of haloperidol and other dopamine antagonists.
- **Tremor, rigidity and bradykinesia**: suggest Parkinson's disease, drug-induced parkinsonism or neuroleptic malignant syndrome.
- **Aggression and violence**: forceful, goal-directed action that may be verbal or physical.

### Disorders of speech

Speech is described in terms of its **rate, volume, quantity, tone and fluency**.

- **Pressure of speech**: rapid, increased amount of speech that is difficult to interrupt (mania, stimulant intoxication).
- **Poverty of speech**: restricted amount of speech, with monosyllabic answers (depression, negative symptoms of schizophrenia).
- **Poverty of content**: adequate amount of speech but conveying little information because it is vague or repetitive.
- **Mutism**: complete absence of speech (catatonia, severe depression, conversion disorder, selective mutism in children).
- **Dysarthria**: difficulty articulating words (a neurological or intoxication sign, not a thought disorder).
- **Aphasia (dysphasia)**: a disturbance in language comprehension or expression (expressive/Broca's, receptive/Wernicke's) due to brain damage, for example stroke — an organic sign that is easily mistaken for confusion or psychosis.
- **Echolalia**: repetition of the examiner's words.
- **Neologisms**: invented new words (schizophrenia).
- **Clang associations**: words chosen for their sound rather than their meaning, for example rhyming (mania).
- **Word salad**: an incoherent mixture of words and phrases.

### Disorders of thinking (thought)

Thinking is assessed in terms of **form (process)** — how thoughts are linked — and **content** — what the person is thinking about.

**Disorders of thought form (formal thought disorder):**

- **Flight of ideas**: rapid, continuous jumping from one idea to another with understandable connections (mania).
- **Loosening of associations (derailment)**: ideas shift from one subject to an unrelated one without the speaker being aware (schizophrenia).
- **Tangentiality**: the person drifts away from the point and never returns to it.
- **Circumstantiality**: the person gives excessive, unnecessary detail but eventually reaches the point.
- **Thought blocking**: an abrupt interruption in the train of thought before an idea is finished (schizophrenia).
- **Perseveration**: persistent repetition of the same response beyond the point of relevance (organic brain disease).
- **Incoherence**: thought that is generally not understandable.

**Disorders of thought content:**

- **Delusion**: a fixed, false belief, out of keeping with the person's cultural and educational background, that is held with conviction despite evidence to the contrary. Types include **persecutory** (paranoid) delusions (being followed, poisoned, bewitched), **grandiose** delusions (special powers or identity), **delusions of reference** (the television or radio is talking about the person), **nihilistic** delusions (the person is dead or their organs are rotting — seen in psychotic depression), **somatic** delusions, **delusional jealousy**, **erotomania**, and **delusions of control**, including **thought insertion, thought withdrawal and thought broadcasting** (classic features of schizophrenia).
- **Overvalued idea**: an unreasonable, sustained false belief held with less than delusional intensity (for example the overvalued idea of being fat in anorexia nervosa).
- **Obsession**: a recurrent, intrusive, unwanted thought, image or urge that the person recognises as the product of their own mind and tries to resist (OCD).
- **Phobia**: persistent, irrational, exaggerated fear of a specific stimulus or situation.
- **Suicidal and homicidal ideation**: thoughts of killing oneself or others — **always ask about these directly** (see Outcome 45).
- **Preoccupation**: thoughts focused on a particular idea, often with strong emotion (for example hopelessness, guilt).

### Disorders of mood and affect

**Mood** is the pervasive and sustained emotion described by the patient (subjective, for example "I feel hopeless"). **Affect** is the outward expression of emotion as observed by the examiner (objective). A useful analogy is that mood is the "climate" and affect is the "weather".

- **Euthymic**: normal mood.
- **Depressed / dysphoric**: low, sad, unpleasant mood.
- **Elevated / euphoric / expansive**: abnormally high mood (mania, stimulant intoxication).
- **Irritable**: easily annoyed and provoked to anger (mania, intoxication, withdrawal, personality disorders).
- **Anxious**: apprehension and tension.
- **Anhedonia**: loss of interest in and enjoyment of usual activities (core symptom of depression).

Affect is described according to its **range** (full, restricted, **blunted** — severely reduced intensity, **flat** — virtually absent), its **appropriateness** (whether it matches the content of speech; for example **inappropriate affect** — laughing while describing a tragedy, seen in schizophrenia), its **reactivity**, and its **stability** (**labile affect** — rapid, abrupt changes in emotional expression unrelated to external stimuli, seen in mania, delirium, intoxication, frontal lobe damage and borderline personality disorder).

### Disorders of memory

Memory is the ability to store and retrieve information. It is described as **immediate** (registration — recall within seconds, for example repeating three words), **recent/short-term** (minutes to days, for example what the patient ate for breakfast), and **remote/long-term** (years, for example date of birth, childhood events).

- **Amnesia**: partial or total inability to recall past experiences. **Anterograde amnesia** is the loss of memory for events after an incident (for example after a head injury or after benzodiazepines/GHB — relevant to drug-facilitated sexual assault). **Retrograde amnesia** is the loss of memory for events before the incident.
- **Confabulation**: unconscious filling of memory gaps with imagined or untrue experiences that the patient believes — classically seen in **Korsakoff syndrome** (chronic thiamine deficiency due to alcohol use disorder).
- **Dissociative amnesia**: inability to recall important personal information, usually of a traumatic nature, that is inconsistent with ordinary forgetting.
- Progressive loss of **recent** memory with relative preservation of remote memory is typical of **Alzheimer's disease**.

### Disorders of orientation

Orientation is the awareness of **time, place and person** (and some include situation). Disorientation is classically lost in the order **time first, then place, then person**. Disorientation is a feature of **organic** brain disease (delirium, dementia, intoxication, head injury, hypoxia, hypoglycaemia). A patient with a primary psychotic illness is usually fully oriented, even when severely deluded. Note that the Glasgow Coma Scale verbal "confused" response is not a substitute for a structured assessment of orientation or capacity; Stander, Hodkinson and Dippenaar (2021) found that South African prehospital providers frequently used the GCS to assess mental capacity, which is an inappropriate use of a scale designed for acute cerebral injury.

### Disorders of perception

Perception is the process of transferring physical stimulation into psychological information.

- **Hallucination**: a false sensory perception occurring in the absence of any external stimulus. Hallucinations may be **auditory** (most common in schizophrenia; for example voices commenting on the person's actions or **command hallucinations** instructing the person to harm themselves or others, which represent a major risk factor for violence and suicide), **visual** (more typical of organic causes such as delirium, alcohol withdrawal, hallucinogens and Lewy body dementia), **tactile** (for example the feeling of insects crawling under the skin — formication — in cocaine/methamphetamine intoxication and alcohol withdrawal), **olfactory** and **gustatory** (temporal lobe epilepsy and other organic causes).
- **Illusion**: a misperception or misinterpretation of a **real** external stimulus (for example mistaking a coat on a hook for an intruder in a darkened room), common in delirium.
- **Hypnagogic and hypnopompic hallucinations**: hallucinations occurring while falling asleep or waking up; these are normal phenomena.
- **Depersonalisation and derealisation**: the sense that oneself or the environment is unreal or changed.

### Disorders of intelligence

**Intelligence** is the ability to understand, recall, mobilise and integrate previously learned knowledge constructively in meeting new situations.

- **Intellectual disability (intellectual developmental disorder)**: deficits in intellectual functioning (reasoning, problem solving, planning, abstract thinking, judgement, academic and experiential learning) **and** in adaptive functioning, with onset during the developmental period (APA, 2022) (see 52.1.1).
- **Dementia**: an acquired, global decline of cognitive function from a previously higher level, without impairment of consciousness (see 52.9.2).
- **Pseudodementia**: cognitive impairment that mimics dementia but is due to depression, and which improves when the depression is treated.

Intelligence is relevant prehospitally because it affects the patient's ability to communicate symptoms (including pain), to understand information, and therefore their **capacity to consent or refuse**. The ECP must adapt communication to the person's level of understanding, use simple language, and involve a trusted caregiver.

### Insight and judgement

Although not listed in the outcome, you should also be able to describe **insight** (the patient's awareness and understanding of their own illness — poor insight is typical of psychosis and mania and is a major reason why patients refuse care) and **judgement** (the ability to assess a situation correctly and act appropriately). Both are essential components of capacity (Evans, Geduld and Stassen, 2018, note that patients who have attempted suicide "may have limited insight into their illness and a restricted ability to cooperate with treatment").

### High-yield points — 39.3

- Disorientation and fluctuating consciousness = organic until proven otherwise.
- Visual/tactile hallucinations suggest organic or toxic causes; auditory (especially command) hallucinations suggest psychosis and increase the risk of violence and suicide.
- Delusion = fixed false belief out of keeping with culture; illusion = misinterpretation of a real stimulus; hallucination = perception without a stimulus.
- Mood (subjective) versus affect (objective).
- Confabulation = Korsakoff syndrome.
- Akathisia and dystonia are drug side effects that can mimic agitation.

## 39.4 Apply effective techniques for interviewing a patient during a behavioural emergency

### Scene safety comes before the interview

Before any interview begins, the ECP must ensure that the scene is safe. Evans, Geduld and Stassen (2018) found that more than half of South African prehospital providers in their survey identified threats to personal safety during the care of suicidal patients; three had been injured (bite wounds, facial scratching and blunt assault), two had witnessed patients stabbing themselves, and one reported a patient jumping from a moving ambulance. Another unpublished South African study cited by the same authors found that 56% of 158 prehospital personnel had been assaulted on duty, consistent with international reports of violence against prehospital providers of 61.0–87.5%. The following safety principles therefore apply to every behavioural interview:

1. Obtain as much information as possible from dispatch (Is there a weapon? Is the patient known? Has SAPS been dispatched?) and **stage away from the scene until SAPS secure it** if there is any suggestion of a weapon or violence.
2. On arrival, scan for weapons and potential weapons (knives, bottles, ligatures, medication), and remove them from the area where it is safe to do so.
3. Position yourself so that **both you and the patient have an exit**, never stand between the patient and the door in a way that makes them feel trapped, and never allow the patient to stand between you and your only exit (RCEM, 2025).
4. Stay **out of arm's reach** — approximately two arm-lengths away (Richmond et al., 2012).
5. Never turn your back on the patient and never interview alone if the patient is potentially violent; have a colleague present who is not directly engaged but who observes.
6. Be aware of your own body language and keep your hands visible.

### General principles of the interview

The aim of the interview is to (1) establish rapport and calm the patient, (2) gather enough information to identify the likely cause (organic, psychiatric or situational) and assess risk, (3) assess capacity, and (4) plan safe management and transport. The following techniques are recommended in emergency psychiatric practice (Richmond et al., 2012; Tintinalli et al., 2020; NAEMT, 2021):

- **Introduce yourself** clearly by name and role, explain why you are there, and tell the patient that your aim is to keep them safe (RCEM, 2025).
- **Have only one person speak** to the patient; multiple voices increase confusion and agitation (RCEM, 2025).
- **Choose a quiet, private, low-stimulus environment** where possible — turn off the television or radio, ask unnecessary bystanders to leave, and switch off sirens and flashing lights near the patient.
- **Speak calmly, slowly and clearly**, in short sentences, and give the patient time to respond; repetition may be needed (RCEM, 2025).
- **Use the patient's preferred language** or a trusted interpreter. In South Africa, language barriers between provider and patient are common and are a frequent cause of misunderstanding; the HPCSA (2023, para 8.1.7) states that where a linguistic or cultural barrier exists, "an interpreter who is culturally sensitised and fluent in the language used by the patient should be present". Avoid using children as interpreters, and be cautious when using a family member who may be the abuser in cases of domestic violence.
- **Use open-ended questions first** ("Tell me what has been happening today"), followed by closed questions to clarify specific facts ("Have you taken any tablets?").
- **Listen actively**: show that you are listening by nodding, paraphrasing ("So you are saying that…"), summarising, and using clarifying statements (RCEM, 2025).
- **Acknowledge and validate feelings** ("It sounds like you are very frightened") without necessarily agreeing with delusional content.
- **Do not argue with delusions or hallucinations**, and do not pretend to share them. A suitable response is: "I can't hear the voices, but I can see they are really upsetting you."
- **Be honest.** Do not lie to or trick the patient. Stander, Hodkinson and Dippenaar (2021) found that South African prehospital providers frequently used deception to get mental health care users to agree to transport; this damages trust, may escalate the situation when the deception is discovered, and undermines future engagement with health services.
- **Be non-judgemental.** Avoid sarcasm, threats, accusations and moralising. Evans, Geduld and Stassen (2018) documented negative attitudes among South African providers, with some describing suicidal patients as "a waste of time", "attention seeking" or "pretending". Such attitudes are unprofessional, are associated with poorer care, and can directly increase the risk of a patient refusing help.
- **Maintain non-threatening body language**: stand at an angle rather than face-on, keep your hands open and visible, avoid prolonged direct eye contact (which may be perceived as threatening) but do not avoid eye contact entirely, and avoid sudden movements.
- **Explain everything before you do it**, especially before touching the patient or taking vital signs.
- **Offer choices** where possible (for example "Would you like to walk to the ambulance or sit on the stretcher?"), as this gives the patient a sense of control (RCEM, 2025).
- **Set clear limits** in a matter-of-fact, non-threatening way (for example "I can't let you hurt yourself or anyone else") (RCEM, 2025).
- **Involve family or friends** if their presence calms the patient; ask them to leave if their presence escalates the situation. Evans, Geduld and Stassen (2018) reported that some providers found family helpful in convincing patients to accept transport, but others found that family "get in the way" or were "more difficult than the patient".

### Content of the interview — what to ask

A structured interview in a behavioural emergency should cover:

1. **The presenting problem**: what happened, when it started, what triggered it, and whether there is a history of similar episodes.
2. **Collateral history** from family, friends, bystanders and SAPS (this is often the most valuable information, particularly regarding baseline behaviour, onset, substances taken, and recent stressors) (RCEM, 2025; Desai, Kulkarni and Verma, n.d.).
3. **Medical history**: diabetes, epilepsy, head injury, HIV status and ARV use, thyroid disease, recent illness or infection, chronic medication and recent changes in medication.
4. **Psychiatric history**: previous diagnoses, admissions, psychiatric medication, compliance with medication, and previous self-harm or violence.
5. **Substance use**: alcohol, cannabis ("dagga"), methamphetamine ("tik"), methaqualone ("mandrax"), heroin or "nyaope/whoonga", cocaine, prescription medications; time of last use (to anticipate withdrawal).
6. **Risk assessment**: **ask directly about suicidal and homicidal thoughts, intent and plans** (see Outcome 45 for specific questions); ask about access to weapons, firearms, medication and other means.
7. **Social history**: living arrangements, support system, recent losses, domestic violence and safety at home.
8. **The patient's own understanding and wishes** (insight), which forms part of the capacity assessment.

### Capacity assessment during the interview

Capacity (the ability to make a specific decision at a specific time) is central to every behavioural emergency, because it determines whether a refusal of treatment or transport can be accepted. A patient has decision-making capacity for a particular decision if they can: (1) **understand** the relevant information (the nature of their condition, the proposed treatment, alternatives, and the consequences of refusal); (2) **retain** that information long enough to make a decision; (3) **use and weigh** the information to arrive at a choice; and (4) **communicate** that choice. The HPCSA (2023, para 8.1.2) states that the decision of a competent adult patient to refuse a particular medical intervention "must be respected, even where this would result in serious harm to them or in their own death unless the patient's state of mind is such that he/she is incapable of taking or participating in the decision". The ECP should document the capacity assessment explicitly on the PRF, including the questions asked and the patient's responses.

### Special interviewing situations

- **The paranoid patient**: maintain greater physical distance, avoid touching, be transparent about everything you do, and do not whisper to colleagues.
- **The manic patient**: be firm and brief, avoid being drawn into arguments, and reduce stimulation.
- **The depressed or withdrawn patient**: allow silences, be patient, use open questions, and always ask about suicide.
- **The intoxicated patient**: keep the interview short and focused on safety; repeat the assessment as intoxication wears off; do not assume that intoxication is the only problem (head injury, hypoglycaemia and co-ingestion are common).
- **The patient with intellectual disability or autism**: speak to someone known to the patient, use simple concrete language, reduce sensory stimulation, and consult any available care plan (RCEM, 2025).
- **The older patient with suspected dementia or delirium**: approach from the front, introduce yourself repeatedly if necessary, restore hearing aids and glasses, and obtain collateral history about baseline cognition (Desai, Kulkarni and Verma, n.d.).

### High-yield points — 39.4

- Safety first: exit route, two arm-lengths, no weapons, SAPS if violent, never alone.
- One voice, calm, short sentences, open questions, active listening, validate feelings.
- Never argue with or collude with delusions; never lie to the patient.
- Always obtain collateral history and always ask directly about suicidal and homicidal intent.
- Assess and document capacity: understand, retain, weigh, communicate.

# Outcome 40. Discuss the utility of MMSE in an emergency care setting

## What the MMSE is

The **Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE)** is a brief, structured, 30-point screening test of cognitive function that was developed by Folstein, Folstein and McHugh (1975). It takes approximately 5–10 minutes to administer and assesses the following cognitive domains:

| Domain | Task | Points |
|---|---|---|
| Orientation to time | Year, season, date, day, month | 5 |
| Orientation to place | Country, province, town, building/place, floor/room | 5 |
| Registration (immediate memory) | Repeat three unrelated words | 3 |
| Attention and calculation | Serial sevens (100 − 7 = 93, etc., five times) or spell "WORLD" backwards | 5 |
| Recall (short-term memory) | Recall the three words | 3 |
| Language — naming | Name two objects (for example a pen and a watch) | 2 |
| Language — repetition | Repeat "No ifs, ands or buts" | 1 |
| Language — three-stage command | "Take this paper in your right hand, fold it in half and put it on the floor" | 3 |
| Reading | Read and obey "Close your eyes" | 1 |
| Writing | Write a sentence | 1 |
| Visuospatial | Copy intersecting pentagons | 1 |
| **Total** | | **30** |

A score of **24–30** is generally regarded as normal, **18–23** suggests mild cognitive impairment, and **0–17** suggests severe impairment, although the commonly used single cut-off is **≤23 or ≤24** out of 30 (Folstein, Folstein and McHugh, 1975). Scores must be interpreted in the light of the patient's **education, literacy, language and culture**, because the MMSE is strongly affected by these factors.

## Utility (usefulness) of the MMSE in the emergency care setting

The outcome asks you to *discuss the utility*, which means you must give both the **advantages** and the **limitations**, and reach a balanced conclusion.

### Advantages and appropriate uses

1. **Objective, standardised and reproducible.** The MMSE provides a numerical score that can be documented and compared over time, allowing the receiving facility to detect improvement or deterioration (for example as delirium resolves or as intoxication wears off).
2. **Screens multiple cognitive domains quickly**, including orientation, memory, attention, language and visuospatial ability.
3. **Helps differentiate organic from functional presentations.** Impaired orientation, attention and recall suggest an organic cause (delirium, dementia, intoxication), whereas a patient with a primary psychotic illness usually scores relatively well despite severe abnormalities of thought content.
4. **Contributes to capacity assessment.** A very low score supports a conclusion that the patient may lack capacity for complex decisions, although the MMSE is **not** itself a test of capacity.
5. **Widely known** and used in hospitals, so a prehospital score is meaningful at handover.
6. **Supports documentation** of the patient's mental state when a refusal of treatment or transport is being considered, or when involuntary care under the MHCA is being considered.

The Behavioural Emergencies Tutorial 1 memo summarises this well: cognitive screens such as the MMSE and the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) "aid in differentiating delirium, dementia, or intoxication and guide treatment planning" and influence decisions about "psychiatric hold, urgent imaging or neurology consult, pharmacological intervention, or liaison with mental health services".

### Limitations in the emergency (and especially the prehospital) setting

1. **Time.** Five to ten minutes is long in an emergency, and the MMSE is not suitable for a patient who is unstable, violent or severely agitated. Scene safety and resuscitation always take priority.
2. **Requires cooperation and adequate consciousness.** It cannot be performed on an agitated, aggressive, intoxicated, sedated or drowsy patient, which describes many behavioural emergency patients.
3. **Strongly influenced by education, literacy, language and culture.** Items such as serial sevens, reading, writing and spelling "WORLD" backwards disadvantage patients with limited formal education, patients who are not English-speaking, and patients from different cultural backgrounds. This is a major limitation in South Africa, which has eleven official languages (twelve, since South African Sign Language was recognised in 2023) and large disparities in educational attainment. A low score in a patient with little schooling may be falsely interpreted as cognitive impairment.
4. **Affected by sensory impairment** (poor hearing or vision), aphasia and physical disability (for example inability to write).
5. **Insensitive to mild impairment and to executive (frontal lobe) dysfunction.** The MoCA (Nasreddine et al., 2005) is more sensitive for mild cognitive impairment.
6. **It does not diagnose delirium.** The MMSE measures cognitive performance at one point in time; delirium is defined by **acute onset, a fluctuating course and inattention**, which a single MMSE cannot capture. A dedicated delirium tool such as the **Confusion Assessment Method (CAM)** (Inouye et al., 1990), or its brief version, the **4AT**, is more appropriate.
7. **It does not assess thought content, perception, mood, suicidality, insight or judgement**, which are often the most important issues in a behavioural emergency.
8. **It does not assess capacity.** Capacity is decision-specific; a patient with a low MMSE may still have capacity for a simple decision, and a patient with a normal MMSE may lack capacity because of delusions or severe depression.
9. **Copyright** — the MMSE is protected by copyright, which limits its free use in some services.
10. **Practical environment** — noise, lack of privacy, poor lighting and the presence of bystanders on scene all reduce validity.

### Conclusion on utility

The MMSE has **limited utility as a routine prehospital tool** in acute behavioural emergencies, because the patients who most need assessment are often too agitated, intoxicated or unstable to complete it, and because the test is biased by education, language and culture. However, it is **useful in the stable, cooperative patient** (for example an older person with suspected dementia or delirium, or a patient who is refusing transport and whose capacity is in question) as an objective, documentable baseline measure of cognition, provided its results are interpreted cautiously and supplemented by the full Mental State Examination, a delirium screen such as the CAM or 4AT, collateral history, and a decision-specific capacity assessment. In practice, many ECPs use an abbreviated screen in the field (orientation to time, place and person; registration and recall of three words; attention, for example months of the year backwards) and document the findings in MSE format.

## 40.1 Apply key elements of the prehospital examination during a behavioural emergency, including the Mental State Examination (MSE)

### Overall structure of the prehospital examination

The prehospital examination of a patient with a behavioural emergency follows the standard systematic approach, with the addition of the MSE:

1. **Scene size-up and safety** (see 39.4 and 43.4), including number of patients, mechanism (for example an empty pill container, a ligature, a firearm), need for SAPS and additional resources.
2. **Primary survey (ABCDE)** — behavioural presentations do not exempt the patient from a full primary survey. Look specifically for airway compromise (intoxication, hanging, burns), breathing problems (hypoxia, Kussmaul breathing in DKA or salicylate poisoning), circulation (shock, bleeding from self-inflicted wounds), disability (GCS, pupils, **blood glucose**, seizure activity, lateralising signs), and exposure (temperature, rashes, track marks, injuries, signs of self-harm, signs of assault, signs of restraint injury).
3. **Vital signs**: pulse, blood pressure, respiratory rate, SpO₂, temperature, blood glucose, GCS and, where appropriate, end-tidal CO₂ and ECG (particularly with overdose, stimulants, or before and after sedation).
4. **Focused history** (SAMPLE/AMPLE plus the behavioural history in 39.4) with collateral information.
5. **Secondary survey** looking for occult trauma (particularly head injury), signs of infection, toxidromes, signs of chronic disease, and signs of abuse or neglect.
6. **Mental State Examination.**
7. **Risk assessment** (suicide, violence, abscondment, vulnerability).
8. **Capacity assessment.**

### The Mental State Examination (MSE)

The MSE is a structured description of the patient's psychological functioning **at the time of examination**. It is the psychiatric equivalent of the physical examination: it records what the clinician observes and what the patient reports, using the standard terminology from 39.3 (Boland, Verduin and Ruiz, 2021). The MSE can be remembered with the mnemonic **"ASEPTIC"** or by following the headings below.

| MSE heading | What to observe and record | Prehospital examples |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Appearance** | Apparent age, gender, build, dress (appropriate to weather and situation), grooming and hygiene, signs of self-neglect, injuries, scars from self-harm, track marks, smell of alcohol, distinguishing features | "Unkempt man appearing older than stated age, wearing a winter coat on a hot day, strong smell of alcohol, fresh superficial lacerations to left forearm" |
| **2. Behaviour** | Level of activity (agitated, restless, retarded), eye contact, rapport, cooperation, posture, abnormal movements (tremor, tics, dystonia, catatonic features), response to internal stimuli (looking around as if hearing voices), aggression | "Pacing, unable to sit, poor eye contact, appears to be responding to unseen stimuli, guarded" |
| **3. Speech** | Rate, volume, quantity, tone, fluency, articulation, latency | "Pressured, loud, difficult to interrupt" or "slow, soft, monosyllabic, long latency of response" |
| **4. Mood** (subjective, in the patient's words) | Ask "How are you feeling in yourself?" | "'I feel like there is no point anymore'" |
| **5. Affect** (objective) | Range, reactivity, appropriateness, stability | "Flat, restricted, congruent with low mood" or "labile, tearful then laughing" |
| **6. Thought form** | Flow and organisation: flight of ideas, loosening of association, tangentiality, blocking | "Flight of ideas, clang associations" |
| **7. Thought content** | Delusions, obsessions, preoccupations, overvalued ideas, **suicidal and homicidal ideation (with intent and plan)** | "Persecutory delusion that neighbours are poisoning his food; denies suicidal ideation; expresses thoughts of harming neighbour" |
| **8. Perception** | Hallucinations (type and content, especially command hallucinations), illusions, depersonalisation | "Reports voices telling him to jump in front of a train" |
| **9. Cognition** | Level of consciousness, orientation (time, place, person), attention and concentration, memory (immediate, recent, remote); MMSE/abbreviated test if appropriate | "Alert, oriented to person only, unable to name months backwards; MMSE not possible due to agitation" |
| **10. Insight** | Does the patient recognise that they are unwell and need help? | "No insight — believes nothing is wrong" |
| **11. Judgement** | Ability to make reasonable decisions about their own safety and care | "Poor judgement — plans to walk home along the highway" |

### Prehospital application: putting the examination together

In the prehospital environment, the MSE is often completed **during** the interview and observation rather than as a separate formal test, and is documented using the headings above. The key prehospital questions that the examination must answer are:

1. **Is there an organic cause that requires immediate treatment?** (hypoglycaemia, hypoxia, head injury, sepsis, overdose, withdrawal). If yes, treat it.
2. **What is the immediate risk** to the patient (suicide, self-neglect, physical deterioration) and to others (violence)?
3. **Does the patient have capacity** to accept or refuse care and transport?
4. **What is the appropriate destination?** Patients with possible organic causes or physical injuries require an emergency centre with medical facilities, not a psychiatric facility; the MHCA requires assessment at a health establishment administered under the auspices of the State for involuntary assessments (MHCA, s 40(1)(a)).
5. **What needs to be communicated at handover and documented on the PRF?** The RCEM (2025) recommends that the prehospital history should ideally include observed behaviours, attempts at verbal and environmental de-escalation, assessments of mental capacity, restraint applied (with duration and indication), security or police involvement (including use of force or Tasers), and the sedative strategy with any adverse events.

### High-yield points — Outcome 40 and 40.1

- MMSE = 30 points; ≤23–24 suggests impairment; adjust for education/language.
- Utility: objective, quick, documentable, helps separate organic from functional, supports capacity assessment — **but** needs cooperation, is culturally and educationally biased, does not diagnose delirium, does not assess suicidality or capacity.
- Use the CAM/4AT for delirium; the MoCA is more sensitive for mild impairment.
- MSE headings: Appearance, Behaviour, Speech, Mood, Affect, Thought form, Thought content, Perception, Cognition, Insight, Judgement.
- Always complete a full ABCDE and check glucose before attributing behaviour to a psychiatric cause.
