# Outcome 50. Analyse relevant South African legislation and legal principles affecting the management of mentally ill persons

## The legislative framework

The management of mentally ill persons in South Africa is governed by the following:

1. **The Constitution of the Republic of South Africa, 1996** — the supreme law. Relevant rights include equality and non-discrimination (s 9, which lists disability as a prohibited ground), human dignity (s 10), life (s 11), freedom and security of the person, including the right not to be deprived of freedom arbitrarily or without just cause, the right not to be treated in a cruel, inhuman or degrading way, and the right to bodily and psychological integrity (s 12), privacy (s 14), access to health care services and the right not to be refused emergency medical treatment (s 27), and children's rights (s 28). Any limitation of these rights must be reasonable and justifiable in terms of the limitation clause (s 36), which is why involuntary care must be lawful, necessary and proportionate.
2. **The Mental Health Care Act 17 of 2002 (MHCA)** and its General Regulations (GN R1467 of 2004).
3. **The National Health Act 61 of 2003 (NHA)** — consent, information, participation, confidentiality and the rights of users.
4. **The Children's Act 38 of 2005** — consent by and for children; protection of children.
5. **The Criminal Procedure Act 51 of 1977** (ss 77–79) — assessment of accused persons who may lack the mental capacity to stand trial or who may not be criminally responsible, leading to State patient status under Chapter VI of the MHCA.
6. **The Prevention of and Treatment for Substance Abuse Act 70 of 2008** — voluntary and involuntary admission for substance dependence.
7. **The Older Persons Act 13 of 2006**, the **Domestic Violence Act 116 of 1998** (as amended) and the **Sexual Offences Act 32 of 2007** (as amended) — protection and reporting duties.
8. **The Protection of Personal Information Act 4 of 2013 (POPIA)** — health information is special personal information.
9. **The Inquests Act 58 of 1959** — unnatural deaths, including suicide.
10. **The Health Professions Act 56 of 1974** and the **HPCSA ethical rules and guidelines** (including Booklet 7, HPCSA, 2023), and the **PBEC scope of practice and CDST** (HPCSA PBEC, 2025).
11. **International instruments**: the UN Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities (CRPD) and the UN Principles for the Protection of Persons with Mental Illness (1991), which strongly influenced the MHCA.

## Key legal principles

- **Least restrictive care**: care should be provided in the least restrictive manner and environment.
- **Consent as the norm; involuntary care as the exception**, permitted only on statutory criteria.
- **Capacity is presumed** in adults unless shown otherwise; it is decision-specific.
- **Best interests**, where a person lacks capacity.
- **Review and oversight**: Mental Health Review Boards review involuntary and assisted care decisions, and the High Court exercises judicial review.
- **Non-discrimination and dignity.**
- **Confidentiality**, subject to statutory exceptions.
- **Necessity**: the common-law defence that justifies otherwise unlawful conduct (for example restraining or treating without consent) where it is necessary to avert an imminent danger to life or health, and the means used are reasonable and proportionate.

## The structure of the Mental Health Care Act

The MHCA (in force from 15 December 2004) replaced the Mental Health Act 18 of 1973 and introduced a rights-based, community-oriented system in which mental health care is integrated into general health services. Its main chapters are:

- **Chapter I** — Introduction (definitions, objects).
- **Chapter II** — Fundamental provisions (including the integration of mental health care into general health services).
- **Chapter III** — Rights and duties relating to mental health care users: respect, human dignity and privacy (s 8); consent to care, treatment and rehabilitation services and admission (s 9); unfair discrimination (s 10); exploitation and abuse (s 11); determinations concerning mental health status (s 12); disclosure of information (s 13); limitation on intimate adult relationships (s 14); right to representation (s 15); discharge reports (s 16); knowledge of rights (s 17).
- **Chapter IV** — Mental Health Review Boards.
- **Chapter V** — Voluntary, assisted and involuntary mental health care (ss 25–40).
- **Chapter VI** — State patients.
- **Chapter VII** — Mentally ill prisoners.
- **Chapter VIII** — Care and administration of property of mentally ill persons.
- **Chapters IX–X** — Regulations and general provisions.

### Definitions that matter for EMS

- **Mental health care user (MHCU)**: a person receiving care, treatment and rehabilitation services, or using a health service at a health establishment aimed at enhancing their mental health status (the definition also extends to certain persons acting on the user's behalf).
- **Mental health care practitioner**: a psychiatrist or registered medical practitioner, or a nurse, occupational therapist, psychologist or social worker who has been trained to provide prescribed mental health care, treatment and rehabilitation services. **Emergency care practitioners are not included in this definition**, which is one reason why ECPs cannot perform the s 33 examinations or initiate a formal involuntary admission.
- **Health care provider**: a person providing health care services, which includes EMS practitioners. Health care providers may act under s 9(1)(c) in an emergency and may make a s 33 application if relatives are unwilling, incapable or unavailable.

### The three categories of user in Chapter V

| Category | Who | Key sections |
|---|---|---|
| **Voluntary** | A user who **consents** to care | s 25 — entitled to appropriate care; may be referred to an appropriate establishment |
| **Assisted** | A user who is **incapable of making informed decisions** due to mental illness or severe or profound intellectual disability, and who **does not refuse** care | ss 26–31 — written application by a spouse, next of kin, partner, associate, parent or guardian (or a health care provider); examination by two mental health care practitioners; review by the Review Board |
| **Involuntary** | A user who is **incapable** of making an informed decision **and unwilling (refuses)**, and who is likely to inflict serious harm to self or others, or for whom care is necessary to protect their financial interests or reputation | ss 32–38 — s 33 application; two practitioners; s 34 72-hour assessment; Review Board; judicial review by the High Court (s 36) |

### Sections you must know verbatim or nearly verbatim

**Section 9 — consent and emergency care without consent.** Section 9(1) provides that a health care provider or a health establishment may provide care, treatment and rehabilitation services to, or admit, a mental health care user **only if** (a) the user has consented; (b) it is authorised by a court order or a Review Board; **or (c) due to mental illness, any delay in providing care, treatment and rehabilitation services or admission may result in the (i) death or irreversible harm to the health of the user; (ii) user inflicting serious harm to himself or herself or others; or (iii) user causing serious damage to or loss of property belonging to him or her or others.** Section 9(2) provides that any person or health establishment that provides care or admits a user under s 9(1)(c) (a) must report this fact in writing in the prescribed manner to the relevant Review Board, and (b) may not continue to provide care for longer than **24 hours** unless an application in terms of Chapter V is made within the 24-hour period.

**Section 32 — criteria for involuntary care.** A user must be provided with care without consent at a health establishment on an outpatient or inpatient basis if (a) a written application is made to the head of the health establishment and granted; (b) at the time of the application there is a reasonable belief that the user has a mental illness of such a nature that (i) the user is likely to inflict serious harm to himself or herself or others, or (ii) care is necessary for the protection of the financial interests or reputation of the user; **and** (c) at the time of the application the user is incapable of making an informed decision on the need for the care and is unwilling to receive it.

**Section 33 — application for involuntary care.** The application may only be made by the user's spouse, next of kin, partner, associate, parent or guardian; if the user is under 18, it must be made by the parent or guardian; if those persons are unwilling, incapable or unavailable, it may be made by a **health care provider**. Applicants must have seen the user within **seven days** before the application (s 33(1)(b)). A health care provider applicant must state why they are making the application and what steps were taken to locate relatives (s 33(2)(b)). On receipt, the head of the establishment must cause the user to be examined by **two mental health care practitioners**, neither of whom may be the applicant, and at least one of whom must be qualified to conduct physical examinations (s 33(4)). They submit written findings on whether the s 32 criteria apply (s 33(5)).

**Section 34 — 72-hour assessment.** If the head of the establishment grants the application, the user is admitted for a **72-hour assessment** of physical and mental health status by a medical practitioner and another mental health care practitioner. Within 24 hours after the 72 hours, the findings are made available to the applicant. If involuntary care is not warranted, the user must be discharged immediately unless they consent to care; if further involuntary outpatient care is warranted, the user is discharged subject to conditions and the Review Board is informed; if further involuntary inpatient care is warranted, a written request must be submitted to the Review Board within seven days.

**Section 40 — intervention by SAPS.** Section 40(1): if a member of SAPS has reason to believe, **from personal observation or from information obtained from a mental health care practitioner**, that a person due to mental illness or severe or profound intellectual disability is likely to inflict serious harm to himself or herself or others, the member **must apprehend** the person and cause that person to be (a) taken to an appropriate health establishment administered under the auspices of the State for assessment of the person's mental health status, and (b) handed over into the custody of the head of the establishment or a designated person. Section 40(2): if the mental health care practitioner who assesses the person considers that the person is likely to inflict serious harm, the person must be admitted for a period **not exceeding 24 hours** for an application to be made in terms of s 33; if unlikely to cause harm, the person must be released immediately. Section 40(3): if no application is made within 24 hours, the person must be discharged immediately. Section 40(4)–(7): on request of the head of a health establishment, SAPS must locate, apprehend and return an absconded assisted or involuntary user, or assist with prescribed transfers, and must be informed of the estimated level of dangerousness. Section 40(8): SAPS members may use "such constraining measures as may be necessary and proportionate in the circumstances".

## 50.1 Discuss the implications of the Mental Health Care Act (Act 17 of 2002) on the ECP and EMS systems in South Africa

*(The study guide lists "Act 15 of 2004"; the correct citation is Act 17 of 2002, in force from December 2004.)*

### 1. The ECP may provide emergency care without consent — but within limits

Section 9(1)(c) applies to "a health care provider", which includes EMS practitioners. An ECP may therefore assess, treat (including sedation within scope), and transport a mental health care user without consent where, due to mental illness, any delay may result in death or irreversible harm, serious harm to self or others, or serious damage to property (Stander, Hodkinson and Dippenaar, 2021, confirm that s 9 allows prehospital providers to provide involuntary care during a behavioural emergency). The CDST operationalises this through its "obligated to transport" criteria (HPCSA PBEC, 2025, p. 48). However:

- the power exists only "due to mental illness" — for a patient whose risk arises from intoxication or a medical condition without mental illness, the ECP relies instead on the NHA s 7(1)(e) emergency provision and the common-law doctrine of necessity;
- the power is limited to what is necessary to deal with the emergency, i.e. to get the person safely to a health establishment for assessment;
- s 9(2) requires a written report to the Review Board and limits care without consent to 24 hours unless a Chapter V application is made. In practice the receiving health establishment completes the formal processes, but the ECP's PRF must clearly document the s 9(1)(c) grounds so that the establishment can rely on them.

### 2. The ECP cannot initiate a formal involuntary admission on scene

The s 33 application must be made in writing to the head of a health establishment, normally by a relative, and requires examination by two mental health care practitioners. Evans, Geduld and Stassen (2018) correctly note that "for prehospital providers, this is not achievable at the scene and therefore requires the rapid assessment and transport of these patients to health facilities for evaluation and possible emergency admission on an involuntary basis".

### 3. SAPS holds the primary statutory role for apprehension and transport

Section 40 places the duty to apprehend and convey persons likely to cause serious harm on SAPS. The MHCA "describes such involuntary transport to be a function of the South African Police Services and does not address the role of EMS", yet "in reality… EMS are often the first to arrive at the scene of a suicide attempt and have to manage these patients without police assistance" (Evans, Geduld and Stassen, 2018). Stander, Hodkinson and Dippenaar (2021) note that this places clinical decisions about mental state in the hands of SAPS members who lack medical training, and that the MHCA "fails to consider the responsibilities of prehospital emergency care providers". Implications for EMS systems include:

- the need for **joint EMS–SAPS protocols**, so that SAPS apprehends and EMS provides medical assessment, sedation and monitored transport;
- the need for SAPS to escort, and where necessary travel in, the ambulance;
- the CDST requirement that SAPS be contacted if restraint is considered, and that mechanical restraint be applied only on SAPS instruction (HPCSA PBEC, 2025, p. 46);
- delays and conflict when SAPS are unavailable, reluctant or poorly informed about the MHCA (Stander, Hodkinson and Dippenaar, 2021).

### 4. Destination

Section 40 requires conveyance to "an appropriate health establishment administered under the auspices of the State", and the 72-hour assessment takes place at designated establishments. EMS systems therefore need clear referral pathways to designated public facilities, and must ensure **medical clearance at an emergency centre** before transfer to psychiatric units. EMS is also heavily involved in **interfacility transfers** of assisted and involuntary users between establishments, which must comply with the prescribed transfer provisions (ss 27(10), 33(9), 34(4)(b), 34(6), 39 and 40(4)).

### 5. Rights of the user during EMS care

The ECP must respect the user's dignity and privacy (s 8), must not unfairly discriminate (s 10), must protect the user from exploitation, abuse and degrading treatment (s 11), and must keep information confidential except where disclosure is permitted (s 13). Restraint must be necessary and proportionate.

### 6. Deinstitutionalisation and EMS demand

The MHCA's deinstitutionalisation agenda was not matched by adequate community services, and EMS has become a de facto entry point for mental health care (Tilley et al., 2026). EMS systems must plan for this demand, including training, dedicated response capacity and pre-arranged agreements with facilities and community services (Evans, Geduld and Stassen, 2018, citing WHO, 2009).

### 7. Training and policy gaps

Only 7% of surveyed providers had training in the MHCA (Evans, Geduld and Stassen, 2018), and neither the MHCA nor the HPCSA provided explicit guidance on prehospital involuntary care (Stander, Hodkinson and Dippenaar, 2021). The CDST (HPCSA PBEC, 2025) now gives prehospital direction on sedation, restraint and obligation to transport, but national EMS policies on refusal by mental health care users and on involuntary transport are still recommended, together with curriculum changes (Evans, Geduld and Stassen, 2018).

### 8. Legal risk to the ECP

Acting outside the Act (for example detaining a competent person without grounds) may expose the ECP to claims of unlawful detention or assault; failing to act when the s 9(1)(c) criteria are met may expose the ECP to negligence claims and disciplinary action. Thorough documentation of the risk assessment, capacity assessment and legal grounds is the ECP's best protection.

### High-yield points — Outcome 50

- MHCA 17 of 2002 (not 15 of 2004); in force December 2004.
- s 9(1)(c): emergency care without consent due to mental illness; s 9(2): report to Review Board, 24 hours.
- s 32: involuntary criteria (serious harm OR financial interests/reputation) AND incapable AND unwilling.
- s 33: application by relative (or health care provider); seen within 7 days; two mental health care practitioners.
- s 34: 72-hour assessment.
- s 40: SAPS must apprehend and take to a State facility; 24-hour admission pending s 33; abscondment.
- ECPs are health care providers but **not** mental health care practitioners.

# Outcome 51. Apply the law insofar as it applies to consent to medical treatment on behalf of mentally incompetent patients

## The elements of valid consent

For consent to be valid in South African law, it must be (1) given by a person with **legal capacity** (age) and **mental capacity** (competence) to consent; (2) **informed** — the person must know and understand the nature and extent of the risk and the consequences (the doctrine of informed consent confirmed in *Castell v De Greef* 1994 (4) SA 408 (C), and codified in ss 6 and 7 of the NHA); (3) **voluntary**, free from coercion or undue influence; (4) given **before** the intervention; and (5) **specific** to the intervention. Consent may be express (written or verbal) or implied by conduct.

The NHA requires that the user be informed of their health status, the range of diagnostic procedures and treatment options, the benefits, risks, costs and consequences of each option, and the user's **right to refuse** health services and the implications, risks and obligations of such refusal (NHA, s 6(1)), in a language the user understands and in a manner that takes the user's level of literacy into account (s 6(2)).

## Assessing competence (mental capacity)

A patient is competent to consent or refuse if they can understand the information, retain it, weigh it in reaching a decision, and communicate the decision (see 39.4). Incompetence may be permanent (severe intellectual disability, advanced dementia), temporary (intoxication, delirium, head injury, hypoglycaemia, post-ictal state), or fluctuating (psychosis, mania, delirium). The HPCSA (2023, para 9.1.1.2) requires practitioners who doubt a patient's capacity to consult other relevant health practitioners.

## Who may consent on behalf of an incompetent adult?

### 1. Under the National Health Act (general health care)

Section 7(1) of the NHA provides that a health service may not be provided to a user without the user's informed consent, unless:

- **(a)** the user is unable to give informed consent and such consent is given by a person **mandated by the user in writing** to grant consent on his or her behalf, or **authorised to give such consent in terms of any law or court order**;
- **(b)** the user is unable to give informed consent and no person is mandated or authorised to give such consent, and the consent is given by the **spouse or partner** of the user or, in the absence of such spouse or partner, a **parent, grandparent, an adult child or a brother or a sister** of the user, **in the specific order as listed**;
- **(c)** the provision of a health service without informed consent is authorised in terms of any law or a court order;
- **(d)** failure to treat the user, or group of people which includes the user, will result in a serious risk to public health; or
- **(e)** any delay in the provision of the health service to the user might result in his or her death or irreversible damage to his or her health and the user has not expressly, impliedly or by conduct refused that service.

The HPCSA (2023, para 3.1) encourages patients to appoint, in writing, a person to make decisions when they are no longer able to, and states that this "patient-selected proxy" must be regarded as the substitute for the patient.

### 2. Under the Mental Health Care Act (mental health care)

For mental health care, the MHCA rather than the NHA is the specific statute:

- **Assisted care** (ss 26–27): for a user incapable of making informed decisions who does not refuse care, an application by a spouse, next of kin, partner, associate, parent or guardian (or a health care provider if they are unwilling, incapable or unavailable) authorises care.
- **Involuntary care** (ss 32–34): for a user who is incapable and refuses, as above.
- **Emergency care** (s 9(1)(c)): no substitute consent is required in an emergency due to mental illness.

### 3. Curators

A High Court may appoint a **curator ad personam** (to the person, for decisions about the person's welfare and health care) or a **curator bonis** (for property and finances) for a person who is unable to manage their affairs. Chapter VIII of the MHCA also provides a process for the appointment of an **administrator** to manage the property of a mentally ill person or a person with severe or profound intellectual disability.

### 4. Children

See 48.3: Children's Act s 129 (consent from age 12 with sufficient maturity; parents/guardians for younger or less mature children; superintendent consent in an emergency; Minister or court in specified circumstances).

### 5. Emergency and necessity

In an emergency, the ECP may provide treatment necessary to prevent death or irreversible harm without consent (NHA, s 7(1)(e)), provided the patient has not validly refused. The HPCSA (2023, para 7.1) supports starting treatment that may be of benefit "until a clearer assessment can be made", and requires that the patient be informed afterwards when capable (para 7.2). The common-law defence of necessity provides additional justification.

### 6. Advance directives

An advance refusal of a specific treatment, made by the patient while competent, should be respected (HPCSA, 2023, para 9.2.1), although its legal status is not specifically legislated in South Africa. A directive cannot compel treatment outside acceptable standards (para 9.1.4).

## Applying the law: worked prehospital scenarios

**Scenario 1.** A 70-year-old woman with known dementia has a fractured hip and is confused and refusing to go to hospital. Her husband is present. *Application*: she lacks capacity to make this decision (cannot understand and weigh the information). Under NHA s 7(1)(b), her spouse may consent on her behalf. If no relative were present and the fracture required urgent treatment, s 7(1)(e) and necessity would apply. Use non-drug calming measures and analgesia, involve the husband, document capacity and the source of consent.

**Scenario 2.** A 28-year-old man with known schizophrenia, off medication, is hearing voices telling him to kill himself and refuses transport. *Application*: due to mental illness, delay may result in serious harm to himself, so s 9(1)(c) of the MHCA applies; the CDST obliges transport. Request SAPS (s 40); de-escalate; offer voluntary sedation; if he remains dangerous, use restraint and CDST rapid sedation; transport to a State emergency centre; document the s 9(1)(c) grounds so that the facility can report to the Review Board and start a s 33 application within 24 hours.

**Scenario 3.** A 35-year-old woman took 20 paracetamol tablets four hours ago. She is calm, oriented, understands the risk of liver failure and death, and refuses transport. *Application*: assess capacity rigorously. The overdose itself, the context (suicidal intent), and possible underlying depression strongly suggest that her decision may be driven by mental illness, so her refusal is unlikely to be a capacitous, autonomous decision, and s 9(1)(c) is likely to apply (risk of death due to mental illness). The CDST obliges transport of a patient who "may be a suicide risk". Seek senior or medical advice, involve family, request SAPS if needed. Leaving her on scene with a signed refusal would be indefensible if she later died (Evans, Geduld and Stassen, 2018).

**Scenario 4.** An unconscious 45-year-old man is found hypoglycaemic, with no family present. *Application*: NHA s 7(1)(e) — treat immediately (CDST: dextrose 50% 20 mL IV or glucagon 1 mg IM).

**Scenario 5.** A competent 50-year-old man with a chronic mental illness who is currently stable refuses transport for a minor laceration. *Application*: a diagnosis of mental illness does not remove capacity. If he understands and weighs the information, his refusal must be respected (HPCSA, 2023, para 8.1.2). Document the refusal and advise.

## 51.1 Discuss how EMS systems and the actions of EMS practitioners have the potential to violate the rights of mentally ill persons

EMS systems and practitioners can violate the rights of mentally ill persons in the following ways:

### Violations through excessive or unlawful intervention

1. **Unlawful detention and deprivation of liberty** (Constitution, s 12): restraining or transporting a competent person against their will without lawful grounds.
2. **Assault and violation of bodily integrity** (Constitution, s 12(2)): treating or sedating a competent patient without consent; excessive force; unsafe restraint (head-locks, prone restraint with weight on the back, Tasers), as reported by South African providers (Evans, Geduld and Stassen, 2018).
3. **Cruel, inhuman or degrading treatment** (Constitution, s 12(1)(e); MHCA, s 11): punitive restraint, humiliating treatment, restraint in public view, leaving a patient soiled.
4. **Chemical restraint for convenience** rather than necessity.

### Violations through neglect and discrimination

5. **Refusal or neglect of emergency treatment** (Constitution, s 27(3)): leaving at-risk patients on scene, refusing to transport, or delaying responses to "psych calls" because of stigma; some providers described suicidal patients as "a waste of time" (Evans, Geduld and Stassen, 2018).
6. **Unfair discrimination** (Constitution, s 9; MHCA, s 10): providing a lower standard of care to mental health care users, for example failing to assess for physical illness ("diagnostic overshadowing") and attributing all symptoms to psychiatric illness, contrary to the CDST instruction not to assume a psychiatric cause.
7. **Failure to treat pain** in patients who cannot express it (dementia, intellectual disability).
8. **Accepting invalid refusals** from patients who lack capacity, leading to preventable death — a violation of the right to life (s 11) and to access to health care (s 27).

### Violations of dignity, autonomy and information

9. **Disrespect and stigmatising language** (Constitution, s 10; MHCA, s 8).
10. **Deception** — lying to obtain cooperation (Stander, Hodkinson and Dippenaar, 2021).
11. **Failure to inform the patient** of what is happening and why (NHA, s 6; HPCSA, 2023, para 7.2).
12. **Failure to involve the patient** in decisions to the extent possible.
13. **Breach of privacy and confidentiality** (Constitution, s 14; MHCA, s 13; POPIA): discussing the patient in front of bystanders, sharing information or images on social media, leaving PRFs unsecured.
14. **Failure to use an interpreter**, so that the patient cannot understand or communicate (HPCSA, 2023, para 8.1.7).

### System-level violations

15. **Inadequate training** of practitioners in the MHCA and mental health care (Evans, Geduld and Stassen, 2018).
16. **Lack of protocols** for involuntary transport and refusal (Stander, Hodkinson and Dippenaar, 2021).
17. **Prolonged response times**, long waits at facilities and lack of suitable beds.
18. **Performance management systems** that penalise time spent with mental health care users (Evans, Geduld and Stassen, 2018).
19. **Inadequate staffing**, so that safe de-escalation and restraint are impossible.
20. **Failure to report abuse** of vulnerable mentally ill persons (SOA, s 54; DVA, ss 2A–2B; Older Persons Act).

### Preventing rights violations

Training; adherence to the CDST and MHCA; capacity assessment and documentation; least restrictive practice; respectful communication; confidentiality; audit and complaints mechanisms; joint EMS–SAPS protocols; and advocacy for the patient.

### High-yield points — Outcome 51

- Valid consent: capacity, informed, voluntary, prior, specific.
- NHA s 7(1): (a) written mandate/law/court; (b) spouse/partner → parent → grandparent → adult child → sibling; (c) law/court; (d) public health; (e) emergency.
- MHCA: assisted (ss 26–27), involuntary (ss 32–34), emergency (s 9(1)(c)).
- Rights at risk: liberty, bodily integrity, dignity, equality, privacy, access to emergency care, life.
- Diagnostic overshadowing is a form of discrimination.
