# Outcome 41. Analyse the principles which should be considered in the prehospital care of a patient with a behavioural emergency

The principles below form the framework that you should apply to every behavioural emergency. They are drawn from the CDST "Aggression or agitation" tool (HPCSA PBEC, 2025, pp. 46–48), the RCEM (2025) guideline, South African legislation and the South African prehospital literature (Evans, Geduld and Stassen, 2018; Stander, Hodkinson and Dippenaar, 2021; Tilley et al., 2026).

## Principle 1: Safety of the crew, the patient and bystanders comes first

The CDST instructs the provider to "ensure the safety of all EMS personnel and the patient", to "be vigilant for unpredictable behaviour", and to "contact SAPS for help if the potential risk to the patient, family or staff is significant" (HPCSA PBEC, 2025, p. 46). A crew member who is injured cannot help the patient and becomes a second casualty. Practical measures include staging until SAPS has secured the scene, scanning for weapons, maintaining an exit, staying out of arm's reach, never turning your back, never allowing yourself to be cornered, and removing any "weapon/mechanism for self-harm" (HPCSA PBEC, 2025, p. 46).

## Principle 2: Assume an organic cause until proven otherwise

The CDST states explicitly: "Do not assume aggression/agitation is due to a psychiatric condition", and directs the provider to "look for and manage injury" (head injury) and to "ask about drug misuse and possible overdose" (HPCSA PBEC, 2025, p. 47). Once the patient is contained, a rapid primary survey must be done, including oxygen saturation (give oxygen if SpO₂ <92%), blood pressure (manage shock if BP <90/60), and **blood glucose (treat if <3.5 mmol/L)** (HPCSA PBEC, 2025, p. 47). This principle is reinforced by Desai, Kulkarni and Verma (n.d.), who warn that "if agitation is treated as a purely psychiatric event when it is actually hypoxia, urinary retention, severe pain, sepsis, intracranial bleeding, or medication toxicity, temporary quiet may be purchased at the cost of diagnostic delay".

## Principle 3: Use the least restrictive intervention, in a stepwise manner

Care should escalate in a stepwise way, only moving to a more restrictive step when the less restrictive step has failed or is unsafe:

1. Verbal and environmental de-escalation.
2. Offer of **voluntary oral or buccal/intranasal sedation** (the CDST asks: "Is the patient willing to accept voluntary sedation for the transfer?").
3. Brief physical restraint, only to facilitate sedation.
4. Parenteral rapid sedation (IM or IV).
5. Mechanical restraint, only on the instruction of SAPS.
6. Advanced airway management if sedation causes airway compromise.

The RCEM (2025) states that any interventions "must be using the least restrictive intervention possible and should be applied for the shortest duration possible", and that offering oral sedation "is an important step in establishing that you are using the least restrictive practice in a patient who lacks capacity".

## Principle 4: Respect the patient's dignity, rights and autonomy

Section 10 of the Constitution of the Republic of South Africa (1996) guarantees everyone inherent dignity, section 12 guarantees freedom and security of the person (including bodily and psychological integrity), and section 27(3) states that no one may be refused emergency medical treatment. Section 8 of the MHCA requires that the person, human dignity and privacy of every mental health care user be respected, and that care be provided in a manner that facilitates community care. The ECP must treat the patient with kindness, respect and empathy (HPCSA PBEC, 2025, p. 46), avoid stigmatising language, protect privacy (for example by not discussing the patient in front of bystanders), and keep the patient informed — "explain to the patient what is about to happen" (HPCSA PBEC, 2025, p. 46).

## Principle 5: Assess capacity and act within the law

Every intervention without consent must have a lawful basis. In South Africa, the main lawful bases are the patient's own informed consent; the emergency provision of the MHCA, which allows care without consent where, due to mental illness, any delay may result in death or irreversible harm to the health of the user, the user inflicting serious harm to themselves or others, or serious damage to property (MHCA, s 9(1)(c)); the emergency provision of the NHA, which allows treatment without consent where any delay might result in death or irreversible damage to health and the patient has not expressly, impliedly or by conduct refused (NHA, s 7(1)(e)); and the common-law defence of necessity. The ECP must assess and document capacity (see 39.4 and Outcome 51).

## Principle 6: The obligation to transport

The CDST states that the provider is **obligated to transport** the patient to the nearest or most appropriate emergency centre (with a family member), and to request SAPS instruction for mechanical restraint, if any of the following are present (HPCSA PBEC, 2025, p. 48):

- the patient is a danger to him/herself or may be a suicide risk;
- the patient is a danger to family or community;
- the patient is causing serious damage or destruction to property;
- SAPS are requesting hospitalisation;
- the patient has been sedated and needs monitoring.

These criteria mirror section 9(1)(c) of the MHCA. This principle is critical because Evans, Geduld and Stassen (2018) found that South African providers frequently, and **erroneously**, believed that "patients cannot be transported to hospital without consent under any circumstances", and that "the importance of the patient signing for their refusal of care was commonly reported and many expressed the belief that this documentation absolved them from legal responsibility". Some providers reported returning to find patients dead after being allowed to sign a refusal. A refusal form signed by a patient who lacks capacity or who meets the criteria above has no legal value and does not protect the provider.

## Principle 7: Monitor continuously, especially after sedation or restraint

The CDST requires BP, HR, RR, SpO₂ and GCS to be monitored and recorded **every 10 minutes** after sedation, with immediate reassessment of airway, breathing and circulation if BP <92 systolic, HR >100 or <50, RR >20 or <9, SpO₂ <92% or a drop in GCS (HPCSA PBEC, 2025, p. 47). If the patient is physically or mechanically restrained, the provider must check and document that the pulses are present in each limb (HPCSA PBEC, 2025, p. 47). The RCEM (2025) recommends continuous monitoring including end-tidal CO₂ (capnography) during and after rapid tranquillisation, because respiratory depression and airway obstruction may be detected earlier by capnography than by pulse oximetry, particularly when the patient is receiving oxygen.

## Principle 8: Choose the appropriate destination

Patients with a possible medical or traumatic cause, those who have been sedated, and those who are injured must go to an **emergency centre** with medical capability, not directly to a psychiatric hospital. The MHCA requires that persons apprehended by SAPS be taken to "an appropriate health establishment administered under the auspices of the State" for assessment (MHCA, s 40(1)(a)). Interfacility transfers to specialised psychiatric services are a second common EMS role (Evans, Geduld and Stassen, 2018).

## Principle 9: Accurate, contemporaneous documentation

The CDST requires documentation of the history (mental illness, medical illness, epilepsy, medication, substance misuse or possible poisoning, fever or neck stiffness, trauma — especially head trauma), the examination (responsiveness, seizures, injuries, vital signs), and management (medications, routes, doses and times, response, and procedures performed) (HPCSA PBEC, 2025, p. 47). In addition, the RCEM (2025) recommends recording the features that supported the decision, all de-escalation attempts, capacity assessments, restraint (duration and indication), police involvement and use of force, and the sedative strategy with any adverse events. HPCSA (2023, para 12) requires that records be made "at the time of the events described or soon thereafter", be "legible, clear, accurate and unambiguous", avoid confusing abbreviations, and comply with the Protection of Personal Information Act 4 of 2013.

## Principle 10: Teamwork, communication and staff welfare

Behavioural emergencies require coordination between EMS, SAPS, family, the receiving facility and mental health services. South African providers report difficulties with SAPS members described as reluctant and lacking understanding of the MHCA (Stander, Hodkinson and Dippenaar, 2021), and a lack of support, counselling and debriefing after adverse events such as a patient's suicide (Evans, Geduld and Stassen, 2018). Tilley et al. (2026) highlight the risk of direct and vicarious traumatisation of emergency care providers who attend suicide scenes, and recommend trauma-informed care. Debriefing the patient and the staff after any restrictive intervention is the final domain of de-escalation in the RCEM (2025) model.

## 41.1 Critically appraise the use and ethics of physical and chemical restraints in the context of patients with psychosis in the emergency care setting

### Definitions

- **Physical restraint** is the use of manual force by staff to restrict a person's movement (for example holding the limbs).
- **Mechanical restraint** is the use of a device (for example soft limb restraints, straps, a spine board) to restrict movement. The CDST permits mechanical restraint **only on the instruction of SAPS** (HPCSA PBEC, 2025, p. 46).
- **Chemical restraint (rapid tranquillisation)** is the use of medication with the primary intent of calming a patient who is exhibiting acutely disturbed or severely agitated behaviour (RCEM, 2025). The RCEM prefers the term "rapid tranquillisation", noting that the intent is "to counteract excessive psychomotor stimulation at the earliest opportunity". Uribe and colleagues emphasise that many clinicians identify **"tranquillisation" (calming)** rather than complete sedation as the ideal target, considering complete sedation "as a last resource" (Saucedo Uribe et al., 2025).

### Why patients with psychosis become agitated

Patients with acute psychosis may become agitated or aggressive because of persecutory delusions (for example the belief that the ambulance crew are kidnappers or that medication is poison), command hallucinations, fear, overstimulation, akathisia from antipsychotic medication, intoxication, or because they experience being restrained as an assault, which increases their fear (RCEM, 2025). Evans, Geduld and Stassen (2018) note that patients who have had previous negative experiences of police restraint often react poorly to it.

### The case for restraint (benefits)

1. **Prevention of harm.** Restraint and sedation may be necessary to prevent the patient from seriously injuring themselves (for example running into traffic, jumping from a height or a moving vehicle) or others.
2. **Enabling assessment and life-saving treatment.** Sedation allows a primary survey, glucose measurement, IV access, monitoring and treatment of underlying conditions such as hypoglycaemia, head injury or overdose (HPCSA PBEC, 2025; RCEM, 2025).
3. **Preventing physiological collapse.** In severe ABD, "rapid tranquilisation… is important to prevent further sympathetic over-stimulation and excessive muscular activity from causing a metabolic storm and subsequent cardiovascular collapse", and its use "for more severe ABD presentations is associated with reduced mortality" (RCEM, 2025).
4. **Shortening the duration of physical restraint.** Prolonged physical restraint is itself dangerous; the RCEM (2025) states that "prolonged restraint should prompt consideration of rapid tranquilisation", and the CDST says restraint should be limited "to a brief period, only to facilitate sedation" (HPCSA PBEC, 2025, p. 46).
5. **Safe transport.** An agitated patient in a moving ambulance is a danger to themselves and the crew; the CDST frames sedation in terms of "voluntary sedation for the transfer" (HPCSA PBEC, 2025, p. 46).

### The case against restraint (risks and harms)

**Physical and mechanical restraint**:

- **Death.** Deaths during or shortly after restraint have been reported, particularly in agitated patients with stimulant intoxication or ABD. The RCEM (2025) notes that "continued exertion under restraint can contribute to poor outcomes (likely due to increasing catecholamine levels, worsening hyperthermia, and metabolic acidosis)", and that "there is no high-quality data to suggest that there are risk-free methods to restrain an undifferentiated, potentially co-morbid patient with ABD". Although the theory that prone restraint causes positional asphyxia "has not been supported by recent research", it remains prudent "to ensure that there is no obstruction to ventilation and to minimise the risk of asphyxiation" — **never kneel on the chest, neck or back, and never restrain prone with weight on the torso**.
- Injuries to the patient (fractures, nerve injuries, skin damage, compartment syndrome, rhabdomyolysis, aspiration) and to staff.
- **Psychological trauma**, re-traumatisation (especially in people with histories of abuse), humiliation, loss of trust in health services, and future avoidance of care.
- **Masking of deterioration** — a restrained patient may be falsely assumed to be "calm" when in fact they are exhausted, hypoxic or in pre-arrest.

**Chemical restraint**:

- **Respiratory depression, airway obstruction and apnoea** — "most sedative agents for ABD have been associated with apnoea, airway obstruction, or a requirement for subsequent intubation", and "respiratory adverse events appear to be more common in patients sedated with benzodiazepines than antipsychotics" (RCEM, 2025). Both midazolam and lorazepam cause respiratory depression (Saucedo Uribe et al., 2025).
- **Hypotension**, particularly in volume-depleted patients and older persons.
- **Antipsychotic adverse effects**: acute dystonia (haloperidol caused significantly more dystonia than placebo and aripiprazole), akathisia, QT prolongation and torsade de pointes, neuroleptic malignant syndrome, and lowering of the seizure threshold (Saucedo Uribe et al., 2025; RCEM, 2025). Haloperidol and droperidol are contraindicated in Parkinson's disease and Lewy body dementia (RCEM, 2025).
- **Ketamine adverse effects**: emergence phenomena, hypersalivation, laryngospasm, vomiting, worsening tachycardia and hypertension (synergistic with stimulants), and high rates of intubation in prehospital series, which the RCEM attributes partly to the need to secure safe transport (RCEM, 2025).
- **Paradoxical agitation** with benzodiazepines, particularly in children, older persons and those with intellectual disability.
- **Loss of diagnostic information** — sedation obscures the neurological examination, the MSE and the evolving picture of an overdose or head injury.
- **Interaction with other substances** — alcohol and other CNS depressants potentiate benzodiazepines.

### Evidence for the choice of drug in psychosis-related agitation

The umbrella review by Saucedo Uribe et al. (2025), which included 20 systematic reviews and meta-analyses covering 51,486 participants, reported that:

- loxapine 10 mg (inhaled) was more effective than 5 mg at reducing agitation within 120 minutes;
- aripiprazole was effective and caused less sedation than olanzapine, although haloperidol needed fewer additional doses;
- intramuscular ziprasidone had a faster onset and was better tolerated than haloperidol;
- lorazepam was effective, with fewer side effects than antipsychotics and greater efficacy when combined with them;
- midazolam provided rapid sedation but carried a risk of serious side effects, especially in older adults;
- droperidol was as effective as olanzapine but sedated faster;
- haloperidol plus promethazine reduced aggression effectively with fewer adverse effects. In one trial, haloperidol alone was stopped early because it was "considered too toxic" (ten cases of acute dystonia versus none in the combination group), and the authors concluded that "haloperidol used alone without something to compensate for its frequent and serious adverse effects seems difficult to justify".

The RCEM (2025) recommends **IM ketamine (4 mg/kg) or IM droperidol (5–10 mg)** as first-line agents for severe ABD in UK emergency departments, with midazolam (5–10 mg IM), lorazepam (4 mg IM) or haloperidol (5 mg IM ± lorazepam 2 mg IM) as alternatives. Midazolam may be preferred in patients at risk from sympathomimetic effects (for example ischaemic heart disease or possible cocaine-induced psychosis).

### What the South African CDST directs (prehospital practice)

The CDST "Aggression or agitation" tool (HPCSA PBEC, 2025, pp. 46–47) directs the following:

**If the patient remains aggressive or severely agitated despite verbal de-escalation, the patient "should be sedated". If the patient is willing to accept voluntary sedation (adult):**

- no IV access: \*midazolam 7.5 mg orally, **or** midazolam 10 mg buccal/intranasal, **or** diazepam 10 mg PO, **or** lorazepam 2 mg PO\*;
- IV access: \*midazolam 2 mg IV (may repeat in 15 minutes)\*.

**If the patient refuses sedation and remains dangerous:** restrain physically and prepare to sedate; explain to the patient what is about to happen; remove any weapon or mechanism for self-harm; restrain with **five people (one for each limb and one for the head) and a sixth person to administer medication**; limit restraint to a brief period, only to facilitate sedation; and apply mechanical restraint only on the instruction of SAPS. Then **give rapid sedation**:

- \*midazolam 0.2 mg/kg IM (maximum 15 mg), undiluted; if older than 60 years, give 0.1 mg/kg\*;
- if midazolam is not available: \*lorazepam 4 mg IM\* **or** ^ketamine 4 mg/kg IM (for more rapid onset)^ (ANT/ECP only).

**Review after 15 minutes (IV) or 30 minutes (IM/PO).** If the patient is still aggressive or severely agitated, give a further rapid sedation dose: \*midazolam 0.1 mg/kg IM (maximum 5 mg); if older than 60 years, 0.05 mg/kg\*; if midazolam is not available, lorazepam 2 mg IM.

**Children (<13 years):** use the dose charts. The CDST drug appendix gives midazolam for aggression in children as 0.2 mg/kg buccal/IN (maximum 10 mg per dose), 0.15 mg/kg IM (maximum total dose 10 mg), 0.05 mg/kg IV (maximum total 0.5 mg/kg), or 0.5 mg/kg PO (maximum total 20 mg); lorazepam 0.1 mg/kg (IM, or PO maximum 4 mg); diazepam 0.2 mg/kg PO (maximum 10 mg per dose); and ketamine 4–5 mg/kg IM (ANT/ECP) (HPCSA PBEC, 2025, pp. 69–72).

Note that the CDST does **not** include haloperidol or droperidol for aggression; the prehospital agents in South Africa are benzodiazepines (midazolam, lorazepam, diazepam) and, for ANT/ECP, ketamine. This reflects the prehospital priorities of a rapid onset, availability, the IM route, and the fact that an undifferentiated prehospital patient may have a medical or toxicological cause for which antipsychotics may be harmful (for example anticholinergic toxicity, alcohol withdrawal, or seizures).

### Ethical analysis

The use of restraint involves a conflict between the four principles of biomedical ethics:

- **Autonomy**: restraint overrides the patient's freedom and choices. It can be ethically justified only where the patient **lacks capacity** because of their mental state (for example severe psychosis), or where the risk to others is such that the law permits intervention.
- **Beneficence**: restraint may be the only way to deliver life-saving assessment and treatment and to prevent self-harm.
- **Non-maleficence**: restraint carries significant physical and psychological risks; the harm of restraint must be proportionate to, and less than, the harm it prevents.
- **Justice**: restraint must never be used because of discrimination, prejudice, convenience, punishment, or staff frustration. Persons with mental illness are vulnerable to stigma and to being restrained more readily than others; the MHCA prohibits unfair discrimination (s 10) and requires protection from exploitation, abuse and degrading treatment (s 11); in addition, the MHCA General Regulations (GN R1467 of 2004) regulate the use of mechanical restraint and seclusion of mental health care users in health establishments, so that these may be used only when necessary to prevent harm and must be recorded.

**Ethical conditions for restraint**, which you should state in an examination answer, are therefore that restraint must be:

1. **Necessary** — there is an imminent risk of serious harm.
2. **A last resort** — less restrictive measures have been tried and have failed, or are clearly unsafe.
3. **Proportionate** — the least restrictive effective method, for the shortest possible time.
4. **Lawful** — within the MHCA, NHA, common law and the provider's scope of practice (CDST).
5. **Performed safely** — by enough trained people, with no pressure on the neck, chest or abdomen, with continuous monitoring.
6. **Never punitive** and always respectful of dignity.
7. **Documented** fully, and followed by debriefing.
8. **Reviewed continuously** — removed as soon as it is no longer necessary.

### Critical appraisal — conclusion

Physical and chemical restraint are high-risk interventions with a weak and largely extrapolated evidence base, particularly in the prehospital environment and in older adults (Saucedo Uribe et al., 2025; Desai, Kulkarni and Verma, n.d.). Yet in severe agitation the risks of **not** intervening (injury, ABD-related cardiac arrest, missed hypoglycaemia or head injury, suicide) may be greater than the risks of intervening. Restraint is therefore ethically defensible only when it is necessary, proportionate, lawful, applied for the shortest possible time, and accompanied by continuous monitoring and the capacity to manage complications such as airway compromise. South African evidence shows that prehospital providers lack training in the MHCA and in the management of mental health care users, rely on deception, and receive inadequate support from SAPS and their services (Evans, Geduld and Stassen, 2018; Stander, Hodkinson and Dippenaar, 2021); improved training, clear national protocols such as the CDST, and dedicated response teams are needed to make restraint both safer and more ethical.

## 41.2 Discuss the social, ethical and legal implications of management in the emergency care setting

### Social implications

1. **Stigma.** Mental illness and suicide carry strong social stigma in South Africa. Stigma discourages help-seeking, leads to refusal of treatment, and may lead families to conceal illness (Evans, Geduld and Stassen, 2018). Arrival of an ambulance and SAPS, and visible restraint in front of neighbours, can increase stigma and shame.
2. **Provider attitudes.** Negative attitudes among providers ("waste of time", "attention seeking") are documented in South Africa (Evans, Geduld and Stassen, 2018) and are associated with reduced commitment to transporting patients to definitive care. Tilley et al. (2026) call for further research into "EC provider stigmatisation towards death by suicide and suicidality".
3. **Social determinants.** Poverty, unemployment, violence, substance abuse and the legacy of apartheid contribute to mental illness and suicide, and limit access to care (Tilley et al., 2026). Many patients cannot afford transport to follow-up appointments or to collect medication.
4. **The mental health treatment gap and deinstitutionalisation.** Deinstitutionalisation under the MHCA was not matched by adequate community services, so EMS has become a de facto primary point of access for mental health care (Tilley et al., 2026). Long waiting times and bed shortages at facilities discourage providers from transporting patients (Evans, Geduld and Stassen, 2018).
5. **Cultural and religious beliefs.** Beliefs about causation (for example witchcraft or ancestral displeasure) influence help-seeking, and families may first consult traditional healers or religious leaders. Respectful exploration of these beliefs improves engagement.
6. **Impact on families and communities.** Families may be exhausted, frightened, or themselves victims of violence by the patient. Conversely, they are often the patient's main support system.
7. **Impact on providers.** Behavioural emergencies are physically dangerous and emotionally taxing; providers describe fear, frustration and feeling drained (Stander, Hodkinson and Dippenaar, 2021; Evans, Geduld and Stassen, 2018), and are at risk of PTSD, depression and burnout (Tilley et al., 2026).

### Ethical implications

1. **Autonomy versus beneficence.** The central dilemma: respecting a patient's refusal versus acting to prevent harm. The HPCSA (2023, para 8.1.2) requires that a competent adult's refusal be respected "even where this would result in serious harm to them or in their own death unless the patient's state of mind is such that he/she is incapable of taking or participating in the decision". In behavioural emergencies the patient's state of mind is frequently the very issue in question, so a careful, documented capacity assessment is essential.
2. **Non-maleficence.** Restraint, sedation and involuntary transport all carry the potential for harm and must be proportionate.
3. **Justice and non-discrimination.** Persons with mental illness must receive the same standard of care as other patients. The HPCSA (2023, para 2.5) states that practitioners "must not allow their views about, for example, a patient's age, disability, race, colour, culture, beliefs, sexuality, gender, lifestyle, social or economic status or other irrational grounds to prejudice the choices of treatment offered or the general standard of care provided".
4. **Confidentiality versus duty to warn and report.** Information about mental health is particularly sensitive (MHCA, s 13; POPIA). Disclosure is permitted where required by law (for example the duty to report sexual offences against vulnerable persons under s 54 of the SOA, the duty to report child abuse, or the duty to report domestic violence against children, older persons and persons with disabilities under the DVA) or where necessary to protect others from serious harm.
5. **Truth-telling.** Deceiving patients to secure transport (Stander, Hodkinson and Dippenaar, 2021) violates the principle of respect for persons and damages trust.
6. **Dual loyalty.** Providers may feel caught between the patient's interests and the demands of SAPS, family, or their employer (for example pressure to clear the scene quickly).
7. **The HPCSA ethical tension in suicidal patients** (see 45.1): the HPCSA Booklet 7 is written for end-of-life decisions in terminal illness. Its emphasis on patient autonomy, advance directives and best interests must not be misapplied to a suicidal patient, whose wish to die is usually a symptom of a treatable mental disorder and whose capacity is often impaired.

### Legal implications

1. **Constitutional rights**: dignity (s 10), freedom and security of the person (s 12), access to health care and the right not to be refused emergency medical treatment (s 27), and children's rights (s 28) (Constitution of the Republic of South Africa, 1996).
2. **Mental Health Care Act 17 of 2002** (see Outcome 50): emergency care without consent (s 9(1)(c)), the duty to report to the Review Board and the 24-hour limit (s 9(2)), voluntary, assisted and involuntary care (ss 25–38), and SAPS intervention (s 40).
3. **National Health Act 61 of 2003**: informed consent (s 7), the right to participate in decisions (s 8), the duty to inform the patient (s 6), confidentiality (s 14), and the right to refuse treatment.
4. **Criminal and civil liability**: treating a competent patient without consent may constitute assault, and restraining or transporting a person without lawful authority may constitute unlawful detention (deprivation of liberty) or kidnapping — which is why some providers wrongly believe that they can never transport without consent (Evans, Geduld and Stassen, 2018). Conversely, **leaving a patient who lacks capacity and is at risk** may constitute negligence, and a resulting death could lead to civil claims, criminal charges (culpable homicide) and HPCSA disciplinary action. The legal standard is that of the reasonable practitioner in the same circumstances.
5. **Scope of practice**: drugs and procedures must be within the provider's registered scope (CDST; HPCSA PBEC, 2025).
6. **Documentation as legal evidence**: the PRF is a legal document and may be used in court, in HPCSA inquiries and in Review Board processes.
7. **Mandatory reporting duties** (SOA s 54; Children's Act s 110; DVA ss 2A and 2B; Older Persons Act 13 of 2006).

### High-yield points — Outcome 41

- Ten principles: safety, organic cause first, least restrictive, dignity, capacity and law, obligation to transport, monitoring, destination, documentation, teamwork and welfare.
- CDST adult rapid sedation: **midazolam 0.2 mg/kg IM (max 15 mg; 0.1 mg/kg if >60 years)**; if unavailable lorazepam 4 mg IM or ^ketamine 4 mg/kg IM^; repeat midazolam 0.1 mg/kg IM (max 5 mg) after 30 minutes if needed.
- Voluntary sedation options: midazolam 7.5 mg PO, 10 mg buccal/IN, diazepam 10 mg PO, lorazepam 2 mg PO, midazolam 2 mg IV.
- Restraint: five people plus one, brief, only to facilitate sedation; mechanical restraint only on SAPS instruction; check distal pulses.
- Monitor every 10 minutes after sedation.
- Restraint is ethical only if necessary, a last resort, proportionate, lawful, safe, non-punitive, documented and reviewed.

# Outcome 42. Differentiate key symptoms and management techniques for selected behavioural and psychiatric disorders

This outcome requires you to distinguish the common behavioural presentations from one another and to match each to the appropriate prehospital management. The detailed DSM-5-TR descriptions are in Outcome 52; the table below is a comparative, field-oriented summary for South African prehospital practice.

| Presentation | Key differentiating symptoms and signs | Key prehospital management |
|---|---|---|
| **Panic attack / acute anxiety** | Abrupt surge of intense fear peaking within minutes; palpitations, sweating, trembling, dyspnoea, chest pain, choking sensation, dizziness, paraesthesia (often perioral and carpopedal from hyperventilation), fear of dying or "going crazy"; oriented; normal SpO₂ | **Exclude organic causes first** (ACS, PE, asthma, hypoglycaemia, thyrotoxicosis, arrhythmia, stimulant use) with vital signs, SpO₂, glucose and a 12-lead ECG. Calm reassurance, remove from the stressor, coach slow breathing. **Do not use paper-bag rebreathing** (risk of hypoxia, especially if the cause is actually PE or ACS). Benzodiazepines are rarely needed. Transport if any doubt about the cause. |
| **Depression with suicidal ideation** | Low mood, anhedonia, hopelessness, psychomotor retardation, poor eye contact, sleep and appetite change, guilt, suicidal thoughts | Direct suicide risk assessment (Outcome 45); remove means; never leave the patient alone; transport (MHCA s 9(1)(c) if at risk and refusing); search for and treat any overdose or self-injury. |
| **Mania** | Elevated, expansive or irritable mood; pressured speech; flight of ideas; grandiosity; decreased need for sleep; distractibility; disinhibition, reckless spending or sexual behaviour; poor insight | Low-stimulus environment, firm brief communication, avoid arguing, set limits; exclude stimulant intoxication and thyrotoxicosis; sedation per CDST if dangerous; transport. |
| **Acute psychosis (schizophrenia spectrum)** | Hallucinations (usually auditory), delusions (persecutory, reference, control), disorganised speech and behaviour, often clear consciousness and preserved orientation; poor insight | Safety, de-escalation, do not argue with delusions, ask specifically about **command hallucinations** and violent or suicidal intent; exclude organic causes (glucose, head injury, drugs, infection); CDST sedation if dangerous; transport under MHCA provisions if lacking capacity and at risk. |
| **Delirium** (see 52.9.1) | **Acute onset, fluctuating course, inattention**, disorientation, altered level of consciousness (hyperactive, hypoactive or mixed); visual hallucinations, illusions; often older patients with infection, hypoxia, medication changes, or withdrawal | This is a **medical emergency**. ABCDE, oxygen if SpO₂ <92%, glucose, temperature, look for infection, trauma, toxins; reorientation, family presence, glasses and hearing aids; avoid benzodiazepines unless withdrawal or seizures; use the lowest effective sedative dose (0.1 mg/kg midazolam IM if >60 years in the CDST) only if dangerous; transport to an emergency centre. |
| **Dementia with behavioural disturbance** | Chronic, progressive memory loss with intact consciousness; agitation often triggered by pain, unfamiliar surroundings, constipation, urinary retention, infection | Treat triggers (pain, retention, fear), calm approach, involve a familiar caregiver; consider superimposed delirium; minimal sedation. |
| **Acute stress reaction / situational crisis** | Recent traumatic event, distress, crying, numbness, anger, dissociation, oriented | Psychological first aid: safety, calm, connect with support, practical help, information; screen for injuries and for suicidal ideation; refer. |
| **Alcohol or drug intoxication** | Smell of alcohol, slurred speech, ataxia, nystagmus, toxidrome features (see 62.3), history of use | ABCDE, airway protection, glucose, thiamine 100 mg IV/IM if malnourished or chronic alcohol use (CDST); look for head injury and co-ingestion; treat the toxidrome; do not assume intoxication explains everything. |
| **Alcohol withdrawal / delirium tremens** | 6–24 h after last drink: tremor, sweating, anxiety, tachycardia, hypertension; 12–48 h seizures; 48–96 h delirium tremens with visual and tactile hallucinations, disorientation, hyperthermia | Benzodiazepines (the agents of choice for withdrawal), thiamine, glucose, fluids, cooling, seizure management per the CDST seizure tool. |
| **Conversion (functional neurological) disorder** | Neurological symptoms (paralysis, blindness, seizures, unresponsiveness) inconsistent with known neurological disease, often after stress | Treat as genuine until organic disease (stroke, seizure, hypoglycaemia) has been excluded; do not use painful stimuli to "prove" the patient is pretending; calm reassurance; transport. |
| **Catatonia** (see 52.3) | Stupor, mutism, negativism, posturing, waxy flexibility, staring, echolalia | Medical emergency (risk of dehydration, aspiration, VTE, malignant catatonia); ABCDE, glucose, temperature; transport; benzodiazepines (lorazepam) are the specific treatment in hospital; avoid antipsychotics until NMS is excluded. |
| **Personality disorder crisis** (for example borderline) | Intense, unstable emotions, impulsivity, self-harm, fear of abandonment, anger | Calm, consistent, non-judgemental; set clear limits; assess and treat self-harm; take every threat of suicide seriously. |
| **Acute behavioural disturbance (ABD)** | Extreme agitation, unusual strength, pain insensitivity, hyperthermia, sweating, tachycardia, tachypnoea, sustained non-compliance; usually stimulants or psychosis | **Life-threatening.** Early rapid sedation (CDST), minimise duration of physical struggle and restraint, never compress the chest or neck, cool, high-flow oxygen, IV fluids, continuous monitoring, rapid transport; be prepared for cardiac arrest (RCEM, 2025). |

### High-yield points — Outcome 42

- Delirium versus dementia versus psychosis: **delirium = acute, fluctuating, inattention, altered consciousness**; dementia = chronic, progressive, clear consciousness; psychosis = clear consciousness and usually preserved orientation.
- Panic attack is a diagnosis of exclusion in the field — always do vital signs, a glucose and an ECG.
- Benzodiazepines are the agents of choice for alcohol/sedative withdrawal and stimulant toxicity.

# Outcome 43. Discuss the management of violent patients in the emergency care setting

## 43.1 Describe the assessment of a potentially violent patient

### Predicting violence

The single best predictor of future violence is a **history of past violence**. Other risk factors include: male sex; young age; substance intoxication or withdrawal; acute psychosis (particularly persecutory delusions and command hallucinations); mania; certain personality disorders (antisocial, borderline); organic brain disease (head injury, delirium, dementia, intellectual disability); access to weapons; a history of being a victim of abuse; and situational factors such as being restrained, being in pain, long waits, crowding, or feeling disrespected (Tintinalli et al., 2020; NAEMT, 2021).

### Warning signs of impending violence (escalation)

The ECP must recognise the behavioural cues that violence may be imminent:

- **Posture and movement**: standing rather than sitting, pacing, clenched fists or jaw, tense muscles, rapid movements, invading personal space, kicking or throwing objects, gripping furniture, adopting a fighting stance.
- **Speech**: loud, rapid, threatening, abusive, profane or sarcastic speech, or sudden silence.
- **Facial expression and eyes**: staring, scanning the room, glaring, flushed face, or avoiding eye contact.
- **Intoxication cues**: smell of alcohol, slurred speech, dilated pupils, agitation.
- **Verbal threats**: any threat should be taken seriously.
- **Holding or reaching for a potential weapon.**

### Structured assessment

1. **Dispatch information and scene size-up**: nature of the call, history of violence at the address, presence of weapons, need for SAPS.
2. **Collateral history before approaching**: the CDST advises that the provider "obtain a brief history from the family/friends, if possible, before you approach the patient" (HPCSA PBEC, 2025, p. 46).
3. **Observation from a safe distance** of behaviour, posture, speech and evidence of intoxication or injury.
4. **Agitation severity scoring**. The **Sedation Assessment Tool (SAT)** (Calver, Stokes and Isbister, 2011), included in the RCEM (2025) guideline, scores both responsiveness and speech:

| Score | Responsiveness | Speech |
|---|---|---|
| +3 | Combative, violent, out of control | Continual loud outbursts |
| +2 | Very anxious and agitated | Loud outbursts |
| +1 | Anxious, restless | Normal/talkative |
| 0 | Awake and calm, cooperative | Speaks normally |
| −1 | Asleep but rouses if name is called | Slurring or prominent slowing |
| −2 | Responds to physical stimulation | Few recognisable words |
| −3 | No response to stimulation | None |

The RCEM (2025) suggests that the most potent parenteral strategies be reserved for severe agitation with an SAT score of +2 or +3.

5. **Medical assessment** once safe: primary survey, vital signs including temperature and glucose, pupils, signs of head injury, toxidromes, signs of infection (fever, neck stiffness) (HPCSA PBEC, 2025, p. 47). The Tutorial 1 memo summarises the important differential diagnoses in a violent patient with altered mental status as intoxication (alcohol, cannabis, amphetamines, sedatives), withdrawal (especially alcohol withdrawal), hypoglycaemia, hypoxia, infection (sepsis, meningitis), head injury and acute psychiatric crisis.
6. **Capacity and risk assessment** (to self and others).

## 43.2 Evaluate measures that may be used to try to safely defuse a potentially violent situation

### Verbal de-escalation

Verbal de-escalation is the first-line, least restrictive intervention, and the RCEM (2025) emphasises that it "should always be attempted", even if it "may feel futile", because it is "an important step in ensuring that the use of restraint and rapid tranquilisation are justified" and "is a continuous process". The RCEM (2025) model, adapted from the American Association for Emergency Psychiatry Project BETA consensus (Richmond et al., 2012), has ten domains:

1. **Respect personal space** — identify exits; stay out of arm's reach.
2. **Do not be provocative** — non-confrontational body language; keep hands visible; do not challenge, insult or argue.
3. **Establish verbal contact** — only one staff member should talk to the patient; introduce yourself; explain why you are there; reassure the patient that your aim is to keep them safe.
4. **Be concise** — short sentences; give the patient time to respond; repeat as needed.
5. **Identify wants and feelings** — identify expectations; empathise.
6. **Listen closely** — use clarifying statements.
7. **Agree, or agree to disagree** — consider "fogging" (agree with the truth, agree in principle, or agree with the odds).
8. **Set clear limits** — state limits as matter-of-fact information, not as a threat.
9. **Offer choices and optimism** — offer acts of kindness (for example a blanket, a drink if sedation is not planned), and offer oral sedative medication.
10. **Debrief the patient and staff** — explain why any intervention was necessary, restore the therapeutic relationship, and identify potential improvements.

The CDST (HPCSA PBEC, 2025, p. 46) gives a practical South African summary:

| Try to | Avoid |
|---|---|
| Approach the patient from a safe distance | Turning your back or allowing yourself to be cornered |
| Talk in a quiet, calm area if possible | Making direct eye contact |
| Listen to the patient's concerns | Touching the patient unnecessarily |
| Be calm and reassuring | Arguing or confronting any delusions |
| Show kindness, respect and empathy | Attempting to reason with the patient |
| Set clear boundaries regarding the interaction | Engaging in prolonged conversation |

### Environmental de-escalation

Features of an ideal environment for de-escalation (RCEM, 2025) include a quiet, low-stimulus space that is not too warm; exits located so that staff cannot be trapped; doors that open outwards; the absence of furniture or objects that could be used as weapons or to barricade an exit; no ligature points; constant observability; and an easy way for staff to summon help. In the prehospital setting this translates into: turning off sirens, radios and televisions; moving bystanders away; removing potential weapons; keeping the ambulance door accessible; and positioning so that both the provider and the patient have an exit. If sedation or anaesthesia is likely, food or drink should not be offered (RCEM, 2025).

### Other measures

- **Show of concern, not show of force**: a large number of staff and SAPS members visible together may either deter violence or escalate fear; their presence should be calm and in the background until needed.
- **Involving trusted family or friends** if they calm the patient (RCEM, 2025).
- **Treating reversible discomforts** — pain, hunger, thirst, cold, a full bladder, fear (Desai, Kulkarni and Verma, n.d.).
- **Offering voluntary oral or intranasal sedation** (CDST).
- **Tactical withdrawal**: if the situation becomes unsafe, withdraw to safety, request SAPS assistance, and maintain observation from a safe distance where possible.

### Evaluation

The strength of verbal de-escalation is that it is non-invasive, preserves dignity and the therapeutic relationship, preserves diagnostic information, and can avoid restraint and sedation entirely. Its limitations are that it requires time and training, is less effective in patients who are severely intoxicated, delirious or in ABD (who may be unable to process communication), and may delay necessary treatment in a physiologically unstable patient. In severe ABD, the RCEM (2025) supports early sedation. The evidence base for de-escalation consists mainly of expert consensus rather than randomised trials, but it is universally recommended (Richmond et al., 2012; RCEM, 2025).

## 43.3 Formulate protocols for the use of patient restraint (physical/chemical), taking into account legal and ethical considerations

The protocol below is formulated for South African prehospital practice, based on the CDST (HPCSA PBEC, 2025), the RCEM (2025) guideline and the legal framework.

### Protocol: Restraint and rapid sedation of the violent or severely agitated patient (adult)

**Step 1 — Indications (all must be present):**

- the patient is a danger to themselves or others, or is causing serious damage to property, or urgently needs assessment or treatment that cannot otherwise be delivered;
- verbal and environmental de-escalation have failed or are unsafe;
- voluntary oral, buccal or intranasal sedation has been offered and refused, or is inappropriate;
- the patient lacks capacity to refuse **or** the MHCA s 9(1)(c) / NHA s 7(1)(e) emergency criteria are met.

**Step 2 — Preparation (safety brief):**

- request SAPS assistance (the CDST says that "if restraint is considered, SAPS to be contacted");
- assign roles: **five people for restraint (one per limb and one for the head), and a sixth to administer medication** (HPCSA PBEC, 2025, p. 46); one team leader directs and talks to the patient; ideally one "safety person" monitors the patient's airway and breathing and is not involved in the restraint (RCEM, 2025);
- prepare drugs (correct concentration for IM use), airway equipment (suction, BVM, airway adjuncts, oxygen), monitoring (SpO₂, ECG, BP, ETCO₂) and the glucometer;
- explain to the patient what is about to happen (HPCSA PBEC, 2025, p. 46).

**Step 3 — Physical restraint technique:**

- approach together on a pre-agreed signal;
- control the large joints (shoulders, elbows, hips, knees) rather than the hands and feet;
- place the patient **supine** (or in the lateral position if vomiting is likely), **not prone**; if prone positioning cannot be avoided momentarily, apply **no weight to the back, chest or neck**, and turn the patient supine or lateral as soon as possible;
- never apply pressure to the neck, chest or abdomen; never use choke holds or "head-locks" (Evans, Geduld and Stassen, 2018, reported providers using a head-lock, which is dangerous and inappropriate);
- limit physical restraint to a brief period, only to facilitate sedation (HPCSA PBEC, 2025, p. 46);
- apply mechanical restraint only on SAPS instruction (HPCSA PBEC, 2025, p. 46), using padded devices, never handcuffs applied by health care workers, and never "hog-tying".

**Step 4 — Rapid sedation (CDST):**

- adult: \*midazolam 0.2 mg/kg IM, undiluted (maximum 15 mg); **0.1 mg/kg if older than 60 years**\*;
- if midazolam is unavailable: \*lorazepam 4 mg IM\* **or** ^ketamine 4 mg/kg IM^ (ANT/ECP) for a more rapid onset;
- children <13 years: use the CDST dose charts (for example midazolam 0.15 mg/kg IM, maximum total 10 mg);
- IM is preferred in the actively restrained patient, because attempting IV cannulation during restraint risks needle-stick injury (RCEM, 2025).

**Step 5 — Immediately after sedation:**

- rapid primary survey: airway (manage if the patient cannot maintain the airway, SpO₂ <92% or breathing very slow or gasping), oxygen if SpO₂ <92%, BP (manage shock if <90/60), **glucose (treat if <3.5 mmol/L)** (HPCSA PBEC, 2025, p. 47);
- place the patient in the recovery (lateral) position if the airway is at risk;
- obtain IV access once contained;
- check and document distal pulses in all restrained limbs;
- look for and manage injury (especially head injury) and possible poisoning.

**Step 6 — Reassessment and repeat dosing:**

- review after 15 minutes (IV) or 30 minutes (IM/PO); if still severely agitated, give \*midazolam 0.1 mg/kg IM (maximum 5 mg; 0.05 mg/kg if older than 60 years)\* or lorazepam 2 mg IM (HPCSA PBEC, 2025, p. 47);
- avoid "stacking" doses before the previous dose has had time to act (Desai, Kulkarni and Verma, n.d.);
- if sedation fails after the full doses, request ALS/ECP or medical advice and consider whether the patient needs advanced airway management.

**Step 7 — Monitoring:**

- BP, HR, RR, SpO₂ and GCS every 10 minutes; reassess immediately if BP <92 systolic, HR >100 or <50, RR >20 or <9, SpO₂ <92% or GCS falls (HPCSA PBEC, 2025, p. 47);
- continuous ECG and, where available, ETCO₂ (RCEM, 2025);
- monitor temperature (hyperthermia in ABD).

**Step 8 — Transport:** to the nearest or most appropriate emergency centre, with a family member if possible, with SAPS escort where needed (HPCSA PBEC, 2025, p. 48).

**Step 9 — Documentation (HPCSA PBEC, 2025, p. 47; RCEM, 2025):** indication for restraint and sedation; de-escalation attempts; capacity assessment and the legal basis; number of people, method, start and end times of restraint; SAPS involvement and any use of force or Taser; drugs, doses, routes and times; response and adverse events; pulse checks; vital signs; handover details.

**Step 10 — Debrief** the patient (once calm), the crew and SAPS; report any injury or adverse event through the service's incident reporting system.

### Legal and ethical safeguards built into the protocol

- **Lawful authority**: patient consent, MHCA s 9(1)(c) (followed by the receiving facility's report to the Review Board and the 24-hour limit in s 9(2)), NHA s 7(1)(e), or common-law necessity; SAPS authority under MHCA s 40.
- **Scope of practice**: only CDST/HPCSA-approved drugs within the provider's registration category.
- **Proportionality and least restriction** at every step.
- **Dignity and privacy** (MHCA s 8; Constitution s 10).
- **No punishment or convenience restraint** (MHCA s 11).
- **Contemporaneous documentation** (HPCSA, 2023, para 12).

## 43.4 Describe the safety measures that should be taken when patient violence is anticipated

1. **Before arrival**: obtain as much information as possible from dispatch; request SAPS early; **stage** at a safe distance until SAPS confirms that the scene is safe if there is any report of a weapon, violence or a hostile crowd; plan an approach and an escape route; agree on a code word with your partner to signal danger and the need to withdraw.
2. **On arrival**: park the ambulance facing the exit route, out of the line of sight of the house where possible, and do not block yourself in; scan for hazards (weapons, dogs, crowds, intoxicated bystanders); approach cautiously, standing to the side of doors rather than in front.
3. **Personal protective measures**: wear appropriate PPE (gloves; eye protection, because patients may spit; consider a mask); remove items that could be grabbed or used as a weapon (stethoscope around the neck, ties, lanyards, scissors, pens in breast pockets); tie back long hair.
4. **Positioning**: two arm-lengths away; at an angle to the patient; with a clear exit for both you and the patient; never turn your back; never allow yourself to be cornered (HPCSA PBEC, 2025, p. 46).
5. **Teamwork**: never enter alone; one person talks, the partner observes and maintains radio contact; establish clear roles.
6. **Remove weapons and means of self-harm** (HPCSA PBEC, 2025, p. 46); SAPS should search the patient for weapons before the patient enters the ambulance.
7. **In the ambulance**: the patient should be secured on the stretcher (sedated and/or restrained if required); the crew member in the back should sit nearest the door, with clear access to the exit; remove sharp objects and loose equipment; lock the side and rear doors from the inside where the design allows it, to prevent the patient jumping from a moving vehicle (Evans, Geduld and Stassen, 2018, reported a patient jumping from a moving ambulance); have a SAPS member travel in the back for high-risk patients; keep the driver informed and maintain radio contact with control.
8. **Withdraw if unsafe**: personal safety takes priority; withdraw, call for SAPS and maintain observation from a safe place.
9. **After an incident**: seek medical attention for injuries (including post-exposure prophylaxis for blood-borne virus exposure from bites or spitting), report the incident, document fully, lay a criminal charge if appropriate, and access debriefing and counselling (Evans, Geduld and Stassen, 2018, found such support lacking).

### High-yield points — Outcome 43

- Best predictor of violence: past violence. Watch for escalation cues.
- RCEM/Project BETA ten domains of de-escalation; CDST "Try to / Avoid" list.
- Protocol: indications → preparation → restraint (5 + 1, brief, supine) → sedation (CDST midazolam 0.2 mg/kg IM) → primary survey and glucose → monitor every 10 minutes → transport → document → debrief.
- Mechanical restraint only on SAPS instruction; check distal pulses.
- Stage until SAPS secures the scene; never enter alone; secure the ambulance doors.

# Outcome 44. Critically appraise the role and practice of emergency care providers in the context of violent patients

## The role of the ECP

The ECP's role with a violent patient has several dimensions:

1. **Clinical**: to identify and treat the underlying cause (medical, toxicological, traumatic or psychiatric), to protect the patient's airway and physiology during agitation and after sedation, and to transport the patient to definitive care. The CDST is explicit that the ECP should "not assume aggression/agitation is due to a psychiatric condition" (HPCSA PBEC, 2025, p. 47).
2. **Safety**: to protect the patient, the public and the crew.
3. **Legal**: to act within the MHCA, the NHA and the provider's scope of practice; to understand when transport without consent is permitted and obligatory; and to document appropriately.
4. **Advocacy**: to protect the dignity and rights of a vulnerable person, including against inappropriate use of force by others.
5. **Leadership and clinical supervision**: as the highest-qualified prehospital practitioner, the ECP is frequently called by BLS and ILS crews for sedation and decision support. Stander, Hodkinson and Dippenaar (2021) found that BLS providers viewed themselves as "an extra set of hands", ILS providers felt they could try to defuse situations but could not sedate, and ALS providers felt that their main expected role was sedation and that they were sometimes called "prematurely before sedation was clinically indicated". Evans, Geduld and Stassen (2018) found that providers frequently sought to shift decision-making responsibility to a more senior role player.
6. **Liaison**: with SAPS, family, the receiving facility and mental health services.

## Critical appraisal of current practice in South Africa

### Strengths

- The ECP is uniquely placed as the first health professional on scene, often the only one, and can interrupt suicidality and limit access to methods of harm (Tilley et al., 2026; Evans, Geduld and Stassen, 2018).
- ECPs have advanced skills, including IM, IV and IN sedation, advanced airway management, and the clinical reasoning to recognise organic causes.
- The CDST (HPCSA PBEC, 2025) now provides a clear, national, stepwise algorithm for aggression and agitation, specifying de-escalation, voluntary sedation, restraint, rapid sedation, monitoring, documentation and the obligation to transport.

### Weaknesses and challenges

1. **Inadequate training.** Eighty per cent of surveyed Cape Town providers had no training in managing psychiatric patients, and only 7% had specific training in the MHCA (Evans, Geduld and Stassen, 2018). There was no association between qualification level and training, which means that higher qualifications did not guarantee competence in this area.
2. **Legal confusion.** Many providers wrongly believed that patients could never be transported without consent, regarded involuntary transport as "a form of kidnap", and relied on refusal forms as legal protection (Evans, Geduld and Stassen, 2018). The MHCA assigns involuntary transport to SAPS (s 40) and does not clearly address the role of EMS; the HPCSA and MHCA have provided no explicit guidance on how involuntary care should be provided prehospitally (Stander, Hodkinson and Dippenaar, 2021). The CDST's "obligated to transport" criteria (HPCSA PBEC, 2025, p. 48) go some way to address this.
3. **Negative attitudes and stigma**, with providers describing these patients as a "waste of time" (Evans, Geduld and Stassen, 2018), which risks substandard care.
4. **Use of deception** to obtain cooperation (Stander, Hodkinson and Dippenaar, 2021).
5. **Unsafe restraint practices**, including head-locks and Taser use reported by providers (Evans, Geduld and Stassen, 2018).
6. **Inappropriate tools**, for example the use of the GCS to assess mental capacity (Stander, Hodkinson and Dippenaar, 2021).
7. **Risks of chemical restraint in the field**: limited monitoring in a moving vehicle, limited staff, and no immediate back-up for airway complications; the RCEM (2025) notes that high rates of intubation after ketamine are "predominantly seen in pre-hospital rather than ED settings".
8. **System factors**: long waiting times and bed shortages at facilities, performance pressure (providers feeling "penalised" for time spent), poor dispatch information, difficult relationships with SAPS, and limited community mental health services (Evans, Geduld and Stassen, 2018; Stander, Hodkinson and Dippenaar, 2021; Tilley et al., 2026).
9. **Staff safety and welfare**: 56% of surveyed South African EMS personnel had been assaulted on duty (Evans, Geduld and Stassen, 2018), and support after traumatic incidents is lacking.

### Recommendations

- Mandatory undergraduate and continuing education in the MHCA, capacity assessment, verbal de-escalation, suicide risk assessment and safe restraint (Evans, Geduld and Stassen, 2018).
- National prehospital guidelines on involuntary transport and refusal by mental health care users (now partly addressed by the CDST).
- Dedicated mental health response teams, joint EMS–SAPS training and protocols (Evans, Geduld and Stassen, 2018).
- Trauma-informed care and a syndemic understanding of suicidality (Tilley et al., 2026).
- Structured debriefing and psychological support for staff.
- Audit of restraint and sedation events.

## Conclusion

The ECP plays a pivotal clinical, legal and ethical role in managing violent patients. Current South African practice is undermined by gaps in training and legal knowledge, negative attitudes, unsafe practices and system pressures. Safe and ethical practice requires the ECP to prioritise safety, look for organic causes, use the least restrictive effective intervention, follow the CDST sedation pathway, act within the law, monitor closely, transport when obligated, document thoroughly, and advocate for the dignity of a highly vulnerable patient group.

### High-yield points — Outcome 44

- Roles: clinical, safety, legal, advocacy, leadership, liaison.
- South African evidence: 80% no psychiatric training, 7% MHCA training (Evans, Geduld and Stassen, 2018); deception, use of GCS for capacity, poor SAPS support (Stander, Hodkinson and Dippenaar, 2021).
- Recommendations: training, national guidelines, dedicated teams, trauma-informed care, debriefing.
