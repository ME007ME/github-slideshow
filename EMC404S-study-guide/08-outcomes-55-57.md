# Outcome 55. Discuss the intersections between the ethical, legal and professional obligations of emergency care providers in the management of domestic violence

## Overview of the intersecting obligations

| Domain | Obligations of the ECP |
|---|---|
| **Ethical** | Beneficence (identify and respond to abuse; prevent further harm), non-maleficence (avoid actions that increase the danger to the survivor, for example confronting the abuser or disclosing information to the abuser), respect for autonomy (an adult survivor's right to make their own decisions about leaving, reporting and accepting help), justice (equal, non-judgemental care for all survivors regardless of gender, sexual orientation or circumstances), confidentiality, and truthfulness |
| **Legal** | Treat injuries (Constitution s 27(3)); obtain consent (NHA s 7); maintain confidentiality (NHA s 14; POPIA) subject to statutory duties; **mandatory reporting** of domestic violence against children, older persons and persons with disabilities (DVA ss 2A–2B), of child abuse (Children's Act s 110), of sexual offences against vulnerable persons (SOA s 54), and of abuse of older persons (Older Persons Act s 26); provide accurate documentation that may be used in court (protection order and criminal proceedings) |
| **Professional** | Practise within the HPCSA ethical rules and scope of practice; maintain competence in recognising and responding to domestic violence; document fully and objectively; refer appropriately; collaborate with SAPS, social workers and NGOs; protect personal safety; seek support for the emotional impact |

These obligations intersect and can conflict. For example, respect for an adult survivor's autonomy (the survivor may not want SAPS involved) may conflict with the ECP's wish to protect them; confidentiality may conflict with the statutory duty to report abuse of a child exposed to the violence; and the ethical duty to help may conflict with the ECP's own safety when the perpetrator is present. The general resolution is: **treat injuries, ensure immediate safety, respect the autonomy of a competent adult survivor, inform them of their rights and the available help, report where the law requires it (children, older persons, persons with disabilities, and sexual offences against vulnerable persons), and document thoroughly**.

## 55.1 Analyse the definition of domestic violence in terms of the Domestic Violence Act

### The Act

The **Domestic Violence Act 116 of 1998** (DVA), as substantially amended by the **Domestic Violence Amendment Act 14 of 2021** (in force from **14 April 2023**), aims to afford victims of domestic violence the maximum protection that the law can provide.

### Definition of domestic violence (s 1, as amended)

"Domestic violence" means:

- (a) **physical abuse**;
- (b) **sexual abuse**;
- (c) **emotional, verbal or psychological abuse**;
- (d) **economic abuse**;
- (e) **intimidation**;
- (f) **harassment**;
- (fA) **sexual harassment**;
- (fB) **related person abuse**;
- (g) **spiritual abuse**;
- (h) **damage to property**;
- (hA) **elder abuse**;
- (hB) **coercive behaviour**;
- (hC) **controlling behaviour**;
- (hD) **to expose a child to domestic violence**;
- (i) **entry into the complainant's residence** (where the parties do not share it) or **workplace or place of study** (where they do not share it) **without consent**; or
- (j) **any other behaviour of an intimidating, threatening, abusive, degrading, offensive or humiliating nature** towards a complainant, where such conduct harms, or inspires the reasonable belief that harm may be caused to, the complainant.

### Analysis of key terms

- **Physical abuse**: any act or threatened act of physical violence (hitting, kicking, strangling, burning, use of weapons).
- **Sexual abuse**: any conduct that abuses, humiliates, degrades or otherwise violates the sexual integrity of the complainant, including marital rape.
- **Emotional, verbal or psychological abuse**: "degrading, manipulating, threatening, offensive, intimidating or humiliating conduct towards a complainant that causes mental or psychological harm", including insults, ridicule and name-calling; threats to cause emotional pain; obsessive possessiveness or jealousy that seriously invades privacy, liberty, integrity or security; wilful destruction of property near the complainant; harming or threatening to harm a household pet; disclosing or threatening to disclose the complainant's sexual orientation or private information; threatening the death or injury of another person; and **threats to commit suicide or self-harm** (DVA, s 1).
- **Economic abuse**: the deprivation of economic or financial resources to which the complainant is entitled or which they require out of necessity (including education expenses, household necessities, and rent or bond payments); the disposal of household effects or property without permission; the use of the complainant's financial resources without permission; or coercing the complainant to relinquish control over assets or income or to sign documents enabling another person to manage their finances (DVA, s 1).
- **Elder abuse**: abuse of an older person as contemplated in s 30(2) of the Older Persons Act 13 of 2006, occurring within a domestic relationship.
- **Exposing a child to domestic violence**: intentionally causing a child to see or hear domestic violence, or to experience its effects.
- **Harassment** now explicitly includes **technology-facilitated abuse**: repeated electronic contact, unauthorised access to communications, **monitoring or tracking movements by technology**, and disclosure of private or intimate electronic communications.
- **Coercive and controlling behaviour** recognise that domestic violence is a **pattern of power and control**, not only isolated incidents of physical violence.

### The domestic relationship

The Act applies only where there is a **domestic relationship** between the complainant and the respondent. The domestic relationships covered include persons who are or were married to each other (including customary and religious marriages); persons who live or lived together in a relationship in the nature of marriage; parents of a child, or persons who have or had parental responsibility for a child; family members related by blood, marriage or adoption; persons in an engagement, dating or customary relationship, including an actual or perceived romantic, intimate or sexual relationship of any duration; and persons who share or recently shared the same residence (DVA, s 1). The definition is therefore broad, covering same-sex relationships, dating relationships, and abuse of older parents by adult children.

### Critical analysis of the definition

**Strengths**: the definition is among the most comprehensive in the world; it is **gender-neutral**; it recognises non-physical forms of abuse (emotional, economic, spiritual, coercive and controlling behaviour) that often precede and accompany physical violence; it protects children exposed to violence and older persons; and it addresses technology-facilitated abuse.

**Challenges**: broad definitions require skilled assessment and may be difficult to prove; implementation depends on SAPS, court and health system capacity; survivors may not recognise non-physical abuse as domestic violence; and economic dependence on the abuser, cultural norms and fear limit use of the Act.

## 55.2 Assess the prevalence of domestic violence in South Africa and analyse the social context in which it takes place

### Prevalence

- **Globally**, about one in four (26%) ever-partnered women aged 15 years and older have experienced physical and/or sexual violence by an intimate partner at least once in their lifetime (WHO, 2021).
- In **South Africa**, the **South African Demographic and Health Survey 2016** found that about **21% of ever-partnered women** aged 18 and older reported having ever experienced physical violence by a partner, and about **8%** had experienced it in the 12 months before the survey; about **6%** reported lifetime sexual violence by a partner, and emotional violence was also commonly reported (National Department of Health et al., 2019). These figures are likely to underestimate the true burden because of under-reporting.
- **Intimate partner femicide**: South Africa's female homicide rate is several times the global average, and Abrahams et al. (2013) found that **more than half of women murdered in South Africa in 2009 (about 57%) were killed by an intimate partner** — one woman every eight hours.
- **Violence against children** in the home is widespread: 45% of households with children reported some form of violence against children in 2021–2022 (Statistics South Africa, 2024, cited by Mesthrie, 2025).
- Tilley et al. (2026) cite an estimate that a rape occurs every 12 minutes in South Africa, and associate intimate partner violence with health behaviours and chronic illness among South African women (Gass et al., 2010).
- **Men** also experience domestic violence, and same-sex intimate partner violence occurs, but both are under-reported because of stigma.

### Social context

Domestic violence in South Africa is rooted in multiple, interacting social factors:

1. **Patriarchy and gender norms.** Mesthrie (2025) notes that "the dominating patriarchal nature of society in South Africa has legitimized violence against women and children as a way of holding power and maintaining control". Norms that accept male dominance, male entitlement to sex within relationships, and the use of violence to "discipline" partners and children sustain domestic violence.
2. **The legacy of apartheid and colonialism.** Apartheid "entrenched poverty and inequality in marginalized communities", cultivating "social conditions in which violence could thrive" (Panday et al., cited by Mesthrie, 2025), including the migrant labour system, which disrupted family structures, and the normalisation of state and interpersonal violence.
3. **Poverty, unemployment and inequality.** With 63% of South Africans living in poverty and 31.9% unemployed (Tilley et al., 2026), economic stress fuels conflict, and economic dependence prevents survivors from leaving.
4. **Alcohol and substance abuse** (18.9% of the population, according to Tilley et al., 2026, citing Myers et al., 2022), which is strongly associated with perpetration.
5. **The intergenerational cycle of violence.** "Consistently witnessing violence in the home has been linked with aggressive behaviour later in life and especially rape and domestic violence perpetration" (Mathews and Bonvenuti, cited by Mesthrie, 2025), and growing up in a violent environment leads children to normalise violence as a way of dealing with conflict.
6. **Community violence and the normalisation of violence**, including high rates of murder and assault (Tilley et al., 2026).
7. **Access to firearms.**
8. **Weak institutional responses**: under-resourced SAPS, courts and social services; secondary victimisation; and poor enforcement of protection orders.
9. **Cultural and religious beliefs** that discourage separation or reporting, practices such as ilobolo when misinterpreted as ownership, and stigma attached to divorce.
10. **Overcrowded housing**, which limits privacy and escape.
11. **HIV**, which interacts with intimate partner violence in both directions (violence increases the risk of HIV, and disclosure of HIV status may trigger violence).

These factors interact as a **syndemic**, amplifying each other (Tilley et al., 2026, citing Caulkins, 2022).

## 55.3 Assess the physical, psychological and economic consequences of domestic violence on survivors and their families

### Physical consequences

- **Injuries**: bruises, lacerations, fractures (face, ribs, forearms), burns, stab and gunshot wounds, head injuries and traumatic brain injury, **strangulation injuries** (with risk of hypoxic brain injury, stroke and death), dental injuries, eye injuries, and injuries in various stages of healing.
- **Death**: femicide, homicide of children, and suicide.
- **Sexual and reproductive health**: unintended pregnancy, unsafe abortion, STIs including **HIV**, pelvic inflammatory disease, chronic pelvic pain, sexual dysfunction.
- **Violence in pregnancy**: miscarriage, placental abruption, preterm labour, low birth weight, fetal injury, and maternal death; violence often begins or escalates during pregnancy.
- **Chronic health problems**: chronic pain syndromes, headaches, gastrointestinal disorders (irritable bowel syndrome), cardiovascular disease, and poorer control of chronic illnesses (Gass et al., 2010, cited by Tilley et al., 2026).
- **Health-risk behaviours**: smoking, alcohol and drug use, unsafe sex.

### Psychological consequences

- **Depression**, anxiety disorders, **PTSD**, complex trauma, sleep and eating disorders, substance use disorders, low self-esteem and shame.
- **Suicidal ideation, self-harm and suicide** — domestic violence is a major risk factor.
- Psychological entrapment ("traumatic bonding"), learned helplessness, and fear.

### Effects on children and families

- Children exposed to domestic violence show emotional and behavioural problems, PTSD, anxiety and depression, poor school performance, and developmental problems; they are at increased risk of being directly abused; and they have an increased risk of later victimisation or perpetration (Mesthrie, 2025).
- Exposure to violence can cause changes in brain structure and function, resulting in cognitive deficits and behavioural and emotional difficulties (Mesthrie, 2025).
- Family breakdown, homelessness, and placement of children in alternative care.

### Economic consequences

- **For survivors**: loss of income through absenteeism, job loss and reduced productivity; economic abuse (deprivation of money, control of income, destruction of property); costs of medical care, relocation and legal proceedings; and poverty and homelessness after leaving.
- **For families**: loss of the family's income, education costs for children, and intergenerational poverty.
- **For society**: costs to health services (including EMS), police, courts, social services and shelters, and lost economic productivity. Mesthrie (2025) notes that "the economic and social costs of violence are also significant, and violence can even have intergenerational consequences".

## 55.4 Explain the key protective measures of the Domestic Violence Act, with a view to counselling survivors of domestic violence

The ECP should be able to explain the following protections in plain language to a survivor.

### 1. Duties of SAPS and peace officers

- **SAPS must assist the complainant** at the scene or as soon as reasonably possible: help them to find suitable shelter and obtain medical treatment, provide the prescribed list of shelters and public health establishments, hand over and explain the prescribed **notice of rights** (in the official language of the complainant's choice), and explain the remedies available, including the right to lay a criminal charge (DVA, s 2).
- **Arrest without a warrant**: a peace officer **must**, without a warrant, arrest any respondent at the scene of an incident of domestic violence whom they reasonably believe has committed an act of domestic violence that constitutes an offence containing an element of violence against the complainant (DVA, s 3(2)).
- SAPS may enter a private residence without a warrant to obtain statements where an offence with an element of physical violence is reported (s 3A).
- SAPS must seize arms and dangerous weapons in the possession or control of the respondent in specified circumstances (s 9).

### 2. Protection orders

- **Who may apply**: the complainant, or any other person with a material interest in the complainant's well-being (for example a counsellor, health service provider, social worker or teacher) on the complainant's behalf, with the complainant's written consent — except where the complainant is a minor, mentally incapacitated, unconscious, or unable to give consent. **A child, or anyone on behalf of a child, may apply without the consent or assistance of a parent or guardian** (s 4(4)).
- **Where and when**: at a magistrate's court (including electronically, in terms of the amendments), at any time, including **outside ordinary court hours** if the complainant may suffer harm if the application is not dealt with immediately (s 4(5)).
- **Interim protection order**: the court **must** issue an interim protection order, even without notice to the respondent, if there is prima facie evidence that the respondent is committing or has committed domestic violence and the complainant is suffering or may suffer harm, and an order is immediately necessary to protect the complainant (s 5(2)). It takes effect once served on the respondent (s 5(6)) and calls on the respondent to show cause on a return date (not less than ten days after service) why it should not be made final.
- **Final protection order**: issued after the hearing on the return date.
- **Contents of a protection order** (s 7): the court may prohibit the respondent from committing any act of domestic violence; enlisting the help of another person to commit such acts; entering the shared residence (or a specified part of it), the complainant's residence, workplace or place of study; preventing the complainant from entering or remaining in the shared residence; contacting the complainant (including electronically); and committing any other act specified. The court may order the seizure of weapons, direct SAPS to accompany the complainant to collect personal property, order **emergency monetary relief** (for losses such as medical expenses, loss of earnings, relocation costs and maintenance), refuse or restrict the respondent's contact with children where it is in their best interests, and order that the complainant's physical address not be disclosed.
- **Domestic violence safety monitoring notice** (s 4A): where the parties share a residence and the respondent poses a threat, the court may order SAPS to contact and visit the complainant at regular intervals, and to enter the residence (using reasonable force if necessary) to see the complainant in private.

### 3. Warrant of arrest

When a protection order (interim or final) is issued, the court **must** also authorise a **warrant of arrest** for the respondent, which is **suspended** subject to compliance with the order (s 8). If the respondent breaches the order, the complainant may hand the warrant, together with an affidavit stating that the respondent has breached the order, to any member of SAPS, who must arrest the respondent if there are reasonable grounds to suspect imminent harm, or otherwise issue a written notice to appear in court.

### 4. Offences and penalties

Contravening a protection order is a criminal offence punishable by a fine or imprisonment (the Act prescribes imprisonment of up to five years for breach of a protection order) (DVA, s 17). Failure by an adult to report domestic violence against a child, a person with a disability or an older person (s 2B) is also an offence.

### 5. Reporting and functionary duties (from 14 April 2023)

- **Functionaries** (medical practitioners, health care personnel, social workers, officials in public health establishments, educators and caregivers) who, in the course of their duties, believe or suspect on reasonable grounds that a **child, a person with a disability or an older person** may be a complainant must, without delay, complete the prescribed report (Form 2 — report and risk assessment) setting out the reasons, submit it to a **social worker or SAPS**, conduct a risk assessment, and may refer the complainant for further services (s 2A(2)). A functionary who reports in good faith is not liable to civil, criminal or disciplinary action, and is entitled to confidentiality of identity (s 2A(3)).
- **Any adult** who has knowledge, reasonable belief or suspicion that domestic violence has been committed against a child, a person with a disability or an older person must report it as soon as possible to a social worker or SAPS (s 2B).

*Note on EMS*: "health care personnel" are functionaries only if they belong to a category designated by directive under the Act. Regardless of whether ECPs are designated, every ECP is bound by the s 2B duty as an adult, by SOA s 54, and should regard the s 2A procedure as best practice.

### 6. Other protections

- The court may order that the complainant's address be kept confidential, and proceedings are held in camera.
- The complainant may lay a **criminal charge** (for example assault, assault with intent to do grievous bodily harm, rape, attempted murder) **in addition to** applying for a protection order.
- The integrated electronic repository for protection orders allows SAPS and courts to verify orders nationally.

### Counselling a survivor: what the ECP should say

1. "What is happening to you is not your fault, and it is against the law."
2. "You have the right to be safe. You can apply for a protection order at the magistrate's court at any time, even after hours, and the police must help you."
3. "A protection order can stop the person from hurting, threatening or contacting you, and can order them to stay away from your home and work."
4. "When a protection order is granted, a warrant of arrest is issued at the same time. If the person breaks the order, you can give the warrant to the police and they can arrest them."
5. "You can also lay a criminal charge, such as assault."
6. "The police must give you a list of shelters and help you get medical treatment."
7. "If there are children in the home, they are also affected, and I have a duty to make sure that they are safe."
8. "Here are numbers you can call for help: the GBV Command Centre (0800 428 428), SAPS (10111), and…"
9. Develop a simple **safety plan**: a safe place to go, an emergency bag (identity documents, money, medication, children's documents, keys), a code word with a trusted person, and an emergency number.

The decision to apply for an order, lay a charge or leave belongs to the **competent adult survivor**; leaving is often the most dangerous time, so the survivor's own assessment of safety must be respected.

### High-yield points — Outcome 55

- DVA 116 of 1998, amended by Act 14 of 2021 (in force 14 April 2023).
- Definition: physical, sexual, emotional/verbal/psychological, economic abuse, intimidation, harassment, sexual harassment, related-person abuse, spiritual abuse, damage to property, elder abuse, coercive and controlling behaviour, exposing a child to DV, unauthorised entry, and any other intimidating/abusive conduct.
- SA prevalence: ~21% lifetime physical IPV among ever-partnered women (SADHS 2016); >50% of female homicides by intimate partners (Abrahams et al., 2013).
- Protections: SAPS duties (s 2), mandatory arrest at scene for violent DV offence (s 3), interim and final protection orders (ss 5–7), suspended warrant of arrest (s 8), seizure of weapons (s 9), breach = offence (s 17), functionary report (s 2A, Form 2), adult duty to report for children/disabled/older persons (s 2B).

# Outcome 56. Critically appraise current emergency care practice with reference to domestic violence

## Current practice and its shortcomings

1. **Under-recognition.** Survivors rarely volunteer that injuries are due to domestic violence, and EMS providers focus on physical injuries without asking about the cause. Calls may be dispatched as "assault", "fall" or "medical" rather than domestic violence.
2. **Lack of training and protocols.** There is no CDST tool specific to domestic violence, and South African EMS curricula have historically given limited attention to gender-based violence. Naidoo (2017, cited by Tilley et al., 2026) argued for strengthening the role and scope of prehospital emergency care in gender-based violence through theory, policy and clinical praxis.
3. **Attitudes and secondary victimisation.** Blaming attitudes ("Why doesn't she just leave?"), dismissiveness, and failure to believe survivors deter disclosure.
4. **Poor documentation**, limiting the usefulness of the PRF in protection order or criminal proceedings.
5. **Failure to address children** in the household.
6. **Confusion about reporting obligations** and confidentiality, particularly after the 2021 amendments.
7. **Safety risks to crews** when perpetrators are present.
8. **Fragmented referral pathways** between EMS, SAPS, social services and NGOs.

## The opportunity

EMS providers are often the only health professionals who see the **home environment** — the scene, the dynamics between the people present, the condition of children, signs of substance abuse, weapons, and damage to property — which gives them a unique opportunity to identify domestic violence that would be invisible in a clinic or emergency department.

## 56.1 Assess the need for routine screening for domestic violence by prehospital care providers

### Arguments for routine screening

- **Prevalence is high** in South Africa, and many survivors are not identified.
- **Unique access** to the home environment.
- **Early identification** may allow intervention before serious injury or femicide.
- The **US Preventive Services Task Force** recommends that clinicians screen women of reproductive age for intimate partner violence and refer those who screen positive to ongoing support services (Curry et al., 2018).
- Asking about violence signals that it is an acceptable topic and that help is available, even if the person does not disclose at that time.
- **Mandatory reporting duties** for children, older persons and persons with disabilities require active identification.

### Arguments against universal routine screening

- The **WHO (2013)** clinical guidelines on responding to intimate partner and sexual violence **do not recommend universal screening** of all women attending health care. Instead, they recommend **clinical (selective) inquiry** — asking about exposure to violence when assessing conditions that may be caused or complicated by intimate partner violence — and recommend routine inquiry only in antenatal care where safe conditions exist. This is because there is insufficient evidence that universal screening improves outcomes, and because screening must be accompanied by the ability to respond appropriately.
- **Minimum requirements for safe inquiry** may be absent in the prehospital setting: privacy (the perpetrator may be present), a trained provider, a protocol for response, and referral pathways (WHO, 2013).
- **Risks**: asking in the presence of the perpetrator may escalate danger; time pressure; provider discomfort; and the risk of harm if disclosure is not handled well.
- **Prehospital-specific evidence** for screening is limited.

### Assessment and recommendation

On balance, **universal routine screening of every EMS patient is not currently supported by evidence or feasible**, but **selective, safe clinical inquiry** is strongly recommended whenever there are indicators of domestic violence (see 56.3), together with **routine screening of the environment** for risks to children, older persons and persons with disabilities, in whom the law imposes reporting duties. This approach is consistent with WHO (2013) recommendations and should be supported by training, a protocol, and established referral pathways.

## 56.2 Formulate general principles for the assessment and management of survivors of domestic violence

*(The study guide repeats the wording of 53.2 here; this section applies the general principles specifically to survivors of domestic violence. The principles in 53.2 also apply where domestic violence includes sexual assault.)*

1. **Safety first**: request SAPS if the perpetrator is present or there is ongoing danger; do not confront the perpetrator; separate the survivor from the perpetrator for assessment and questioning ("I need to examine her privately in the ambulance").
2. **Treat injuries** according to the primary and secondary survey; pay particular attention to **strangulation** (airway, voice change, petechiae, neurological signs — transport even if the survivor appears well), head injuries, abdominal injuries in pregnancy, and fractures.
3. **Ask privately and sensitively** (56.3), using an interpreter who is not a family member.
4. **Believe and validate**: "Thank you for telling me. No one deserves to be hurt. It is not your fault."
5. **Respect autonomy**: a competent adult survivor decides whether to report to SAPS, apply for a protection order, go to a shelter or return home. Never force an adult survivor to leave or to report (except where the law requires reporting for children, older persons and persons with disabilities).
6. **Assess immediate risk**: Is it safe to go home? Has the violence increased in frequency or severity? Has there been strangulation, threats to kill, use or access to weapons, threats to the children, or threats of suicide by the perpetrator? Is the survivor pregnant? These are recognised predictors of severe or lethal violence.
7. **Assess children and other dependents** in the household; report concerns (Children's Act s 110; DVA ss 2A–2B; SOA s 54 where a sexual offence is suspected).
8. **Inform the survivor of their rights and the protection order process** (55.4), and provide referral numbers discreetly (the perpetrator may check the survivor's phone or belongings).
9. **Provide psychological first aid** and assess suicide risk.
10. **Preserve evidence** and document meticulously (objective descriptions, body diagrams, the survivor's own words, photographs only according to service policy and with consent).
11. **Transport** to an emergency centre for treatment and forensic documentation (the medical practitioner completes the J88), and refer to social services.
12. **Confidentiality**: do not disclose information to the perpetrator or other family members.
13. **Self-care and debriefing** for the crew.

## 56.3 Apply prehospital domestic violence screening procedures

### Indicators that should prompt inquiry

- Injuries inconsistent with the explanation, delay in seeking care, repeated injuries, injuries at different stages of healing, or injuries in typical locations (head, face, neck, breasts, abdomen, defensive forearm injuries).
- Strangulation marks, bite marks, burns.
- Injuries during pregnancy.
- A partner who answers for the patient, will not leave the patient alone, appears controlling or hostile, or minimises the injuries.
- A patient who appears fearful, evasive, anxious or depressed, or who looks to the partner before answering.
- Frequent EMS calls to the same address; substance abuse in the home.
- Children who are fearful, withdrawn, injured or neglected.
- Damage to property, weapons, or signs of a struggle at the scene.
- Suicide attempts, depression, anxiety or substance use in the patient.

### Procedure

1. **Ensure privacy**: ask questions only when the partner and other family members (including children over about two years old) are not present. Move the patient to the ambulance.
2. **Introduce the topic in a normalising way**: "Because violence at home is very common, I ask many patients about it…"
3. **Ask direct, simple questions**, for example from validated tools such as **HITS** (Sherin et al., 1998) — "How often does your partner physically **H**urt you, **I**nsult you, **T**hreaten you with harm, **S**cream or curse at you?" — or **HARK** (Sohal, Eldridge and Feder, 2007) — "Within the last year, have you been **H**umiliated or emotionally abused by your partner? Have you been **A**fraid of your partner? Have you been **R**aped or forced into any kind of sexual activity by your partner? Have you been **K**icked, hit, slapped or otherwise physically hurt by your partner?"
4. Other useful questions:
   - "Did someone do this to you?"
   - "Do you feel safe at home?"
   - "Has anyone ever tried to strangle or choke you?"
   - "Are you afraid of anyone at home?"
   - "Has anyone threatened to kill you or your children?"
   - "Are there weapons in the house?"
   - "Are the children safe?"
5. **If the patient discloses**: listen, believe, validate; assess immediate safety; offer SAPS involvement, transport and referral; explain rights; document.
6. **If the patient does not disclose** but indicators are present: respect the response, document the indicators objectively, provide information discreetly ("If you ever need help, these are numbers you can call"), and inform the receiving facility.
7. **Report** where required by law (children, older persons, persons with disabilities; sexual offences against vulnerable persons).

### High-yield points — Outcome 56

- WHO (2013): no universal screening; selective clinical inquiry when there are indicators; minimum conditions — privacy, training, protocol, referral.
- USPSTF (2018): screen women of reproductive age.
- Recommended prehospital approach: selective, private inquiry + environmental screening for children/older persons/disabled.
- Screening tools: HITS, HARK.
- Strangulation = high lethality risk; always transport.

# Outcome 57. Discuss the management of domestic violence incidents with reference to the role of emergency care providers and law enforcement

## Roles in a domestic violence incident

### Emergency care providers

- Respond safely: stage until SAPS secures the scene if there is any indication of ongoing violence or weapons; approach with caution (domestic violence scenes are among the most dangerous for responders, because emotions are high, weapons are often present, and both parties may turn on the responders).
- Treat injuries and transport; recognise strangulation, head injury and injuries in pregnancy.
- Identify domestic violence through observation and inquiry, including risks to children, older persons and persons with disabilities.
- Provide trauma-informed care, psychological first aid and information about rights.
- Preserve evidence and document objectively.
- Report where required by law (DVA ss 2A–2B; Children's Act s 110; SOA s 54; Older Persons Act s 26) and inform SAPS of concerns.
- Refer to social services and NGOs; hand over to the emergency centre.
- **Do not**: act as investigators, take sides, mediate the dispute, confront the perpetrator, or promise outcomes.

### Law enforcement (SAPS)

- Secure the scene and ensure the safety of everyone, including EMS crews.
- Assist the complainant: shelter, medical treatment, the notice of rights, explanation of remedies (DVA s 2).
- **Arrest the respondent without a warrant** where there are reasonable grounds to believe that an act of domestic violence constituting an offence with an element of violence has been committed (DVA s 3(2)).
- Seize weapons (s 9); enter premises for evidence (s 3A); execute warrants of arrest for breach of protection orders (s 8); conduct safety monitoring visits (s 4A).
- Take statements, open a case docket and investigate criminal charges (assault, rape, attempted murder).
- Remove children in need of care and protection to temporary safe care (Children's Act s 152).
- Apprehend persons who are a danger due to mental illness (MHCA s 40).

### The intersection

Effective management depends on EMS and SAPS working together: SAPS provides scene safety and legal action, EMS provides medical care and documentation, and both refer to social services. Problems arise when SAPS is delayed or does not respond, when EMS is expected to manage a violent scene alone, or when SAPS members lack training in domestic violence or the MHCA (Stander, Hodkinson and Dippenaar, 2021). Joint protocols, joint training and clear communication on scene improve outcomes.

### Scene management sequence

1. Dispatch information → request SAPS → stage if unsafe.
2. SAPS secures the scene; separate the parties.
3. EMS assesses and treats all injured persons (the alleged perpetrator may also be injured and is also entitled to care — but from a separate crew where possible).
4. Private inquiry and risk assessment; check on children.
5. SAPS takes statements, arrests where indicated, and provides the notice of rights.
6. EMS transports the injured survivor to the emergency centre; evidence handed to SAPS with documentation.
7. Reports submitted (Form 2 to a social worker or SAPS where applicable).
8. Referral to social services, shelters and NGOs.
9. Debrief.

## 57.1 Identify key agencies for referral of victims of domestic violence

- **SAPS** — emergency number **10111**; any police station; **Family Violence, Child Protection and Sexual Offences (FCS) Units**.
- **Magistrates' courts** — for protection orders (including online and after-hours applications).
- **Gender-Based Violence Command Centre** (Department of Social Development) — **0800 428 428**; "please call me" USSD **\*120\*7867#**.
- **Department of Social Development** — social workers (who are designated recipients of DVA s 2A and 2B reports).
- **Thuthuzela Care Centres** — where domestic violence includes sexual violence.
- **Childline South Africa** — **116**.
- **Shelters for abused women and children** (for example those coordinated by the National Shelter Movement of South Africa).
- **Lifeline South Africa** and **POWA (People Opposing Women Abuse)** — counselling and legal support.
- **SADAG** — mental health and suicide crisis support.
- **Legal Aid South Africa** — free legal assistance.
- **Designated child protection organisations** and provincial departments of social development (Children's Act).
- **Department of Social Development and older persons' organisations** — for elder abuse (Older Persons Act).

*(Verify current helpline numbers on the official agency websites before relying on them in practice.)*

### High-yield points — Outcome 57

- EMS: safety, treatment, identification, evidence, documentation, reporting, referral — not investigation or mediation.
- SAPS: scene safety, assistance and notice of rights (s 2), mandatory arrest for violent DV offences (s 3), weapons (s 9), warrants (s 8), safety monitoring (s 4A).
- Referral: SAPS 10111, GBV Command Centre 0800 428 428, Childline 116, magistrates' courts, social workers, shelters, TCCs, Legal Aid.
