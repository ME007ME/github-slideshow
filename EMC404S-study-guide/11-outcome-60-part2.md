## 60.17 Salicylate poisoning

**Sources**: aspirin (acetylsalicylic acid) tablets and powders, combination analgesics, **methyl salicylate (oil of wintergreen)** in liniments and muscle rubs (extremely concentrated — 5 mL of 98% methyl salicylate contains about 7 g of salicylate), and bismuth subsalicylate.

**Toxic doses**: acute ingestions of more than about 150 mg/kg are potentially toxic, more than about 300 mg/kg cause serious toxicity, and more than about 500 mg/kg are potentially lethal (Tintinalli et al., 2020). **Chronic toxicity** (therapeutic overuse in older people) is often more dangerous than the levels suggest and is frequently misdiagnosed as sepsis, delirium or dementia.

**Pathophysiology**:

1. **Direct stimulation of the respiratory centre** in the medulla → hyperventilation → **respiratory alkalosis** (the early finding in adults).
2. **Uncoupling of oxidative phosphorylation** in mitochondria → increased oxygen consumption, heat production (**hyperthermia**), increased CO₂ production, and anaerobic metabolism → **high anion gap metabolic acidosis** (lactate, ketones, salicylic acid).
3. Interference with the Krebs cycle and carbohydrate metabolism → hypo- or hyperglycaemia; **neuroglycopenia** (low CNS glucose) even with normal blood glucose.
4. **Acidaemia increases toxicity**: in an acidic environment, more salicylate exists in its non-ionised, lipid-soluble form, which crosses the blood–brain barrier — CNS salicylate toxicity is the main cause of death. This is why preventing acidaemia (and avoiding hypoventilation) is critical, and why alkalinisation helps.
5. Increased capillary permeability → **non-cardiogenic pulmonary oedema** and cerebral oedema.
6. Fluid losses (vomiting, sweating, hyperventilation) → dehydration; hypokalaemia.
7. Platelet dysfunction and hypoprothrombinaemia (bleeding).

The classic acid–base picture in adults is a **mixed respiratory alkalosis and high anion gap metabolic acidosis**; young children may present with metabolic acidosis alone (Hüser et al., 2025).

**Clinical presentation**: nausea, vomiting, **tinnitus** and deafness (early and characteristic), tachypnoea and hyperpnoea (deep, rapid breathing), sweating, **hyperthermia**, tachycardia, dehydration, agitation, confusion, delirium, hallucinations, seizures, coma; pulmonary oedema; hypoglycaemia; and, in severe cases, cardiovascular collapse.

**Children**: more prone to rapid metabolic acidosis, hypoglycaemia and CNS toxicity; methyl salicylate ingestion can be fatal in small amounts.

**Prehospital management**:

- ABCDE; oxygen; **glucose** (treat hypoglycaemia — and remember neuroglycopenia).
- IV fluids for dehydration.
- Activated charcoal **if fully conscious and within one hour** (salicylates are adsorbed; Hüser et al., 2025, note that a longer window may apply to large or enteric-coated ingestions and pharmacobezoars).
- **If intubation is required**, it must be done by experienced providers and the patient must be **hyperventilated** to match the pre-intubation minute ventilation — a sudden rise in CO₂ after intubation causes acidaemia, shifts salicylate into the brain and can cause cardiac arrest (Hüser et al., 2025: "Avoid hypoventilation if intubation is necessary").
- Cooling for hyperthermia; benzodiazepines for seizures.
- Transport; Poisons Information Helpline.

**Hospital**:

- Serial salicylate levels (every two to four hours until falling), blood gas, electrolytes, glucose.
- **Urinary alkalinisation with sodium bicarbonate** — the aim is a urine pH ≥7.5 while avoiding a blood pH >7.5; potassium replacement is essential, because hypokalaemia prevents urinary alkalinisation (Hüser et al., 2025).
- **Haemodialysis** for severe poisoning — for example very high salicylate concentrations, altered mental status, new hypoxaemia requiring oxygen, or acidaemia (pH ≤7.20) despite treatment (EXTRIP recommendations; Juurlink et al., 2015).
- Glucose supplementation even with normal blood glucose in patients with altered mental status.

## 60.18 Tricyclic poisoning

**Agents**: tricyclic antidepressants (TCAs) such as **amitriptyline** (commonly prescribed in South Africa for depression, neuropathic pain, migraine and insomnia), imipramine, clomipramine, nortriptyline and dothiepin. TCAs are among the most lethal medications in overdose; toxicity is expected with ingestions of about 10 mg/kg, and more than about 15–20 mg/kg may be life-threatening.

**Pathophysiology** — TCAs have multiple receptor and channel effects:

1. **Fast sodium channel blockade** (membrane-stabilising, quinidine-like effect) in the myocardium → slowed depolarisation → **QRS widening**, a **terminal R wave in aVR**, right-axis deviation of the terminal QRS, ventricular arrhythmias (wide-complex tachycardia, VT, VF), negative inotropy and hypotension. This is the main cause of death.
2. **Anticholinergic (antimuscarinic) effects** → tachycardia, dilated pupils, dry skin, urinary retention, ileus (delayed absorption), hyperthermia, delirium.
3. **Alpha-1 adrenergic blockade** → vasodilatation and hypotension.
4. **Inhibition of noradrenaline and serotonin reuptake** → early tachycardia and hypertension, then catecholamine depletion.
5. **GABA-A antagonism** and other CNS effects → **seizures**, coma.
6. **Potassium channel blockade** → QT prolongation.

**Clinical presentation**: rapid deterioration, often within one to two hours of ingestion — a patient who is talking on scene can become comatose, fit and arrest within minutes. Features include anticholinergic signs (tachycardia, mydriasis, dry flushed skin, urinary retention, decreased bowel sounds, agitation, delirium), **decreased level of consciousness and coma**, **seizures**, **hypotension**, and **ECG changes** — sinus tachycardia, **QRS >100 ms** (associated with seizures), **QRS >160 ms** (associated with ventricular arrhythmias), a terminal R wave >3 mm in aVR, and QT prolongation (Hüser et al., 2025).

**Children**: a few tablets can be fatal; seizures and arrhythmias may be the first signs.

**Prehospital management (CDST, HPCSA PBEC, 2025, p. 56)**:

- ABCDE; oxygen; IV access; **attach the ECG immediately** and repeat 12-lead ECGs to measure the QRS.
- **Sodium bicarbonate (ANT/ECP)**: if signs of toxicity — **QRS widened, ventricular arrhythmia, seizures, or hypotension not responsive to fluids** — give ^sodium bicarbonate 8.4% 100 mL IV over 3 min^ (child: ^2 mL/kg^); reassess after 10 minutes; repeat if toxicity persists; then consult a senior medical advisor or the Poisons Information Helpline.
  - **Mechanism**: the sodium load overcomes sodium channel blockade, and **alkalinisation** (target blood pH about 7.45–7.55) reduces TCA binding to sodium channels (Hüser et al., 2025).
- **If intubated**: ^hyperventilate with 100% oxygen at 20–24 breaths per minute^, aiming for an ETCO₂ of 20–25 mmHg (respiratory alkalosis) (HPCSA PBEC, 2025, p. 56).
- **Hypotension**: IV crystalloid; bicarbonate; vasopressors (adrenaline infusion within scope).
- **Seizures**: benzodiazepines (CDST Seizures tool); seizures cause acidosis, which worsens cardiotoxicity.
- **Avoid**: flumazenil, physostigmine (contraindicated in TCA overdose with widened QRS — Hüser et al., 2025), class IA and IC antiarrhythmics (procainamide, flecainide), and activated charcoal in a patient whose consciousness may decline.
- Transport urgently with continuous ECG monitoring; pre-alert.

**Hospital**: further bicarbonate to keep pH 7.45–7.55, with monitoring for hypernatraemia and hypokalaemia; hypertonic saline or lidocaine for refractory arrhythmias (on expert advice); lipid emulsion and VA-ECMO as rescue therapies (Hüser et al., 2025); ICU monitoring for at least 12–24 hours.

## 60.19 Ethylene glycol

**Sources**: antifreeze and coolant for car radiators, de-icing solutions, brake fluid, some solvents and paints. It is sweet-tasting, which increases the risk of ingestion by children and animals. It is closely related to the other **toxic alcohols** — **methanol** (methylated spirits, illicit or contaminated home-brewed alcohol, windscreen washer fluid) and diethylene glycol (a contaminant in medicines).

**Pathophysiology**: ethylene glycol itself is relatively non-toxic (causing inebriation like ethanol), but it is metabolised by **alcohol dehydrogenase** to glycoaldehyde, then to **glycolic acid** (the main cause of the **high anion gap metabolic acidosis**), glyoxylic acid and **oxalic acid**, which combines with calcium to form **calcium oxalate crystals** that deposit in the renal tubules (**acute kidney injury**), brain and other tissues, causing **hypocalcaemia** (Kraut and Mullins, 2018). Methanol is metabolised to formaldehyde and **formic acid**, which causes metabolic acidosis and **retinal and optic nerve toxicity** (blindness) and basal ganglia damage. An early **raised osmolal gap** (from the parent alcohol) is followed by a raised anion gap as the toxic metabolites accumulate.

**Clinical presentation** — classically in three overlapping stages:

1. **Neurological stage (30 minutes to 12 hours)**: apparent drunkenness without the smell of ethanol, nausea, vomiting, ataxia, slurred speech, confusion, seizures, coma.
2. **Cardiopulmonary stage (12–24 hours)**: tachycardia, hypertension or hypotension, tachypnoea and **Kussmaul breathing** (compensation for metabolic acidosis), heart failure, pulmonary oedema, shock.
3. **Renal stage (24–72 hours)**: flank pain, oliguria, haematuria, crystalluria, **acute kidney injury**; hypocalcaemia (tetany, QT prolongation, seizures).

Methanol causes headache, abdominal pain, metabolic acidosis, and **visual disturbances** ("snowfield" vision, blurred vision, blindness) after a latent period of 12–24 hours (longer if ethanol was co-ingested).

**Children**: accidental ingestion of sweet antifreeze; a small volume can be dangerous.

**Prehospital management**:

- ABCDE; airway protection; oxygen; glucose; seizures per CDST; IV fluids.
- **Do not give activated charcoal** — alcohols are not adsorbed (HPCSA PBEC, 2025, p. 54 lists alcohol among exclusions).
- Recognise Kussmaul breathing as a sign of metabolic acidosis; if intubation is needed, maintain a high minute ventilation.
- Take the container; contact the Poisons Information Helpline; transport (delayed toxicity — the patient may appear well initially).

**Hospital (Kraut and Mullins, 2018; Kaswa, 2024)**:

- **Alcohol dehydrogenase inhibition**: **fomepizole** (preferred where available) or **ethanol** (which competes for alcohol dehydrogenase — Kaswa, 2024, lists ethyl alcohol as the antidote for methanol), to prevent the formation of toxic metabolites.
- **Haemodialysis** to remove the parent alcohol and toxic acids and to correct acidosis and renal failure — indicated in severe metabolic acidosis, renal failure, visual impairment (methanol) or high levels (Kaswa, 2024; Hüser et al., 2025).
- Sodium bicarbonate for acidosis.
- Cofactors: **thiamine and pyridoxine** (ethylene glycol), **folinic acid/folate** (methanol).
- Calcium only for symptomatic hypocalcaemia or seizures.

## 60.20 Colchicine

**Sources**: colchicine tablets (used for gout and familial Mediterranean fever, and pericarditis) and colchicine-containing plants — notably the **flame lily (*Gloriosa superba*)**, which is indigenous to southern Africa and has been implicated in poisonings (including through traditional medicine use), and the autumn crocus (*Colchicum autumnale*).

**Pathophysiology**: colchicine binds tubulin and **inhibits microtubule polymerisation**, arresting cell division in metaphase. Rapidly dividing cells are affected first (gastrointestinal mucosa, bone marrow, hair follicles), followed by multi-organ dysfunction. It has a narrow therapeutic index; ingestions above about 0.5 mg/kg are associated with severe toxicity and above about 0.8 mg/kg are often fatal (Finkelstein et al., 2010).

**Clinical presentation** — three phases (Finkelstein et al., 2010):

1. **Gastrointestinal phase (0–24 hours)**: profuse vomiting, diarrhoea (which may be bloody) and abdominal pain, leading to dehydration and hypovolaemia; leucocytosis.
2. **Multi-organ dysfunction phase (24 hours to 7 days)**: **bone marrow suppression** (pancytopenia — infection and bleeding), cardiogenic and distributive shock, arrhythmias and sudden cardiac arrest, ARDS, acute kidney injury, liver injury, rhabdomyolysis, DIC, metabolic acidosis, electrolyte disturbances, seizures, coma and death.
3. **Recovery phase (after about seven days)**: rebound leucocytosis and **alopecia** (hair loss).

Early severe GI symptoms after a colchicine or plant ingestion should be taken very seriously, because the patient may deteriorate days later.

**Children**: small numbers of tablets can be dangerous; ingestion of flame lily tubers.

**Prehospital management**: ABCDE; IV fluids for dehydration and shock; glucose; activated charcoal if fully conscious within one hour (colchicine is adsorbed); contact the Poisons Information Helpline; **transport all colchicine ingestions**, even if asymptomatic.

**Hospital**: there is **no specific antidote** in routine use; very early gastric decontamination (Hüser et al., 2025, list high-dose colchicine among the exceptional cases in which gastric lavage may be considered very early, and early activated charcoal is recommended); MDAC (enterohepatic recirculation); intensive supportive care (fluids, vasopressors, ventilation, granulocyte colony-stimulating factor for bone marrow suppression, ECMO for refractory shock).

## 60.21 Paracetamol

**Background**: paracetamol (acetaminophen) is the single most common medicine involved in poisoning exposures reported to the Tygerberg Poison Information Centre (15.8%) (Veale, Wium and Müller, 2013), and is a leading cause of acute liver failure worldwide. It is widely available over the counter, alone and in combination products (for example with codeine).

**Toxic dose**: a single acute ingestion of **more than about 150 mg/kg** (or 7.5–10 g in an adult) is potentially hepatotoxic (Hüser et al., 2025); risk is increased in chronic alcohol use, malnutrition, chronic liver disease and enzyme-inducing drugs (some anti-TB drugs and anticonvulsants). Staggered ingestions and repeated supratherapeutic ingestions are also dangerous.

**Pathophysiology**: at therapeutic doses, about 90% of paracetamol is conjugated (glucuronidation and sulphation) to non-toxic metabolites, and about 5–10% is oxidised by cytochrome P450 (mainly CYP2E1) to the highly reactive metabolite **NAPQI** (N-acetyl-p-benzoquinone imine), which is immediately detoxified by conjugation with **glutathione**. In overdose, the conjugation pathways become saturated, more NAPQI is produced, **glutathione stores are depleted** (below about 30% of normal), and free NAPQI binds to hepatocyte proteins, causing **centrilobular (zone 3) hepatic necrosis**. Renal tubular necrosis can also occur. **N-acetylcysteine (NAC)** works by replenishing glutathione (acting as a glutathione precursor), and is most effective if started within eight hours of ingestion (Tintinalli et al., 2020).

**Clinical presentation** — four stages:

1. **Stage 1 (0–24 hours)**: often **asymptomatic**, or mild nausea, vomiting, malaise and sweating; this is why paracetamol overdose is easily underestimated.
2. **Stage 2 (24–72 hours)**: right upper quadrant pain and tenderness; rising transaminases (ALT/AST), prolonged INR; nausea and vomiting may improve.
3. **Stage 3 (72–96 hours)**: **fulminant hepatic failure** — jaundice, coagulopathy and bleeding, hypoglycaemia, encephalopathy, lactic acidosis, renal failure, and death.
4. **Stage 4 (4 days to 2 weeks)**: recovery (with complete hepatic regeneration in survivors) or death.

**Children**: young children are relatively less susceptible after single accidental ingestions (smaller amounts, greater sulphation capacity), but toxicity occurs with large ingestions and with repeated supratherapeutic dosing (for example caregivers giving too much syrup or combining products).

**Prehospital management**:

- ABCDE; glucose (hypoglycaemia in late stages).
- **Activated charcoal** if fully conscious and within one hour (CDST) — paracetamol is well adsorbed.
- Establish the **time of ingestion**, the amount (calculate mg/kg), the formulation and any co-ingestants.
- **Always transport** a potentially toxic paracetamol ingestion and all deliberate overdoses, even if the patient looks well — this is one of the most important teaching points, because the patient is asymptomatic during the window in which treatment is most effective.
- Contact the Poisons Information Helpline.

**Hospital**:

- **Serum paracetamol level at four hours or later** after ingestion, plotted on the treatment nomogram to decide on NAC (not interpretable before four hours, or with staggered ingestions or unknown times, which are treated empirically).
- **NAC**: the traditional 21-hour IV regimen is **150 mg/kg over one hour, then 50 mg/kg over four hours, then 100 mg/kg over 16 hours** (Hüser et al., 2025); abbreviated two-bag regimens such as the Scottish and Newcastle Acetylcysteine Protocol (SNAP) reduce adverse reactions. NAC is most effective within eight hours, but should be given even if the patient presents late or in liver failure.
- Monitoring of liver function, INR, renal function, glucose and acid–base status; referral to a liver unit for fulminant hepatic failure (transplant criteria).
- Psychosocial assessment before discharge.

## 60.22 Theophylline

**Background**: theophylline and aminophylline (methylxanthines) are used for asthma and COPD; their use has declined, but they remain available, often in sustained-release formulations. Theophylline has a narrow therapeutic index (therapeutic levels about 10–20 mg/L), and toxicity occurs with acute overdose, chronic accumulation (older people, liver disease, heart failure), or drug interactions (ciprofloxacin, erythromycin and other enzyme inhibitors).

**Pathophysiology**: **adenosine receptor antagonism** (CNS stimulation, seizures, arrhythmias), **phosphodiesterase inhibition** (increased cAMP — tachycardia, vasodilatation), and **catecholamine release** with **β-adrenergic stimulation** (tachyarrhythmias, hypokalaemia from intracellular potassium shift, hyperglycaemia, metabolic acidosis, hypotension from β2 vasodilatation).

**Clinical presentation**: **persistent, intractable vomiting** (a hallmark — Kaswa, 2024), haematemesis, abdominal pain; agitation, tremor, insomnia; **sinus tachycardia** and **tachyarrhythmias** (atrial fibrillation, multifocal atrial tachycardia, SVT, ventricular ectopy, VT, VF — Kaswa, 2024, notes arrhythmia in theophylline toxicity); hypotension (acute overdose); **hypokalaemia**, hyperglycaemia, hypophosphataemia, metabolic acidosis; and **seizures**, which are often prolonged, refractory to treatment, and indicate a poor prognosis (Kaswa, 2024, lists theophylline among causes of seizures requiring airway support). Sustained-release preparations cause delayed peak toxicity.

**Children**: accidental ingestions can cause severe toxicity; chronic toxicity also occurs.

**Prehospital management**:

- ABCDE; oxygen; IV access; continuous ECG monitoring.
- **Seizures**: benzodiazepines (CDST Seizures tool); expect refractory seizures and prepare for airway management.
- Antiemetic if within scope; IV fluids for hypotension; glucose.
- Activated charcoal if fully conscious and within one hour.
- Transport; Poisons Information Helpline.

**Hospital**: **multiple-dose activated charcoal** (theophylline is a classic MDAC indication — Hüser et al., 2025); **haemodialysis** for severe toxicity (very high levels, seizures, life-threatening arrhythmias, or shock) (EXTRIP); potassium replacement (with caution, because hypokalaemia is due to a shift); beta-blockade (esmolol or propranolol) for tachyarrhythmias and hypotension due to β2 stimulation, used cautiously in patients with bronchospasm; benzodiazepines and then phenobarbital for seizures (phenytoin is ineffective); whole bowel irrigation for sustained-release preparations.

## 60.24 Hydrofluoric acid

**Sources**: glass etching and frosting, rust and stain removers, wheel and aluminium cleaners, brick and masonry cleaners, semiconductor manufacturing, petroleum refining, and some household rust-removal products. Hydrofluoric acid (HF) is a **weak acid** but an extremely dangerous poison.

**Pathophysiology**: because HF is a weak acid, it remains largely **non-ionised** and **penetrates deeply** through the skin and tissues. Once in the tissues, it dissociates, and the free **fluoride ion** binds **calcium and magnesium**, forming insoluble salts, causing:

- **local tissue destruction** (liquefactive necrosis, bone erosion) and **severe pain** (from calcium depletion and potassium efflux affecting nerves), often **out of proportion to visible signs**, and often delayed by hours with dilute solutions (<20%);
- **systemic toxicity**: **hypocalcaemia**, **hypomagnesaemia** and **hyperkalaemia**, leading to QT prolongation, ventricular arrhythmias, **ventricular fibrillation** and sudden death — even with relatively small burns of concentrated acid (for example burns involving more than about 1–2% of body surface area with concentrated HF, or any ingestion or significant inhalation).

**Clinical presentation**:

- **Dermal**: severe throbbing pain (sometimes with minimal visible injury initially), erythema, blanching, then grey-white discoloration, blistering and necrosis; fingertip exposures through gloves are common in occupational settings.
- **Eye**: severe pain, corneal damage, blindness.
- **Inhalation**: airway irritation, bronchospasm, pulmonary oedema.
- **Ingestion**: GI burns plus rapid and often fatal systemic hypocalcaemia.
- **Systemic**: tetany, carpopedal spasm, prolonged QT, arrhythmias, cardiac arrest.

**Children**: household rust removers; children have a larger surface area relative to weight and are at greater risk of systemic toxicity.

**Prehospital management**:

- **PPE** (HF penetrates standard gloves; use appropriate chemical-resistant gloves); remove contaminated clothing.
- **Irrigate copiously with water** (skin at least 20 minutes, ideally longer; CDST chemical burns — cooling with running water for at least one hour; eyes at least 20 minutes) (HPCSA PBEC, 2025, pp. 34, 54).
- **Topical calcium gluconate gel (2.5%)** applied to dermal burns and massaged into the area (often with a glove filled with gel for hand burns) — the fluoride binds the calcium in the gel; this is available in industrial first-aid kits and in hospitals.
- ABCDE; continuous **ECG monitoring** (QT prolongation, peaked T waves, arrhythmias).
- **Systemic calcium**: Kaswa (2024) lists **calcium gluconate** as the antidote for hydrofluoric acid. In the CDST, IV calcium (^calcium gluconate 10%^ or ^calcium chloride 10%^) is within ANT/ECP scope for poisoning (HPCSA PBEC, 2025, p. 68); for suspected systemic HF toxicity (large or concentrated burns, ingestion, inhalation, arrhythmias, tetany), discuss IV calcium with a senior medical advisor or the Poisons Information Helpline, and give it if instructed or if the patient is peri-arrest with signs of hypocalcaemia.
- Analgesia (persistent pain indicates ongoing fluoride activity and the need for more calcium).
- Do not induce vomiting; for ingestion, transport urgently.
- Transport all HF exposures other than trivial dilute skin contact; pre-alert.

**Hospital**: repeated topical calcium; **subcutaneous infiltration of calcium gluconate** or **intra-arterial calcium** for digital burns unresponsive to gel; **IV calcium and magnesium** with frequent electrolyte monitoring; treatment of hyperkalaemia; haemodialysis in severe systemic toxicity; surgical debridement.

## 60.25 Iron

**Sources**: iron supplements (ferrous sulphate, ferrous fumarate, ferrous gluconate — which contain about 20%, 33% and 12% elemental iron respectively), prenatal vitamins and multivitamins. Iron tablets are a classic cause of serious accidental poisoning in young children, because they look like sweets and are found in homes with pregnant or postpartum women.

**Toxic dose** (elemental iron): **less than 20 mg/kg** — usually asymptomatic; **20–60 mg/kg** — GI symptoms; **more than 60 mg/kg** — potentially serious systemic toxicity (Hüser et al., 2025, list >60 mg/kg as the threshold for deferoxamine consideration); more than about 100–200 mg/kg — potentially lethal (Tintinalli et al., 2020).

**Pathophysiology**:

- **Direct corrosive effect** on the GI mucosa → haemorrhagic gastroenteritis, fluid and blood loss, and later scarring.
- Once transferrin is saturated, **free iron** circulates and enters cells, where it **disrupts mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation** and catalyses free-radical formation → **metabolic (lactic) acidosis**, shock (hypovolaemic, distributive and cardiogenic), coagulopathy, and **hepatic necrosis** (the liver receives the first-pass iron load).

**Clinical presentation** — classically five stages (which may overlap; Tintinalli et al., 2020):

1. **Gastrointestinal stage (0–6 hours)**: vomiting (including haematemesis), diarrhoea (including bloody), abdominal pain; **the absence of vomiting within six hours makes serious toxicity unlikely**.
2. **Latent or "quiescent" stage (6–24 hours)**: apparent improvement of GI symptoms, but ongoing cellular toxicity — **a dangerous false reassurance**; subtle tachycardia and acidosis may be present.
3. **Shock and metabolic acidosis stage (12–48 hours)**: hypotension, profound metabolic acidosis, coagulopathy, lethargy, coma, seizures, multi-organ failure.
4. **Hepatotoxicity (2–3 days)**: hepatic necrosis and failure.
5. **Bowel obstruction (2–6 weeks)**: gastric outlet or small bowel strictures from scarring.

**Children**: the main at-risk group; even a small number of adult-strength tablets may be dangerous for a toddler.

**Prehospital management**:

- ABCDE; IV access; **IV fluids** for hypovolaemia (aggressive fluid resuscitation is often required); glucose.
- **Activated charcoal does not bind iron** — do not give it (CDST exclusion) (HPCSA PBEC, 2025, p. 54); do not induce vomiting.
- Establish the product, number of tablets and elemental iron content (mg/kg), and time of ingestion; take the container.
- **Transport** any child with a potentially toxic ingestion (>20 mg/kg elemental iron) or any symptoms, and all deliberate ingestions, even if they appear to have improved (latent stage).
- Contact the Poisons Information Helpline.

**Hospital**: serum iron level (at about four to six hours); abdominal X-ray (iron tablets are radio-opaque); **whole bowel irrigation** for large ingestions with tablets visible on X-ray (Kaswa, 2024; Hüser et al., 2025); **deferoxamine** (a chelator that binds free iron to form ferrioxamine, excreted in the urine, which may turn a reddish "vin rosé" colour) at **15 mg/kg/h IV**, maximum about 6–8 g/day, for severe toxicity — systemic symptoms, metabolic acidosis, shock, or very high iron levels (Hüser et al., 2025); monitoring for hypotension and, with prolonged infusions, ARDS.

## 60.26 Oral hypoglycaemic medications

**Agents**:

- **Sulphonylureas** (glibenclamide, gliclazide, glimepiride) — stimulate insulin release from pancreatic β-cells by closing ATP-sensitive potassium channels;
- **meglitinides** (repaglinide) — similar mechanism, shorter-acting;
- **biguanides** — **metformin** — reduce hepatic glucose production and increase peripheral glucose uptake; rarely cause hypoglycaemia alone, but can cause **metformin-associated lactic acidosis (MALA)** in overdose or with renal failure;
- **DPP-4 inhibitors** (sitagliptin), **SGLT2 inhibitors** (dapagliflozin, empagliflozin — risk of euglycaemic diabetic ketoacidosis), **thiazolidinediones** (pioglitazone) — low risk of hypoglycaemia;
- **insulin** (injected) for comparison.

**Pathophysiology and presentation**:

- **Sulphonylureas** cause **profound and prolonged hypoglycaemia** (up to 24–72 hours, especially with long-acting agents and in renal impairment), which may be **delayed** for up to 8–16 hours after ingestion (or longer with modified-release preparations), and may recur after apparently successful treatment because glucose administration stimulates further insulin release. Symptoms: adrenergic (sweating, tremor, palpitations, anxiety, hunger) and neuroglycopenic (confusion, abnormal behaviour — often mistaken for intoxication or a psychiatric emergency — slurred speech, focal deficits mimicking stroke, seizures, coma). **A single sulphonylurea tablet can cause serious hypoglycaemia in a toddler.**
- **Metformin overdose**: nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain, then **severe lactic acidosis** (Kussmaul breathing, hypotension, altered consciousness), hypothermia, and multi-organ failure; hypoglycaemia is uncommon.

**Children**: high risk with sulphonylureas; may present with seizures or coma; hypoglycaemia may be delayed.

**Prehospital management (CDST glucose management, HPCSA PBEC, 2025, pp. 47, 54)**:

- Check glucose in every patient with altered consciousness, seizures or abnormal behaviour.
- **If glucose <3.5 mmol/L**:
  - conscious and able to swallow: oral glucose; or smear **25 g oral glucose** inside the cheek/gums (child: <5 years 5 g, 5–11 years 10 g, >12 years 15 g);
  - otherwise: \***dextrose 50% 20 mL IV**\* (consider dilution with 20 mL of sterile water or normal saline) (child: **dextrose 10% 3 mL/kg IV**);
  - no IV access: \***glucagon 1 mg IM**\* (child 0.5 mg) — note that glucagon is less effective in patients with depleted glycogen stores (malnutrition, alcohol) and may be less effective in sulphonylurea toxicity;
  - **recheck glucose after 10 minutes** and repeat if still <3.5 mmol/L;
  - give **thiamine 100 mg IV/IM** if there is concern about vitamin deficiency (malnourishment, chronic alcohol use) (HPCSA PBEC, 2025, p. 73).
- After correcting hypoglycaemia, give oral carbohydrate if the patient can eat, and monitor glucose closely.
- Activated charcoal if fully conscious within one hour.
- **Transport all sulphonylurea overdoses and all children** who may have ingested a sulphonylurea, even if the glucose is currently normal (delayed and recurrent hypoglycaemia); **refusal of transport after treatment of hypoglycaemia caused by an oral agent is unsafe**.
- Metformin overdose with acidosis: ABCDE, fluids, transport.

**Hospital**: dextrose infusion with frequent glucose monitoring; **octreotide** (a somatostatin analogue that inhibits insulin release) for sulphonylurea-induced recurrent hypoglycaemia; observation for at least 24 hours (longer with modified-release preparations); **haemodialysis** for severe metformin-associated lactic acidosis.

## 60.27 Lithium

**Background**: lithium carbonate is a mood stabiliser used for bipolar disorder (52.4). It has a **narrow therapeutic index** (therapeutic serum levels about 0.6–1.2 mmol/L), is not metabolised, and is excreted almost entirely by the kidneys, so toxicity is precipitated by dehydration (vomiting, diarrhoea, heat, reduced fluid intake), renal impairment, low-sodium diets, and drugs that reduce lithium clearance (**NSAIDs, ACE inhibitors, ARBs, thiazide diuretics**).

**Types of toxicity** (Decker et al., 2015):

1. **Acute toxicity** (a single large ingestion in a lithium-naïve person): GI symptoms dominate initially; neurotoxicity may be delayed as lithium slowly distributes into the brain; serum levels may be very high with relatively few neurological signs at first.
2. **Chronic toxicity** (gradual accumulation in a patient on long-term lithium): **neurological symptoms dominate**, often at lower serum levels, because brain levels are already high; it is common in older people and is often precipitated by intercurrent illness or new medications.
3. **Acute-on-chronic toxicity** (an overdose in a patient taking lithium regularly): the most dangerous.

**Pathophysiology**: lithium interferes with sodium and potassium transport, second-messenger systems (inositol phosphate and cAMP), and neurotransmission; it accumulates in the brain, causing neurotoxicity, and in the kidney causes **nephrogenic diabetes insipidus** (polyuria, dehydration — which further reduces lithium excretion).

**Clinical presentation**:

- **GI**: nausea, vomiting, diarrhoea.
- **Neurological**: fine tremor becoming **coarse tremor**, ataxia, dysarthria, **confusion**, agitation, **hyperreflexia, clonus, myoclonus, fasciculations**, choreoathetosis, seizures, coma; **SILENT** (Syndrome of Irreversible Lithium-Effectuated Neurotoxicity — persistent cerebellar dysfunction and cognitive impairment) after severe toxicity.
- **Cardiac**: T-wave flattening or inversion, bradycardia, sinus node dysfunction, QT prolongation (usually not life-threatening).
- **Renal**: polyuria (nephrogenic DI), acute kidney injury.
- Lithium can also contribute to serotonin syndrome when combined with serotonergic drugs.

**Children**: accidental ingestion of a parent's lithium; toxicity follows the acute pattern.

**Prehospital management**:

- ABCDE; glucose; seizures per CDST.
- **IV normal saline** to correct dehydration and promote renal lithium excretion (cautious in heart failure).
- **Activated charcoal does not bind lithium — do not give it** (CDST exclusion) (HPCSA PBEC, 2025, p. 54).
- Ask about NSAIDs, ACE inhibitors and diuretics, and about recent illness, vomiting or diarrhoea.
- Transport; Poisons Information Helpline; bring all medications.

**Hospital (Decker et al., 2015)**:

- Serial serum lithium levels (repeated every four to six hours because of delayed absorption, especially with sustained-release preparations; Hüser et al., 2025), electrolytes, renal function, ECG.
- **Whole bowel irrigation** for large ingestions of sustained-release lithium (Kaswa, 2024; Hüser et al., 2025).
- **Haemodialysis** (lithium is small, water-soluble, not protein-bound and therefore highly dialysable): EXTRIP recommends haemodialysis in severe poisoning — for example lithium >4.0 mmol/L with impaired kidney function, or the presence of a decreased level of consciousness, seizures or life-threatening dysrhythmias irrespective of the level — and suggests it when the level is >5.0 mmol/L, when there is significant confusion, or when the expected time to reach <1.0 mmol/L with optimal management is more than 36 hours.
- Stop lithium and interacting drugs.

## 60.28 Psychiatric medications

TCAs (60.18) and lithium (60.27) are covered separately. This section covers **antipsychotics**, **SSRIs/SNRIs and other antidepressants**, **anticonvulsant mood stabilisers**, and the important drug-induced syndromes (**serotonin syndrome** and **neuroleptic malignant syndrome**).

### Antipsychotics

**Agents**: first-generation (haloperidol, chlorpromazine, flupenthixol, zuclopenthixol) and second-generation (olanzapine, risperidone, quetiapine, clozapine, aripiprazole) antipsychotics.

**Pathophysiology and presentation in overdose**:

- **Dopamine D2 blockade** → extrapyramidal effects (acute dystonia, akathisia, parkinsonism), and rarely **neuroleptic malignant syndrome**.
- **Alpha-1 blockade** → vasodilatation, **hypotension**, reflex tachycardia (especially quetiapine, chlorpromazine, clozapine). Hüser et al. (2025) note that hypotension due to alpha-antagonism may respond well to phenylephrine or vasopressin.
- **Antihistamine (H1) effects** → **sedation, CNS depression, coma** (quetiapine, olanzapine, clozapine).
- **Antimuscarinic effects** → anticholinergic toxidrome (olanzapine, quetiapine, clozapine, chlorpromazine).
- **Potassium channel blockade** → **QT prolongation and torsade de pointes** (haloperidol, droperidol, quetiapine); some cause sodium channel blockade (thioridazine, chlorpromazine).
- **Seizures** (clozapine, chlorpromazine, olanzapine).
- **Hypothermia or hyperthermia**.
- **Clozapine**: hypersalivation, seizures, myocarditis, agranulocytosis.

**Children**: a single tablet of some antipsychotics can cause sedation, dystonia or hypotension in a toddler.

**Management**: ABCDE; airway protection for CNS depression; IV fluids for hypotension (vasopressors if refractory); continuous ECG (QT); benzodiazepines for seizures; activated charcoal only if CDST criteria are met; transport. **Acute dystonia** is treated in hospital (or by protocol) with an anticholinergic (biperiden or benztropine) or diphenhydramine; **torsade de pointes** with IV magnesium sulphate and correction of hypokalaemia (hospital/ACLS).

### SSRIs, SNRIs and other antidepressants

- **SSRIs** (fluoxetine, sertraline, citalopram, escitalopram, paroxetine): relatively safe in isolated overdose — nausea, vomiting, drowsiness, tremor, tachycardia; **seizures** and **QT prolongation** with **citalopram and escitalopram**; risk of **serotonin syndrome**, especially with combinations.
- **SNRIs** (venlafaxine, duloxetine): more toxic than SSRIs — seizures, tachycardia, hypotension, **QRS widening** and arrhythmias with large venlafaxine overdoses, and serotonin syndrome.
- **Mirtazapine**: sedation, usually benign.
- **Trazodone**: sedation, hypotension (alpha-blockade), priapism.
- **Bupropion**: **seizures** (common, may be delayed with sustained-release forms), tachycardia, QRS widening and cardiotoxicity (Hüser et al., 2025).
- **MAOIs** (rarely used): delayed, severe sympathomimetic and serotonergic toxicity, hypertensive crises with tyramine-containing foods or interacting drugs.

### Serotonin syndrome (serotonin toxicity)

- **Cause**: excess serotonergic activity in the CNS, usually from combinations of serotonergic drugs (SSRIs, SNRIs, MAOIs, TCAs, tramadol, pethidine, fentanyl, MDMA, linezolid, lithium, St John's wort, ondansetron in combinations) or overdose (Boyer and Shannon, 2005).
- **Onset**: rapid — usually within hours (often within six hours) of a dose change or overdose.
- **Triad**: (1) **altered mental status** — agitation, anxiety, confusion; (2) **autonomic instability** — tachycardia, hypertension, hyperthermia, diaphoresis, mydriasis, diarrhoea; (3) **neuromuscular hyperactivity** — **clonus** (spontaneous, inducible or ocular), **hyperreflexia**, tremor, rigidity (especially of the lower limbs). Diagnosed with the **Hunter criteria** (Dunkley et al., 2003).
- **Management**: stop serotonergic drugs; ABCDE; **benzodiazepines** for agitation, tremor and hyperthermia (CDST Aggression tool); **active cooling** for hyperthermia (CDST: ≥38 °C — remove clothing, water spray, fan or air-conditioning); IV fluids; transport. Hospital: **cyproheptadine** (a 5-HT2A antagonist, via nasogastric tube) or IV olanzapine (Hüser et al., 2025); chlorpromazine for severe serotonin toxicity with hyperthermia in addition to benzodiazepines (RCEM, 2025); intubation and paralysis for temperature above about 41 °C. **Avoid physical restraint** (worsens hyperthermia and lactic acidosis) and avoid fentanyl (RCEM, 2025).

### Neuroleptic malignant syndrome (NMS)

- **Cause**: dopamine D2 blockade (antipsychotics, metoclopramide) or abrupt withdrawal of dopaminergic drugs (levodopa) — an idiosyncratic reaction, not dose-dependent.
- **Onset**: gradual, over days (usually within the first two weeks of starting or increasing the drug).
- **Features**: **"lead-pipe" muscle rigidity**, **hyperthermia**, **altered mental status** (confusion, stupor, mutism), **autonomic instability** (labile BP, tachycardia, diaphoresis, incontinence), **elevated creatine kinase** (rhabdomyolysis), leucocytosis; **bradyreflexia** and absence of clonus distinguish it from serotonin syndrome.
- **Management**: stop the causative drug; ABCDE; **active cooling**; IV fluids (rhabdomyolysis, AKI); benzodiazepines for agitation and rigidity; transport. Hospital: **bromocriptine** (dopamine agonist), amantadine, **dantrolene** (muscle relaxant) (Hüser et al., 2025); ICU care; ECT for refractory cases.

| Feature | Serotonin syndrome | NMS |
|---|---|---|
| Drug | Serotonergic agents | Dopamine antagonists |
| Onset | Hours | Days |
| Neuromuscular | **Clonus, hyperreflexia, tremor** | **Lead-pipe rigidity, bradyreflexia** |
| Pupils | Dilated | Normal |
| Bowel sounds | Increased, diarrhoea | Normal/decreased |
| Resolution | Usually within 24 hours | Days to two weeks |
| Specific treatment | Cyproheptadine | Bromocriptine, dantrolene |

### Anticonvulsant mood stabilisers

- **Valproate**: CNS depression and coma, respiratory depression, hypotension, metabolic acidosis, **hyperammonaemia** (encephalopathy, cerebral oedema), hypernatraemia, hepatotoxicity, pancreatitis, bone marrow suppression; hospital treatment includes **L-carnitine** for hyperammonaemia and haemodialysis for severe toxicity.
- **Carbamazepine**: ataxia, **nystagmus**, dysarthria, drowsiness, coma, **seizures** (paradoxically), anticholinergic effects, respiratory depression, hypotension, **sodium channel blockade** (QRS widening, arrhythmias), hyponatraemia; delayed and erratic absorption (forms bezoars); **MDAC** is indicated (Hüser et al., 2025); haemodialysis for severe toxicity; sodium bicarbonate for QRS widening.
- **Lamotrigine**: seizures, QRS widening in large overdoses.

## 60.29 Ethanol

**Sources**: alcoholic beverages (beer, wine, spirits, traditional sorghum beer "umqombothi", illicit home-brewed alcohol), and non-beverage ethanol in **hand sanitisers, mouthwashes, perfumes, colognes and cooking extracts** (a particular risk for children).

**Pathophysiology**: ethanol is rapidly absorbed (mainly from the small intestine), crosses the blood–brain barrier, and enhances **GABA-A** activity and inhibits **NMDA glutamate** receptors, causing CNS depression. It is metabolised mainly in the liver by **alcohol dehydrogenase** (to acetaldehyde) and aldehyde dehydrogenase (to acetate), at roughly constant (zero-order) rates; this metabolism generates NADH, which **inhibits gluconeogenesis**, causing **hypoglycaemia** (especially in children and in malnourished or fasting adults), and contributes to lactic acidosis and **alcoholic ketoacidosis**. Ethanol also causes vasodilatation (heat loss — **hypothermia**), diuresis (inhibition of ADH), and gastric irritation.

**Clinical presentation** (correlates approximately with blood alcohol concentration, modified by tolerance):

- mild: euphoria, disinhibition, impaired judgement and coordination;
- moderate: slurred speech, ataxia, nystagmus, labile mood, aggression, nausea and vomiting;
- severe: stupor, coma, **respiratory depression**, **aspiration** of vomitus, hypotension, **hypothermia**, **hypoglycaemia**, and death.

Associated problems include **trauma** (head injury — "never assume a drunk patient is just drunk"), violence, road traffic collisions, drowning, co-ingestion of other drugs, alcoholic ketoacidosis, Wernicke's encephalopathy, withdrawal and delirium tremens (52.8.3).

**Children**: are particularly prone to **hypoglycaemia, hypothermia, seizures and coma** after relatively small amounts (for example drinking from adults' glasses, or ingesting hand sanitiser, mouthwash or perfume).

**Toxic alcohols**: methanol and ethylene glycol (60.19) and isopropanol (rubbing alcohol — CNS depression, haemorrhagic gastritis, ketosis without acidosis) must be considered when a patient appears intoxicated without the smell of ethanol, or has an unexplained metabolic acidosis.

**Prehospital management**:

- ABCDE: **airway protection** (lateral/recovery position, suction; airway tool if unable to maintain the airway); oxygen; BVM if hypoventilating.
- **Glucose** — treat hypoglycaemia (CDST); **thiamine 100 mg IV/IM** in chronic alcohol users or malnourished patients (HPCSA PBEC, 2025, pp. 42, 73).
- **Temperature** — prevent and treat hypothermia.
- **Full secondary survey for injuries**, especially head injury; GCS that does not improve or deteriorates must be attributed to head injury or other causes until proven otherwise.
- **No activated charcoal** (not adsorbed) (HPCSA PBEC, 2025, p. 54).
- Agitation and aggression: CDST Aggression tool (with caution — benzodiazepines add to respiratory depression).
- Withdrawal seizures: CDST Seizures tool.
- Capacity: an intoxicated patient may lack capacity to refuse care; do not leave an intoxicated patient who cannot protect their airway or who is at risk.
- Transport (children always).

## 60.30 Hydrocarbons

**Definition and classification**: hydrocarbons are organic compounds composed mainly of carbon and hydrogen, derived from petroleum, coal, wood or plants. They are classified as:

1. **Aliphatic (straight- or branched-chain)**: paraffin (kerosene), petrol, diesel, lighter fluid, mineral spirits, mineral oil, lubricating oils, butane and propane.
2. **Aromatic (benzene ring)**: **benzene** (bone marrow toxicity, leukaemia), **toluene** (glue, paint thinners — CNS toxicity, **renal tubular acidosis with hypokalaemia**), xylene.
3. **Halogenated**: carbon tetrachloride (hepatotoxicity and nephrotoxicity), trichloroethane and trichloroethylene (cardiac sensitisation), **methylene chloride** (paint strippers — metabolised to **carbon monoxide**).
4. **Terpenes (plant-derived)**: turpentine, pine oil, eucalyptus oil, camphor (seizures).

**Pathophysiology** — four main toxic effects (Tintinalli et al., 2020):

1. **Pulmonary (aspiration pneumonitis)** — the most common and important, determined by low **viscosity**, low **surface tension** and high **volatility** (60.16).
2. **CNS** — intoxication, depression, seizures and coma, especially with inhaled volatile, aromatic and halogenated hydrocarbons; chronic encephalopathy with abuse.
3. **Cardiac** — **myocardial sensitisation to catecholamines** with halogenated and aromatic hydrocarbons → ventricular arrhythmias and **sudden sniffing death**.
4. **Other organ toxicity** — hepatotoxicity (carbon tetrachloride), nephrotoxicity, haematological toxicity (benzene), peripheral neuropathy (n-hexane), carbon monoxide production (methylene chloride), and skin and eye burns.

**Clinical presentation**: as in 60.16 — coughing, choking, tachypnoea, wheeze, crackles and hypoxia after ingestion and aspiration (with fever and possibly delayed onset over six hours); CNS depression; arrhythmias; and characteristic odours.

**Children**: paraffin ingestion is common in South African children (Balme et al., 2012), with a high risk of aspiration.

**Prehospital management**: as in 60.16 — decontamination (remove clothing, wash skin), oxygen, airway support, bronchodilators for bronchospasm, **no induced vomiting and no activated charcoal** (HPCSA PBEC, 2025, p. 54), avoidance of exertion and adrenaline in inhalant abusers (except cardiac arrest), continuous ECG, seizure management, and transport; observe asymptomatic children who coughed or choked for at least six hours in hospital. For methylene chloride, treat as carbon monoxide poisoning (100% oxygen).

## 60.31 NSAID

**Agents**: ibuprofen (the most commonly ingested), diclofenac, naproxen, **mefenamic acid**, indomethacin, ketoprofen, and others; aspirin is covered under salicylates (60.17). In South Africa, ibuprofen, diclofenac and mefenamic acid are widely available.

**Pathophysiology**: NSAIDs inhibit **cyclooxygenase (COX-1 and COX-2)**, reducing prostaglandin synthesis. In overdose this causes **GI irritation and bleeding** (loss of protective prostaglandins), **renal effects** (reduced renal blood flow — acute kidney injury, especially with dehydration, ACE inhibitors and diuretics; hyperkalaemia), and, with very large ingestions, **CNS depression**, **seizures** (particularly with **mefenamic acid**), and **metabolic acidosis**; NSAIDs also reduce lithium clearance (60.27).

**Clinical presentation**: most ibuprofen overdoses are mild or asymptomatic — nausea, vomiting, epigastric pain, drowsiness, headache, tinnitus. **Ibuprofen ingestions below about 100 mg/kg are usually asymptomatic**, whereas **more than about 400 mg/kg** can cause serious toxicity — coma, seizures, metabolic acidosis, hypotension, acute kidney injury, respiratory depression and, rarely, death (Tintinalli et al., 2020). Mefenamic acid overdose frequently causes seizures. GI bleeding and AKI may occur with large ingestions or in vulnerable people (older adults, dehydration).

**Children**: accidental ingestion of ibuprofen syrup is common and usually benign; calculate the mg/kg dose.

**Prehospital management**:

- ABCDE; glucose.
- **Seizures** (mefenamic acid, massive ibuprofen): CDST Seizures tool.
- **Activated charcoal** if fully conscious and within one hour of a potentially toxic ingestion (CDST).
- IV fluids if dehydrated or hypotensive.
- Calculate mg/kg; contact the Poisons Information Helpline; per CDST, a non-toxic dose in a well patient may be managed at home with advice, but deliberate ingestions require transport and psychosocial assessment (HPCSA PBEC, 2025, p. 55).

**Hospital**: supportive care; renal function and electrolytes; acid–base monitoring; proton pump inhibitors for GI symptoms; there is no specific antidote.

## 60.32 Chemical warfare agents

Chemical warfare agents (CWAs) are toxic chemicals used deliberately to kill, injure or incapacitate. They may be encountered in terrorism, military conflict, or as industrial chemicals with similar effects (chlorine, ammonia, cyanide, organophosphates). Riot-control agents (pepper spray) are used routinely by SAPS and private security, and are a common prehospital exposure.

### Principles of response (CBRN incidents)

1. **Recognise** a chemical incident: multiple casualties with similar symptoms, unexplained odours or clouds, dead animals, reports of explosions with unusual symptoms.
2. **Scene safety**: approach from **upwind and uphill**; do not enter the **hot zone** (contaminated area) without appropriate PPE and training; establish **hot, warm (decontamination) and cold (treatment) zones**; inform control and request HAZMAT/fire services and SAPS.
3. **Decontamination** before treatment in the cold zone (except for immediate life-saving interventions by appropriately protected personnel): **remove clothing** (which removes the majority of contamination), then wash with copious water (soap and water) (HPCSA PBEC, 2025, p. 54; Henretig, Kirk and McKay, 2019).
4. **Mass-casualty triage** (for example START/SALT).
5. **Antidotes** where available, and supportive ABCDE care.
6. **Notify** the receiving hospitals early to avoid secondary contamination of emergency departments.

### Classes of chemical warfare agents

| Class | Examples | Mechanism | Features | Management |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Nerve agents** | Tabun (GA), sarin (GB), soman (GD), VX, Novichok agents | Organophosphates — irreversible acetylcholinesterase inhibition (very rapid ageing with soman) | **Cholinergic crisis**: miosis, rhinorrhoea, bronchorrhoea, bronchospasm, salivation, sweating, vomiting, diarrhoea, fasciculations, weakness, seizures, apnoea, death within minutes | Decontamination; **atropine** (titrated until secretions dry — as in 60.8, CDST ^atropine 2 mg IV^ doubling every 3 min); **oximes** (pralidoxime) early, before ageing; **benzodiazepines** (diazepam/midazolam) for seizures; ventilation (avoid succinylcholine); military autoinjectors combine atropine and pralidoxime |
| **Vesicants (blister agents)** | Sulphur mustard, nitrogen mustard, lewisite, phosgene oxime | Alkylating agents (mustard) — DNA damage; arsenical (lewisite) | Mustard: **delayed** (2–24 h) erythema and blistering, eye injury (conjunctivitis, blindness), airway injury, bone marrow suppression; lewisite: **immediate** pain | Immediate decontamination (effects are delayed but damage begins within minutes); eye irrigation; burn-type care; airway management; **dimercaprol (BAL)** for lewisite (hospital) |
| **Pulmonary (choking) agents** | **Chlorine**, **phosgene**, diphosgene, chloropicrin, ammonia | Airway and alveolar injury (60.10); phosgene (smell of freshly cut hay/grass) causes **delayed non-cardiogenic pulmonary oedema** (up to 24–48 h) | Cough, dyspnoea, chest tightness, wheeze, then pulmonary oedema | Remove from exposure; oxygen; **rest** (exertion worsens pulmonary oedema with phosgene); bronchodilators; observe for 24–48 h |

### High-yield points — 60.17 to 60.32

- **Salicylates**: tinnitus, hyperventilation, mixed respiratory alkalosis + high anion gap metabolic acidosis, hyperthermia; never hypoventilate after intubation; urinary alkalinisation; haemodialysis.
- **TCAs**: rapid deterioration; QRS >100 ms → seizures; >160 ms → VT; CDST sodium bicarbonate 8.4% 100 mL (child 2 mL/kg), hyperventilate if intubated (ETCO₂ 20–25 mmHg); no flumazenil or physostigmine.
- **Ethylene glycol / methanol**: osmolal then anion gap; calcium oxalate → AKI (EG); blindness (methanol); no charcoal; fomepizole/ethanol, haemodialysis.
- **Colchicine**: GI phase → multi-organ failure → alopecia; flame lily (*Gloriosa superba*); no antidote; transport all.
- **Paracetamol**: >150 mg/kg; NAPQI; asymptomatic first 24 h; level at ≥4 h; NAC 150/50/100 mg/kg; transport all.
- **Theophylline**: intractable vomiting, tachyarrhythmias, hypokalaemia, refractory seizures; MDAC, haemodialysis.
- **HF acid**: pain out of proportion; hypocalcaemia, hypomagnesaemia, hyperkalaemia, VF; irrigate, calcium gluconate gel, IV calcium.
- **Iron**: >60 mg/kg serious; five stages including a deceptive latent phase; no charcoal; WBI; deferoxamine.
- **Oral hypoglycaemics**: sulphonylureas — prolonged, delayed and recurrent hypoglycaemia; CDST dextrose 50% 20 mL / 10% 3 mL/kg / glucagon; transport all; octreotide in hospital.
- **Lithium**: narrow therapeutic index; chronic toxicity — neurotoxicity; no charcoal; saline; haemodialysis (EXTRIP).
- **Psychiatric drugs**: antipsychotics — sedation, hypotension, QT; serotonin syndrome (clonus, hours) vs NMS (rigidity, days).
- **Ethanol**: airway, glucose, thiamine, temperature, look for head injury; children — hypoglycaemia.
- **Hydrocarbons**: aspiration pneumonitis, CNS, cardiac sensitisation, organ toxicity.
- **NSAIDs**: usually benign; >400 mg/kg ibuprofen serious; mefenamic acid seizures.
- **Chemical warfare agents**: upwind, zones, undress and decontaminate; nerve agents → atropine, oximes, benzodiazepines; phosgene → rest, delayed oedema.
