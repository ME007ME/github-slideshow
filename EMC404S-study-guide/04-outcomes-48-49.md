# Outcome 48. Assess variations in approach to behavioural emergencies in children and adolescents

## Why children and adolescents are different

Children and adolescents differ from adults developmentally, physiologically, legally and socially. Their ability to describe distress depends on their developmental stage; distress often presents as behaviour (tantrums, aggression, withdrawal, regression, school refusal, somatic complaints) rather than words; they depend on caregivers who may themselves be the source of harm; their drug doses are weight-based; and their legal status (consent, confidentiality, mandatory reporting) is different. The CDST defines a child as **younger than 13 years** for clinical purposes, following the South African hospital definition, but advises clinical judgement about whether to use weight-based calculations in older children and adolescents (HPCSA PBEC, 2025, p. 3). Legally, a child is a person under 18 years (Children's Act 38 of 2005, s 1; Constitution, s 28(3); HPCSA, 2023, para 1).

## 48.1 Reflect on the factors the paramedic must consider when assessing a patient's risk of behavioural emergencies in children

### Medical and organic factors

Organic causes must be excluded first, as in adults. In children these include hypoglycaemia (including in neonates and children with diabetes), hypoxia, febrile delirium and CNS infections (meningitis, encephalitis, cerebral malaria), seizures and post-ictal states, head injury (including non-accidental injury), poisoning (accidental poisoning in young children, deliberate overdose in adolescents), substance intoxication in adolescents, and pain the child cannot verbalise. The RCEM (2025) notes that **pre-adolescent** children "are unlikely to have a similar differential diagnosis" to adults, while **adolescents** "are likely to have a similar differential diagnosis to adults and pose similar management challenges".

### Developmental and psychiatric factors

- **Neurodevelopmental disorders** — autism spectrum disorder, ADHD, intellectual disability, communication disorders (see 52.1) — in which distress is often triggered by sensory overload, a change in routine, pain or frustration at not being understood.
- **Mood disorders and suicidality**: suicide is the third leading cause of death in people aged 15–29 (Tilley et al., 2026); risk factors include depression, bullying (including cyber-bullying), academic pressure, family conflict, sexual identity-related stigma, abuse, substance use, and exposure to a peer's suicide (contagion).
- **Self-harm**: lifetime and 12-month self-harm prevalence in adolescents is high worldwide (Tilley et al., 2026, citing Lim et al., 2019).
- **Eating disorders**, typically in adolescent girls (see 52.7).
- **Early-onset psychosis and bipolar disorder**, which commonly emerge in late adolescence.
- **Trauma-related disorders** (acute stress disorder, PTSD).
- **Substance use**: alcohol, cannabis, inhalants, methamphetamine and "nyaope/whoonga" among South African adolescents.

### Social and environmental factors: violence against children in South Africa

Mesthrie (2025) describes violence against children in South Africa as "a widespread and pressing problem in need of urgent attention". Key facts from her analysis are:

- The estimated child homicide rate in South Africa (5.5 per 100,000) is nearly twice the global average.
- 45% of South African households with children reported some form of violence against children in 2021–2022 (Statistics South Africa, 2024, cited by Mesthrie, 2025).
- One in five South African children are likely to be victims of sexual abuse, and two in five male school pupils are likely to be sexually abused (Cloete, 2022, cited by Mesthrie, 2025).
- At least 250,000 homeless children and young adults live on the streets.
- Children's exposure to violence "is often not a once-off event, but rather part of a condition of life" (Falconer et al., 2021, cited by Mesthrie, 2025).
- Exposure to violence changes brain structure and function, producing "cognitive deficits, behavioural and emotional problems, social and educational difficulties and high-risk behaviour such as engaging in unsafe sexual practices and substance abuse", and children exposed to violence have a higher risk of later perpetrating violence (Mesthrie, 2025).
- There is a clear gender dynamic: sexual violence is more pervasive in girls, while adolescent boys are more likely to be victims and perpetrators of physical violence and homicide (Mesthrie, 2025).

The paramedic must therefore consider **child abuse and neglect** as a cause of, or contributor to, every paediatric behavioural emergency. Indicators include an explanation of injury inconsistent with the injury or the child's developmental stage, delay in seeking care, injuries of different ages, patterned injuries (burns, slap or bite marks, loop marks), injuries in non-mobile infants, sexualised behaviour or genital injuries, fearful or "frozen watchfulness" behaviour, and a caregiver who is hostile, intoxicated or evasive.

### Children with disabilities

The Behavioural Emergencies Tutorial 2 memo, drawing on Mesthrie and the disability rights framework (the UN Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities and the African Disability Protocol), notes that children with disabilities face disproportionate exposure to abuse, neglect and exploitation because of communication barriers, stigma and dependence on caregivers. The National Child Care and Protection Policy itself acknowledges a past "lack of adequate support for children with disabilities" (NCPP, 2021, cited by Mesthrie, 2025). The paramedic must use accessible communication, involve a trusted person, and maintain a high index of suspicion for abuse.

## 48.2 Formulate prehospital management techniques for a patient in a behavioural emergency in children

### General approach

1. **Safety** of the child, the family and the crew; SAPS if there is a risk of violence or abuse.
2. **Primary survey (ABCDE)** with age-appropriate vital signs (CDST Quick Reference Chart), **glucose** (treat if <3.5 mmol/L: oral glucose smeared on the gums, or 3 mL/kg 10% dextrose IV, or glucagon 0.5 mg IM if no IV access; HPCSA PBEC, 2025, p. 47), temperature, pupils, and a search for injuries and signs of poisoning.
3. **Developmentally appropriate communication**:
   - get down to the child's eye level; speak calmly and simply; use the child's name;
   - allow the parent or caregiver to stay and hold the child **unless the caregiver is the source of distress or a suspected abuser**;
   - use distraction (toys, a favourite object, a phone video) for younger children;
   - for adolescents, offer privacy and the opportunity to speak without parents present, explain confidentiality and its limits honestly (for example "What you tell me stays with the medical team unless I'm worried that you or someone else is in danger"), and treat them with respect.
4. **Verbal and environmental de-escalation** — low stimulation, fewer people, a quiet space. The RCEM (2025) observes that in pre-adolescent children "brief restraint interventions are more likely to be successful if verbal/environmental de-escalation fails", meaning that a parent holding a young child briefly is often enough, without medication.
5. **Pharmacological sedation (CDST, children <13 years)** — only if the child remains dangerous after de-escalation, using the CDST dose charts (HPCSA PBEC, 2025, pp. 69–72):
   - \*midazolam 0.2 mg/kg buccal or intranasal (maximum 10 mg per dose)\*;
   - \*midazolam 0.15 mg/kg IM (maximum total dose 10 mg)\*;
   - \*midazolam 0.05 mg/kg IV (maximum total 0.5 mg/kg)\*;
   - \*midazolam 0.5 mg/kg PO (maximum total 20 mg)\*;
   - alternatives: \*lorazepam 0.1 mg/kg (PO maximum 4 mg; or IM)\*, diazepam 0.2 mg/kg PO (maximum 10 mg per dose), ^ketamine 4–5 mg/kg IM^ (ANT/ECP);
   - monitor for paradoxical agitation (more common with benzodiazepines in children) and respiratory depression; monitor age-appropriate vital signs every 10 minutes (HPCSA PBEC, 2025, p. 47).
   - For adolescents (13 years and older), the adult CDST doses apply, using clinical judgement for weight.
6. **Specific situations**:
   - **suicide attempt or self-harm**: as in Outcome 46, always transport; ask directly about suicidal intent; consider abuse;
   - **accidental ingestion in a young child**: CDST Poisoning tool (Outcome 62);
   - **child with autism**: reduce noise and lights, allow the caregiver to lead, avoid unnecessary touch, use a known comfort object, allow extra time, and consult any care plan (RCEM, 2025);
   - **suspected abuse**: treat injuries, avoid repeated questioning (which can contaminate later forensic interviews — ask open, non-leading questions only as needed for care), document the child's own words verbatim, preserve evidence, and report (see 48.3).
7. **Transport** with a parent or guardian where appropriate and safe; if abuse is suspected, inform SAPS and the receiving facility, and ensure the child is not released back into an unsafe environment.

## 48.3 Critically appraise emergency care practice in relation to legal and ethical implications related to behavioural emergencies in children

### The constitutional and international framework

- **Section 28 of the Constitution** guarantees every child the right, among others, to family or parental care, to basic nutrition, shelter, basic health care services and social services, and to be protected from maltreatment, neglect, abuse or degradation; and provides that **a child's best interests are of paramount importance in every matter concerning the child** (s 28(2)).
- **Article 19 of the UN Convention on the Rights of the Child** (ratified by South Africa in June 1995) obliges States to "take all appropriate legislative, administrative, social and educational measures to protect the child from all forms of physical or mental violence, injury or abuse, neglect or negligent treatment, maltreatment or exploitation, including sexual abuse", including procedures for "identification, reporting, referral, investigation, treatment and follow-up" (Mesthrie, 2025).
- The CRC's four general principles are non-discrimination (Article 2), the best interests of the child (Article 3), the right to life, survival and development (Article 6), and the right to be heard (Article 12). General Comment 13 adopts a **child rights-based approach**, which treats children as active rights-holders rather than passive objects of welfare, and emphasises child participation (Mesthrie, 2025).

### Consent

- **Children's Act 38 of 2005, s 129(2)**: a child may consent to his or her own medical treatment (or that of his or her child) if the child is **over the age of 12 years** and of **sufficient maturity and mental capacity** to understand the benefits, risks, social and other implications of the treatment.
- **s 129(3)**: a child may consent to a surgical operation if over 12, of sufficient maturity, **and** duly assisted by a parent or guardian.
- **s 129(4)–(5)**: for children younger than 12, or older children lacking sufficient maturity, a parent, guardian or care-giver may consent.
- **s 129(6)**: the superintendent of a hospital or the person in charge (in their absence) may consent to the medical treatment of or surgical operation on a child if the treatment is necessary to preserve the life of the child or to save the child from serious or lasting physical injury or disability, and the need is so urgent that it cannot be deferred for the purpose of obtaining consent.
- In the prehospital emergency, treatment necessary to preserve life may be given without consent under the emergency provisions (NHA, s 7(1)(e)) and the common-law doctrine of necessity.
- **Refusal by a child**: the HPCSA (2023, para 15.2) states that practitioners "should respect the decisions of children who have the legal capacity to make decisions about refusing health care – except in cases where the practitioners believe that it is not in the child's best interests", in which case they should approach the parents or guardians, the person in charge of the health establishment, the Minister, the High Court or the Children's Court. A suicidal adolescent's refusal is therefore not determinative.
- **Children who lack legal capacity** but have sufficient maturity to understand the procedures "should be consulted during the decision-making process" (HPCSA, 2023, para 15.4), which reflects the CRC right to be heard.
- **Mental Health Care Act**: an application for involuntary care of a user under 18 must be made by the parent or guardian (MHCA, s 33(1)(a)(i)).
- **Parental refusal** of necessary treatment that endangers a child may be overridden through s 129(6)–(10) of the Children's Act (including by the Minister or a court), and may itself indicate that the child is in need of care and protection (s 150).

### Mandatory reporting

- **Children's Act, s 110(1)**: a defined list of professionals (including medical practitioners, nurses, psychologists, social workers, teachers, traditional health practitioners and others) who, on reasonable grounds, conclude that a child has been abused in a manner causing physical injury, sexually abused or deliberately neglected, **must** report that conclusion in the prescribed form to a designated child protection organisation, the provincial department of social development, or a police official. **Section 110(2)** allows **any person** who believes on reasonable grounds that a child is in need of care and protection to report. Once a report is made to the police, the police must ensure the child's safety and notify the provincial department of social development or a designated child protection organisation within 24 hours (s 110(4); Mesthrie, 2025). As an ECP, you should regard yourself as bound to report.
- **Sexual Offences Act, s 54(1)** (as amended by Act 13 of 2021): **any person** who has knowledge, reasonable belief or suspicion that a sexual offence has been committed against a person who is vulnerable (which includes a child) must report it **immediately to a police official**; failure to do so is an offence punishable by a fine or imprisonment not exceeding five years, or both (s 54(2)).
- **Domestic Violence Act, s 2A and s 2B** (inserted by Act 14 of 2021, in force from 14 April 2023): functionaries (medical practitioners, health care personnel, social workers, officials in public health establishments, educators and caregivers) who believe or suspect on reasonable grounds that a child may be suffering domestic violence must complete the prescribed report and risk assessment (Form 2) and submit it to a social worker or SAPS; any adult with knowledge, reasonable belief or suspicion of domestic violence against a child must report it to a social worker or SAPS (s 2B). **Exposing a child to domestic violence** (causing a child to see or hear it, or experience its effects) is itself defined as domestic violence (DVA, s 1 as amended).
- **Children in need of care and protection** (Children's Act, s 150) include children who have been abandoned, who live on the street or beg, who are addicted to substances without support, who are exploited, who live in circumstances that may seriously harm their physical, mental or social well-being, or who are physically or mentally neglected. A police official or social worker may remove a child to temporary safe care without a court order in an emergency (s 152).

### Confidentiality

Adolescents are entitled to confidentiality in the same way as adults, but confidentiality is limited by the child's best interests, the duty to report abuse and sexual offences, and serious risk of harm. The paramedic should be honest with the adolescent about these limits.

### Critical appraisal of current practice

1. **Over-reliance on parental decision-making.** Evans, Geduld and Stassen (2018) found that in vignettes involving minors who had attempted suicide, prehospital providers "would allow family to make the ultimate decision about transport". This is problematic: the child's best interests are paramount (Constitution, s 28(2)); a parent's refusal of necessary care may be overridden; and the family may be part of the problem (for example abuse or conflict). The provider must act in the child's best interests, involve SAPS where needed, and transport an at-risk child.
2. **Failure to recognise and report abuse.** Abuse is often missed when the presenting problem is behavioural. Under-reporting is common, driven by uncertainty, fear of confrontation, lack of training, and doubt about whether EMS providers are legally obliged to report. As shown above, the SOA s 54 duty and the DVA s 2B duty apply to everyone, so the ECP is legally obliged to report.
3. **Inadequate child participation.** Mesthrie (2025) argues that South African law and policy pay insufficient attention to child participation, and that "fulfilling the right to protection from all forms of violence necessitates the fulfilment of the right to be heard". In emergency care, this means explaining what is happening in age-appropriate language, asking the child's views, and taking them into account (HPCSA, 2023, para 15.4).
4. **Gender blindness.** Mesthrie (2025) criticises the Children's Act and the NCPP for not taking an explicit gendered stance, despite the gendered pattern of violence. The ECP should be aware of the high risk of sexual violence against girls and of physical violence against adolescent boys.
5. **Limits of protectionism.** The Tutorial 2 memo (drawing on Chikoko's work on street children in Harare) notes that purely protectionist approaches — removing children from the streets without addressing poverty, abuse and exclusion — risk perpetuating harm. Prehospital care should link children to holistic services (social workers, child protection organisations, health and education services).
6. **Use of sedation and restraint in children** carries particular risks (paradoxical reactions, airway compromise, psychological trauma) and should be a last resort, with caregiver involvement and weight-based CDST dosing.
7. **Lack of paediatric mental health resources and training** across the system.

### High-yield points — Outcome 48

- Child (legal) <18 years; CDST child <13 years.
- Children's Act s 129: consent from age 12 if mature; s 129(6) emergency consent by superintendent; HPCSA para 15.2: a child's refusal may be overridden in their best interests.
- Mandatory reporting: Children's Act s 110 (professionals; any person may report), SOA s 54 (any person — immediately to SAPS; offence if not), DVA ss 2A/2B.
- Best interests of the child are paramount (Constitution s 28(2)).
- Mesthrie (2025): child homicide rate twice the global average; 45% of households report violence against children; need for child participation and a gendered approach.
- CDST paediatric midazolam: 0.2 mg/kg buccal/IN, 0.15 mg/kg IM, 0.05 mg/kg IV, 0.5 mg/kg PO.

# Outcome 49. Assess variations in the approach to behavioural emergencies in geriatrics

## Why older adults are different

Desai, Kulkarni and Verma (n.d.) state that "older adults are not simply younger agitated patients with more years attached to them. Frailty, sensory impairment, multimorbidity, polypharmacy, sarcopenia, lower physiologic reserve, and preexisting cognitive impairment all alter how agitation emerges and how treatment behaves once it is given." Age-related changes in pharmacokinetics (reduced renal and hepatic clearance, increased body fat, reduced albumin) and pharmacodynamics (increased brain sensitivity to sedatives) "narrow the margin between useful calming and preventable harm".

## 49.1 Reflect on the factors the paramedic must consider when assessing a patient's risk of behavioural emergencies in geriatric patients

### 1. Delirium — the most important cause to identify

Delirium is common in older persons, is frequently missed, and carries a high mortality. It is characterised by acute onset, a fluctuating course, inattention, disorganised thinking and an altered level of consciousness, and may be **hyperactive** (agitated, restless), **hypoactive** (quiet, withdrawn, drowsy — easily mistaken for depression or "tiredness") or **mixed** (Inouye, Westendorp and Saczynski, 2014). Desai, Kulkarni and Verma (n.d.) emphasise that "medically ill older adults with fluctuating consciousness and new inattention should be presumed delirious until shown otherwise". New visual hallucinations or abrupt changes in arousal should heighten concern for delirium (except in Lewy body dementia, where visual hallucinations are part of the disease).

### 2. Underlying medical causes

A cause-oriented assessment should begin with physiology and safety. Desai, Kulkarni and Verma (n.d.), citing Shenvi et al. and Kennedy et al., advise immediate evaluation for **hypoxia, hypoglycaemia, shock, acute coronary syndromes, stroke, intracranial haemorrhage (including subdural haematoma after minor falls), sepsis and severe pain**, and stress that **urinary retention, constipation, medication effects and environmental triggers** are also common and easily missed. Common infections presenting as behavioural change are urinary tract infection and pneumonia, often without fever.

### 3. Medications and polypharmacy

Medication effects are "often decisive": anticholinergics, recent opioid escalation, abrupt sedative withdrawal, dopamine-blocking agents causing akathisia, corticosteroids and polypharmacy interactions "can each be sufficient to produce or amplify agitation" (Desai, Kulkarni and Verma, n.d.). The 2023 American Geriatrics Society Beers Criteria list benzodiazepines, antipsychotics and anticholinergic drugs among those that can induce or worsen delirium, cognitive impairment, falls and fractures in older adults (American Geriatrics Society Beers Criteria Update Expert Panel, 2023). Alcohol use and withdrawal are frequently overlooked in older people.

### 4. Dementia

Around 97% of people with dementia experience at least one behavioural or psychological symptom during their illness, and agitation affects about 30–50% of people with Alzheimer's disease and related dementias (Kales et al. and Carrarini et al., cited by Desai, Kulkarni and Verma, n.d.). Agitation in dementia is often triggered by pain, unfamiliar surroundings, overstimulation, hunger, thirst, the need to use the toilet, sleep disruption, missing hearing aids or glasses, and the way staff approach the person. The **dementia subtype** matters: patients with **Lewy body dementia and Parkinson's disease are extremely sensitive to dopamine-blocking antipsychotics** (haloperidol and droperidol are contraindicated — RCEM, 2025).

### 5. Depression and suicide in older persons

Older men have high suicide rates in many countries; risk factors include bereavement, social isolation, chronic pain and illness, disability, loss of independence, and alcohol use. Older people who attempt suicide use more lethal methods and have higher intent. Tilley et al. (2026) cite evidence linking mental disorders and cognitive impairment with suicide risk in older adults (Kułak-Bejda, Bejda and Waszkiewicz, 2021).

### 6. Sensory impairment and communication

Hearing and visual impairment cause misinterpretation, fear and apparent confusion; aphasia after stroke may be mistaken for confusion or psychosis.

### 7. Elder abuse and neglect

Elder abuse includes physical, psychological, sexual and financial abuse and neglect, and is frequently committed by family members or caregivers (Lachs and Pillemer, 2015). Behavioural change, fearfulness, unexplained injuries, poor hygiene, malnutrition, pressure sores, missing medication or the inappropriate use of sedation by caregivers may be signs. The amended DVA expressly includes **elder abuse** within the definition of domestic violence.

### 8. Environmental factors

The emergency setting itself — "bright, noisy, crowded, sleep-disrupting, and cognitively disorienting" — may trigger agitation (Desai, Kulkarni and Verma, n.d.); the ambulance and the emergency centre are such settings.

## 49.2 Formulate prehospital management techniques for a patient in a behavioural emergency in geriatric patients

### A cause-oriented, layered assessment

The Tutorial 2 memo, based on Desai, Kulkarni and Verma (n.d.), describes a layered evaluation:

1. **Reversible acute threats**: hypoxia, hypoglycaemia, shock, stroke, sepsis, severe pain.
2. **Baseline cognitive status and dementia type** (from collateral history).
3. **Precipitating stressors**: environmental overstimulation, toileting needs, sensory impairment.
4. **Iatrogenic contributors**: polypharmacy, recent dose changes, immobilisation.

**Collateral history is crucial** to establish baseline cognition, onset, fluctuation and previous similar episodes. Prehospitally, this means: primary survey; full vital signs including temperature and glucose; SpO₂ and oxygen if <92% (CDST); 12-lead ECG if possible; neurological examination including FAST for stroke; a search for injuries from falls; checking the abdomen for a distended bladder; a medication list (bring all medication to hospital); and a delirium screen (for example the CAM or 4AT) where practicable.

### Non-pharmacological management first

Desai, Kulkarni and Verma (n.d.) argue that non-pharmacological care "is often discussed as if it were a humane but secondary add-on to the real intervention", but that "the literature does not support that hierarchy" — these measures "frequently determine whether medication becomes necessary at all". Techniques include:

- **one voice**, slow and simple speech, short concrete statements, and simple choices rather than repeated commands;
- **approach from the front**, introduce yourself (repeatedly if necessary), and avoid unnecessary touching of frightened or perceptually disorganised patients;
- **do not confront delusional content**;
- **restore sensory aids** (hearing aids, glasses, dentures);
- **reduce noise and light**, dim lights where possible, turn off unnecessary alarms and sirens;
- **involve a familiar caregiver** at the bedside or in the ambulance;
- **treat comfort needs**: pain (analgesia per the CDST "Patient in pain" tool), thirst, hunger, toileting;
- **minimise restraints** and immobilising devices;
- offer simple purposeful activity (a blanket to fold, family photographs, familiar music) (Desai, Kulkarni and Verma, n.d.).

An agitated patient with an ischaemic limb or a displaced fracture "may become cooperative after analgesia without receiving any antipsychotic at all", and a hypoxaemic patient "may settle when respiratory support is improved rather than when cognition is suppressed" (Desai, Kulkarni and Verma, n.d.).

### Pharmacological management — only when danger exceeds what non-drug care can control

The key geriatric principles are summarised by Desai, Kulkarni and Verma (n.d.): **start low, avoid stacking (delay repeat dosing), match the drug to the syndrome, monitor continuously, prefer calm over deep sedation, and plan discontinuation**. The goal is "calm enough for care, not suppression for its own sake".

**CDST doses for older persons (>60 years) — note the halved doses** (HPCSA PBEC, 2025, pp. 46–47, 72):

- rapid sedation: \*midazolam **0.1 mg/kg IM** (instead of 0.2 mg/kg)\*, maximum 15 mg;
- repeat dose after 30 minutes if needed: \*midazolam **0.05 mg/kg IM**\* (instead of 0.1 mg/kg), maximum 5 mg;
- voluntary sedation options as for adults, but use the lowest effective dose.

The RCEM (2025) similarly recommends halving doses in people over 65, a "start low, go slow" strategy, and notes that the differential diagnosis in this group "is less likely to feature stimulant drugs, and more likely to feature hyperactive delirium".

**Evidence on specific drug classes in older adults:**

- **Benzodiazepines** have a limited role: they are appropriate for **alcohol or benzodiazepine withdrawal** and **seizures**, but are discouraged as routine first-line agents for undifferentiated delirium or dementia-related agitation because of respiratory depression, worsening delirium, falls and prolonged cognitive impairment; **midazolam** in particular raises "substantial safety concern" in older adults (Casey et al., cited by Desai, Kulkarni and Verma, n.d.; Saucedo Uribe et al., 2025). This is a genuine tension with the prehospital CDST, in which midazolam is the main sedative available; the resolution is to use non-drug measures first, use the reduced CDST dose, avoid stacking, and monitor closely.
- **Antipsychotics** (hospital-level agents): the Beers Criteria advise avoiding antipsychotics for behavioural problems of dementia or delirium unless non-pharmacological options have failed or are not possible and the patient threatens substantial harm. Haloperidol carries extrapyramidal and QT risks and is associated with increased mortality in older adults; low-dose olanzapine may have the most favourable short-term profile; quetiapine is preferred in Parkinson's disease and Lewy body dementia; haloperidol and droperidol are contraindicated in Parkinson's disease and Lewy body dementia (Desai, Kulkarni and Verma, n.d.; RCEM, 2025).
- **Ketamine**: although effective in younger profoundly agitated patients, ketamine "should not be imported into routine geriatric agitation protocols", because older adults "face greater cardiovascular variability, aspiration concern, and post-sedation diagnostic complexity", and age-specific evidence is lacking (Desai, Kulkarni and Verma, n.d.). It should be reserved for exceptional cases.

**Monitoring after sedation** must include airway, vital signs, cardiac rhythm (QT), extrapyramidal symptoms, sedation level, falls risk and cognition (Desai, Kulkarni and Verma, n.d.), and the CDST 10-minute observation cycle.

**Cumulative exposure**: "a single emergency dose is rarely truly single by the time the whole chain of care is examined. An ambulance crew may have given midazolam. A nursing facility may have administered quetiapine before transfer" (Desai, Kulkarni and Verma, n.d.). The ECP must ask what sedatives have already been given and must hand over clearly what was given, when, and why.

### Transport and handover

Transport to an emergency centre (delirium is a medical emergency), with a familiar caregiver where possible, and hand over the baseline cognition, onset, medication list, any sedation given, and concerns about abuse.

## 49.3 Critically appraise emergency care practice in relation to legal and ethical implications of incapacity related to behavioural emergencies in geriatric patients

### Capacity in older adults

Capacity is **decision-specific and time-specific**, and must not be assumed to be absent because of age or a diagnosis of dementia. A person with mild dementia may have capacity to refuse transport, while a person with delirium may lack it today but regain it tomorrow. The HPCSA (2023, para 9.1.1.1) requires that where patients have difficulty retaining information, communicating, or are only intermittently competent, practitioners "should provide any assistance a patient might need to enable him or her to reach and communicate a decision" — for example restoring hearing aids, using simple language, choosing a time of better orientation, and involving a familiar person.

### Who can consent when an older person lacks capacity?

South Africa does not have a single "Mental Capacity Act". The legal routes are:

1. **NHA, s 7(1)(a)–(b)**: if the user is unable to give consent, consent may be given by a person mandated **in writing** by the user to grant consent on their behalf, or authorised by law or court order; if no such person exists, consent may be given by the **spouse or partner**, or in their absence a **parent, grandparent, adult child, brother or sister**, in that order of priority.
2. **NHA, s 7(1)(e)**: in an emergency, treatment may be provided without consent where any delay might result in death or irreversible damage to health and the user has not expressly, impliedly or by conduct refused the service.
3. **Curatorship**: a court may appoint a curator to the person and/or a curator bonis (for property) for a person who is incapable of managing their own affairs.
4. **Advance directives / living wills**: not specifically legislated in South Africa, but recognised by the HPCSA as an expression of autonomy that "should be taken into consideration" (HPCSA, 2023, paras 3.3 and 9.1.4). A directive "cannot compel the healthcare team to provide treatments that lie outside acceptable standards of care". The ECP should respect a valid advance refusal of specific treatment (HPCSA, 2023, para 9.2.1), but in an emergency with uncertainty should start treatment that may be of benefit until a clearer assessment can be made (HPCSA, 2023, para 7.1).
5. **MHCA assisted care (ss 26–27)**: for a mental health care user who is incapable of making informed decisions due to mental illness or severe or profound intellectual disability but who **does not refuse** care, an application for assisted care can be made — relevant to some older persons with dementia requiring admission. If the user refuses, the involuntary pathway (ss 32–34) applies.
6. **Older Persons Act 13 of 2006**: protects older persons from abuse and neglect, requires persons who deal with older persons in a professional capacity to report suspected abuse (s 26), and provides for the protection of older persons in need of care and protection.

### Ethical issues

1. **Autonomy versus protection (beneficence)**: older persons have the right to make choices that others consider unwise, including refusing hospital admission. Overriding a competent refusal because of age is ageist and unlawful. Conversely, accepting the "refusal" of a delirious patient who cannot understand the risks is negligent.
2. **Ageism and justice**: the HPCSA (2023, para 2.5) prohibits allowing views about age or disability to prejudice care. Older patients with dementia may be undertreated (for example pain not assessed) or over-sedated.
3. **Chemical restraint as a substitute for care**: sedating an older person for convenience, or because the environment is unsuitable, violates non-maleficence and dignity; Desai, Kulkarni and Verma (n.d.) emphasise that medication "can make the room safer without making the patient better in any deep sense", and that drugs started in a crisis "have a persistent tendency to survive the situation that justified them".
4. **Best interests and family involvement**: where the patient lacks capacity and there is no advance directive, close family must be consulted and a decision made in the patient's best interests (HPCSA, 2023, para 3.4). The HPCSA also requires that cultural practices related to death and dying be respected (para 4.4).
5. **Withholding and withdrawing treatment at the end of life**: some older patients with advanced dementia or terminal illness may have advance directives or "do not resuscitate" decisions. The HPCSA Booklet 7 guides such decisions (consultation, documentation, palliative care); the CDST includes a "Palliative end-of-life care" tool (HPCSA PBEC, 2025).
6. **Elder abuse and the duty to report**: the Older Persons Act (s 26) and the amended DVA (s 2A requires functionaries to report suspected domestic violence against older persons on Form 2; s 2B requires any adult with knowledge or suspicion to report) create reporting obligations.

### Critical appraisal of current practice

- **Strengths**: the CDST explicitly halves midazolam doses for patients older than 60 years, reflecting age-specific risk; the CDST and RCEM both emphasise looking for medical causes.
- **Weaknesses**: prehospital tools for delirium screening are rarely used; providers frequently rely on the GCS, which is inappropriate for capacity (Stander, Hodkinson and Dippenaar, 2021); the available prehospital sedative (midazolam) is the agent with the greatest safety concern in older adults (Desai, Kulkarni and Verma, n.d.); there is a lack of geriatric-specific evidence (major trials and guidelines such as the 2024 ACEP policy excluded patients over 65; Desai, Kulkarni and Verma, n.d.); and handover of cumulative sedation is often incomplete.
- **Improvements**: routine collateral history, delirium screening, non-pharmacological first-line care, reduced doses with no stacking, explicit capacity assessment and documentation, involvement of family and advance directives, and vigilance for elder abuse.

### High-yield points — Outcome 49

- Presume delirium in an older person with acute confusion and inattention; hypoactive delirium is easily missed.
- Look for hypoxia, hypoglycaemia, sepsis (UTI, pneumonia), stroke, subdural haematoma, pain, urinary retention, constipation, medications.
- Non-drug measures first: one voice, front approach, sensory aids, family, comfort.
- CDST: midazolam **0.1 mg/kg IM if >60 years**, repeat **0.05 mg/kg**.
- Avoid haloperidol/droperidol in Parkinson's/Lewy body dementia; benzodiazepines mainly for withdrawal and seizures; ketamine not routine in older adults.
- Consent for incapacitated adults: NHA s 7(1)(b) hierarchy — spouse/partner, parent, grandparent, adult child, sibling; emergency s 7(1)(e).
- Report elder abuse (Older Persons Act s 26; DVA ss 2A/2B).
