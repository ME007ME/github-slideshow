# SECTION H — TOXICOLOGY

*Note on order.* This section covers the general principles first (Outcomes 58, 59, 61 and 62), because you need them to understand the specific poisonings in Outcome 60. Outcomes 60 and 63–65 follow.

## Key definitions

- **Poison (toxin/toxicant)**: any substance that causes harm to a living organism through its chemical action when taken in sufficient quantity. Kaswa (2024) defines **poisoning** as "the deliberate or unintentional injury or impairment of a living organism by a substance through its chemical action". Paracelsus' principle, "the dose makes the poison", reminds us that almost any substance can be toxic at a high enough dose.
- **Toxic exposure**: contact with a substance (unintentional). **Overdose**: intentional or accidental exposure to an excessive amount; an overdose "does not typically result in poisoning unless it triggers symptoms" (Kaswa, 2024).
- **Toxidrome**: a constellation of signs and symptoms characteristic of a class of toxins (62.3).
- **Antidote**: a substance that counteracts the effect of a poison.
- **Venom / envenomation**: a toxin injected by an animal through a bite or sting.

# Outcome 58. Comprehensively discuss the routes by which poisons may enter the body

## 58.1 Comprehensively discuss the routes by which poisons may enter the body under the following headings

The route of entry determines the speed of onset, the organs affected first, the decontamination required, and the precautions needed to protect the crew.

### 58.1.1 Ingestion

**Ingestion** (swallowing) is the most common route of poisoning, accounting for the majority of both accidental poisonings in children and deliberate self-poisoning in adults (Tintinalli et al., 2020).

- **Examples**: medication overdoses (paracetamol, tricyclic antidepressants, ARVs, anti-epileptics), household products (paraffin, bleach, detergents, drain cleaners), pesticides (organophosphates, carbamates such as aldicarb "Two-step", rat poisons), plants and mushrooms, traditional medicines, contaminated food (scombroid, botulism), alcohol and methanol, and drug packets (body packing and stuffing).
- **Absorption**: mainly in the small intestine (large surface area); weak acids are partly absorbed in the stomach. Onset is usually 30 minutes to two hours, but may be **delayed** by sustained-release or enteric-coated formulations, anticholinergic drugs (which slow gastric emptying), pharmacobezoars (tablet masses, for example iron, salicylates), or food. After absorption, many drugs undergo **first-pass metabolism** in the liver, which can create toxic metabolites (for example the paracetamol metabolite NAPQI, or the toxic metabolites of methanol and ethylene glycol).
- **Local effects**: caustics and corrosives cause burns of the lips, mouth, oropharynx, oesophagus and stomach; hydrocarbons cause aspiration pneumonitis if vomited or aspirated.
- **Prehospital implications**: establish what, how much, when, and the formulation; bring containers; consider activated charcoal only within the strict CDST criteria (62.2); do not induce vomiting (Outcome 61); assess the airway for burns and swelling after caustic ingestion.

### 58.1.2 Injection

**Injection** introduces a toxin directly through the skin into subcutaneous tissue, muscle or the bloodstream.

- **Examples**: intravenous drug use (heroin, "nyaope", methamphetamine, cocaine); iatrogenic drug errors and overdoses (for example insulin, opioids, potassium, local anaesthetics); deliberate injection of insulin or other drugs; and **envenomation** — snakebites, scorpion stings, spider bites, bee and wasp stings, and marine stings (Outcome 64).
- **Absorption**: intravenous injection produces the **most rapid onset** (seconds), with no opportunity for decontamination; intramuscular and subcutaneous routes are slower and depend on perfusion.
- **Associated risks**: local tissue damage and necrosis (cytotoxic venoms), infection (abscesses, cellulitis, endocarditis), and blood-borne viruses (HIV, hepatitis B and C) — **needle-stick risk for the crew**.
- **Prehospital implications**: look for track marks and bite or sting marks (the CDST advises checking for "bite/sting/injection marks"; HPCSA PBEC, 2025, p. 55); remove constrictive clothing and jewellery from a bitten limb (HPCSA PBEC, 2025, p. 54); immobilise the limb; dispose of sharps safely; treat the systemic effects.

### 58.1.3 Absorption (dermal and mucous membrane)

**Absorption** refers to the entry of a toxin through intact or damaged **skin**, **eyes** or **mucous membranes**.

- **Examples**: **organophosphate and carbamate pesticides** (highly lipid-soluble, readily absorbed through intact skin — a major occupational hazard for farm workers and a risk to EMS crews); hydrofluoric acid (penetrates deeply, causing hypocalcaemia); phenol; solvents; nicotine (green tobacco sickness); fentanyl patches; corrosive acids and alkalis (local burns); and chemical warfare agents (nerve agents, vesicants).
- **Factors increasing absorption**: lipid solubility, high concentration, a large surface area, prolonged contact, damaged skin (burns, abrasions), hydrated or thin skin (children and infants, who also have a larger surface area relative to body weight), and occlusion (contaminated clothing).
- **Eye exposure**: alkalis cause liquefactive necrosis and penetrate deeply, causing severe corneal damage and blindness; acids cause coagulative necrosis.
- **Prehospital implications** (CDST): "If poison on patient's skin: protect yourself: wear gloves, apron, eye protection and mask. **Remove clothes and wash skin with soap and water.** If poison in eye/s: **immediately flush under running water or normal saline for at least 20 min**" (HPCSA PBEC, 2025, p. 54). Removal of clothing alone removes a large proportion of contamination. Bag contaminated clothing. For chemical burns, the CDST Burns tool advises cooling with running water for at least one hour (HPCSA PBEC, 2025, p. 34). Contaminated patients must be decontaminated **before** entering the ambulance to prevent secondary contamination of the crew (Kaswa, 2024, notes that health care workers handling patients exposed to organophosphates or nerve gases "must use appropriate protective equipment").

### 58.1.4 Inhalation

**Inhalation** brings toxins into the respiratory tract, where the large alveolar surface area (about 70 m²) and rich blood supply allow **very rapid absorption** — almost as fast as intravenous injection.

- **Examples**:
  - **asphyxiant gases**: simple asphyxiants that displace oxygen (carbon dioxide, methane, nitrogen); and **chemical asphyxiants** — **carbon monoxide** (from fires, charcoal braziers ("imbawulas"), paraffin heaters, generators and vehicle exhausts in enclosed spaces) and **hydrogen cyanide** (from burning plastics, wool and silk in house fires, and from industrial sources) (60.9, 60.11);
  - **irritant gases**: highly water-soluble gases (ammonia, chlorine at high concentration) that injure the upper airway, and less soluble gases (phosgene, nitrogen dioxide) that cause delayed pulmonary oedema; **chlorine** (swimming pool chemicals, mixing bleach with acids) (60.10);
  - **volatile substances and solvents** ("glue sniffing", petrol, paint thinners, butane), which cause CNS depression and "sudden sniffing death" from arrhythmias;
  - **smoke** (thermal and chemical injury);
  - **drugs** (smoked methamphetamine, crack cocaine, cannabis, heroin; inhaled loxapine therapeutically);
  - **hydrocarbon aspiration** (paraffin, petrol) — a form of inhalation injury following ingestion (60.16, 60.30);
  - **riot-control agents and chemical warfare agents** (60.32).
- **Prehospital implications**: **scene safety is paramount** — never enter an enclosed space with a suspected toxic atmosphere without appropriate breathing apparatus (fire and rescue services); remove the patient to fresh air; give high-flow oxygen (the CDST Burns tool gives 100% oxygen by non-rebreather mask for suspected carbon monoxide or cyanide poisoning or a fire in an enclosed space; HPCSA PBEC, 2025, p. 34); look for signs of inhalation injury (facial burns, singed nasal hairs, soot, hoarseness, stridor); note that pulse oximetry is **falsely normal** in carbon monoxide poisoning; and anticipate delayed pulmonary oedema with some gases. The CDST Poisoning tool advises checking the scene for "fires in poorly ventilated room (cyanide/carbon monoxide poisoning)" (HPCSA PBEC, 2025, p. 54).

### Comparison of routes

| Route | Speed of onset | Common examples | Key prehospital action |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ingestion | Minutes to hours (may be delayed) | Medications, paraffin, pesticides, plants | History, containers, consider charcoal (CDST criteria), do not induce vomiting |
| Injection | Seconds (IV) to minutes (IM/SC) | IV drugs, insulin, envenomation | Look for marks, immobilise bitten limb, treat systemic effects, sharps safety |
| Absorption | Minutes to hours | Organophosphates, HF acid, solvents, corrosives | PPE, remove clothing, wash skin with soap and water, flush eyes ≥20 min |
| Inhalation | Seconds to minutes | CO, cyanide, chlorine, solvents, smoke | Scene safety, fresh air, high-flow O₂, airway assessment |

### High-yield points — Outcome 58

- Four routes: ingestion (most common), injection (fastest IV), absorption (skin/eyes), inhalation (very rapid).
- CDST: PPE; remove clothes and wash skin with soap and water; flush eyes ≥20 minutes.
- Organophosphates are absorbed through intact skin — protect the crew.
- Pulse oximetry is falsely normal in CO poisoning.

# Outcome 59. Discuss the epidemiology of poisoning

## Global and South African overview

According to the Global Burden of Disease study, in 2019 unintentional poisoning accounted for 0.14% of all global deaths and self-harm for 1.34%, and the WHO regards poisoning as a significant public health problem in low- and middle-income countries (Kaswa, 2024). Poisonings account for 3–10% of ICU admissions in high-income settings, with a mortality of 2.6–4.5%; about half involve a single substance and half mixed ingestions; pharmaceuticals make up about 67% of toxicants, with psychotropics (~49%) and analgesics (~23%) predominating (Hüser et al., 2025). Kaswa (2024) notes that South African poisoning patterns "mirror the international trends", but that the true number of poisonings is difficult to ascertain, and that chronic environmental exposures are not reported. Health care providers should report notifiable poisonings, which in South Africa include **lead, mercury, and agricultural or stock remedy (pesticide) poisoning**, as notifiable medical conditions (Kaswa, 2024, citing the National Institute for Communicable Diseases).

Two South African studies provide important data. An analysis of 4,771 human exposure consultations to the **Tygerberg Poison Information Centre** found that accidental exposures were more common than intentional poisonings (65.2% versus 34.8%); that 55.8% of cases involved adults, of whom 57.6% were female; that **61.4% of adult cases were intentional**, and 64.3% of these were female; and that **98.8% of exposures in children were accidental**, with a male predominance (59.7%) (Veale, Wium and Müller, 2013). Across all ages, **non-drug chemicals accounted for 52.7%** of exposures, **medicines 35.2%** and **biological toxins (bites, stings, plants) 12.6%**. Among non-drug chemicals, **pesticides (34.8%)**, irritant/corrosive substances (27.7%) and volatile hydrocarbons (8.3%) were most common, and the most commonly ingested were **cholinesterase inhibitors (organophosphates and carbamates, 8.8%)**, anticoagulant rodenticides (7.1%) and pyrethroids (5.0%); **aldicarb** and amitraz were identified as toxicovigilance targets. Among medicines, analgesics were the most common class (26.1%), and **paracetamol (15.8%)**, benzodiazepines (9.2%) and antihistamines (5.2%) were the most common individual agents (Veale, Wium and Müller, 2013). An analysis of 2,872 childhood poisoning incidents at the **Red Cross War Memorial Children's Hospital** in Cape Town found that **paraffin (kerosene) was the commonest agent (24%)**, and concluded that "paraffin and drugs remain the principal agents responsible for paediatric exposures and poisonings, with increasing incidents due to household cleaning products and pesticides" (Balme et al., 2012). In the prehospital setting, **overdose or deliberate self-poisoning made up 52%** of mental-health-related EMS incidents in the Garden Route District, and women more often used poisoning or overdose as a method of suicide (Tilley et al., 2026).

**Age patterns**:

- **Children** (particularly toddlers aged one to five years) — mainly **accidental (unintentional)** ingestions of household products, paraffin and medications found in the home, usually small amounts, with a low mortality but important exceptions (paraffin aspiration, iron, TCAs, opioids, oral hypoglycaemics, organophosphates and traditional medicines). Tilley et al. (2026) excluded accidental poisonings of children under eight years from their mental-health dataset because they are not deliberate.
- **Adolescents and adults** — mainly **intentional (deliberate self-poisoning)**, often with multiple agents and alcohol, and higher severity.
- **Older adults** — medication errors, polypharmacy, chronic toxicity (lithium, digoxin), and deliberate overdose with high lethality.

## 59.1 Discuss epidemiology of poisoning in adults and children under the following headings

### 59.1.1 Environmental toxins

Environmental toxins are poisons present in the air, water, soil, food or dwelling environment.

- **Carbon monoxide** — a leading cause of poisoning deaths, from indoor coal and wood braziers ("imbawulas"), paraffin heaters and stoves, generators, and fires in informal settlements (where shack fires are common). Exposures peak in winter, and often affect whole families, including children.
- **Smoke inhalation** in house and shack fires (carbon monoxide and cyanide).
- **Lead** — from old lead-based paint, informal battery recycling, mining, contaminated soil and some traditional remedies and cosmetics; children are most vulnerable (neurodevelopmental effects).
- **Mercury** — from artisanal and small-scale gold mining and some skin-lightening creams.
- **Arsenic and other heavy metals** in mining areas and contaminated groundwater.
- **Air pollution** from coal-fired power stations and domestic fuel burning (the Mpumalanga Highveld has some of the highest air pollution levels in the world).
- **Naturally occurring toxins**: poisonous plants and mushrooms (Outcome 63), envenomation by snakes, scorpions and spiders (Outcome 64), and marine toxins (64.4, Outcome 65).
- **Contaminated water and food** (for example cyanobacterial toxins in water, and food-borne toxins such as scombroid).

### 59.1.2 Industrial and agricultural toxins

- **Pesticides** are a major cause of serious poisoning and death in South Africa, both occupational (farm workers, through skin absorption and inhalation) and intentional (self-poisoning in rural areas). The most important are **organophosphates** and **carbamates**. Illegally decanted agricultural pesticides sold by street vendors as rat poison in townships, especially **aldicarb ("Two-step")** — a highly toxic carbamate — are an important cause of poisoning in children and adults (Rother, 2010; Davies, Hlela and Rother, 2023, cited by Kaswa, 2024). Other important agricultural chemicals include paraquat, rodenticides (anticoagulants, strychnine) and fumigants (aluminium phosphide).
- **Industrial chemicals**: chlorine and ammonia (water treatment, refrigeration), hydrogen sulphide (sewers, mining), **cyanide** (gold extraction in mining, electroplating), **hydrofluoric acid** (glass etching, rust removal, petroleum industry), solvents, acids and alkalis, and heavy metals.
- **Hazardous materials incidents**: transport accidents (tanker spills on national roads), industrial explosions and leaks.
- **Occupational exposures** affect mainly working-age adults; children are exposed through take-home contamination, storage of chemicals in food or drink containers, and living near farms.

### 59.1.3 Accidental poisoning

- **Children** account for most accidental poisonings, peaking between one and five years of age, when children are mobile, curious and explore by putting objects in their mouths.
- **Paraffin (kerosene)** ingestion is one of the most common childhood poisonings in South Africa, particularly in low-income households where paraffin is used for cooking, heating and lighting and is often stored in cold-drink bottles (Balme et al., 2012). The main danger is aspiration pneumonitis.
- Other common accidental exposures: medications (adult medications left within reach, including iron tablets, ARVs, TCAs, opioids, antihypertensives and oral hypoglycaemics), household cleaning products (bleach, detergents, dishwasher products), caustic drain cleaners, and pesticides.
- **Adults**: medication errors (especially in older adults with polypharmacy), therapeutic misadventures, occupational exposures, and food poisoning; carbon monoxide.
- **Risk factors**: poor storage, lack of child-resistant packaging, decanting of chemicals into unlabelled containers, overcrowding, poverty and lack of supervision.

### 59.1.4 Medication overdose

- **Medicines account for about a third (35.2%) of poisoning exposures** reported to the Tygerberg Poison Information Centre, and most adult exposures are intentional (Veale, Wium and Müller, 2013); internationally, pharmaceuticals make up about two-thirds of toxicants in ICU poisoning admissions (Hüser et al., 2025).
- Commonly implicated medications in South Africa include **paracetamol** (the single most common medicine exposure, 15.8%, according to Veale, Wium and Müller, 2013, and widely available over the counter), **benzodiazepines** (9.2%), **antihistamines** (5.2%), **NSAIDs**, **antiretrovirals** (particularly efavirenz), **antidepressants** (including TCAs such as amitriptyline, which is commonly prescribed for pain and depression and is highly toxic in overdose), **anti-epileptics** (carbamazepine, valproate, phenobarbital), **antipsychotics**, **antihypertensives** (beta-blockers, calcium channel blockers), **oral hypoglycaemics** and **isoniazid** (TB treatment — causes refractory seizures in overdose).
- Overdose is more common in **females** for attempts, whereas lethality is often higher in males and older persons (Tilley et al., 2026).
- **Mixed ingestions** are common, frequently with alcohol (Hüser et al., 2025).
- Medication overdose in older adults is often accidental (confusion, polypharmacy) or chronic.

### 59.1.5 Household chemical overdose

Household products include bleach (sodium hypochlorite), detergents and dishwasher products, drain and oven cleaners (strong alkalis), toilet cleaners (acids), paraffin, methylated spirits, petrol, antifreeze (ethylene glycol), button batteries, cosmetics, mothballs and pesticides stored at home.

- **Children**: most household exposures are accidental and minor, but caustic ingestions (drain cleaners), paraffin aspiration, button-battery ingestion (which can cause oesophageal perforation within hours), and ethylene glycol and methanol are dangerous.
- **Adults**: household chemicals (bleach, paraffin, pesticides, caustics) are used in deliberate self-poisoning, particularly in low-income settings where medications are less accessible.
- **Dangerous mixtures**: mixing bleach with acids releases chlorine gas, and with ammonia releases chloramine gas.

### 59.1.6 Substance abuse

- **Alcohol** is the most commonly abused substance in South Africa and is involved in a large proportion of injuries, violence, road deaths and co-ingestions.
- **Illicit drugs**: cannabis, methamphetamine ("tik", especially in the Western Cape), methaqualone ("Mandrax"), heroin and heroin-based mixtures ("nyaope/whoonga"), cocaine, MDMA and hallucinogens; prescription opioid (codeine, tramadol) and benzodiazepine misuse; and inhalant use (glue, petrol, thinners) among street children and adolescents (Mesthrie, 2025, notes the large population of homeless children).
- Tilley et al. (2026) cite an estimate that 18.9% of South Africans abuse alcohol and/or drugs (Myers et al., 2022).
- Substance abuse causes acute poisoning, behavioural emergencies (Outcome 52.8), trauma, and withdrawal emergencies; adulterants (for example fentanyl, levamisole in cocaine, ARVs in "nyaope") add unpredictable toxicity.
- **Children** are exposed through accidental ingestion of drugs found at home (for example cannabis edibles, methadone) and, in adolescence, through experimentation.

### High-yield points — Outcome 59

- Children: accidental, age 1–5, paraffin and pharmaceuticals; adults: intentional, pharmaceuticals, mixed ingestions with alcohol.
- Pesticides (organophosphates, aldicarb "Two-step") are a major cause of serious poisoning and death in South Africa.
- CO from braziers and paraffin heaters in winter; shack fires.
- Notifiable poisonings: lead, mercury, agricultural/stock remedy poisoning.
- Tygerberg PIC data: non-drug chemicals 52.7% (pesticides most common), medicines 35.2% (paracetamol most common), biological toxins 12.6%; 61.4% of adult exposures intentional; 98.8% of child exposures accidental (Veale, Wium and Müller, 2013).
- Red Cross Children's Hospital: paraffin the commonest agent (24%) (Balme et al., 2012).

# Outcome 61. Discuss the role of emesis in the management of ingested poisons

## Background

Induced emesis (vomiting), traditionally with **syrup of ipecac** (from *Cephaelis ipecacuanha*, containing emetine and cephaeline, which irritate the gastric mucosa and stimulate the chemoreceptor trigger zone), was once widely recommended for home and prehospital management of ingested poisons. Other methods (salt water, mustard water, fingers in the throat) are dangerous and ineffective; salt water can cause fatal hypernatraemia.

## Current evidence and recommendations

The joint position paper of the American Academy of Clinical Toxicology (AACT) and the European Association of Poisons Centres and Clinical Toxicologists (EAPCCT) concluded that **ipecac syrup should not be administered routinely** in the management of poisoned patients, because:

1. there is **no evidence from clinical studies that ipecac improves the outcome** of poisoned patients;
2. the amount of toxin removed is **variable and diminishes rapidly with time** (most experimental studies show limited removal even when given within an hour);
3. vomiting may continue for an hour or more, **delaying the administration of activated charcoal, oral antidotes** (for example oral N-acetylcysteine) and whole bowel irrigation;
4. it causes **complications**: aspiration pneumonia, Mallory–Weiss tears, oesophageal and gastric rupture, diarrhoea, lethargy (which can confuse the clinical picture), and, with chronic abuse (in bulimia), cardiomyopathy (Höjer et al., 2013).

The CDST is explicit: "**Avoid milk/inducing vomiting**" (HPCSA PBEC, 2025, p. 54). Ipecac is not stocked by South African EMS services. Kaswa (2024) and Hüser et al. (2025) do not include emesis among decontamination strategies; gastric decontamination is limited to activated charcoal and whole bowel irrigation, with gastric lavage reserved for exceptional cases.

## 61.1 Recognise the instances and situations in which emesis can be effectively used as a therapy

### Contraindications to induced emesis

Emesis must **never** be induced in a patient with:

- a **reduced level of consciousness** or the likelihood of a rapidly declining level of consciousness, or an unprotected airway (risk of aspiration);
- ingestion of a substance likely to cause **seizures** or **rapid CNS depression** (for example TCAs, isoniazid, camphor, strychnine, opioids, benzodiazepines, calcium channel blockers, beta-blockers);
- ingestion of **corrosives** (acids or alkalis) — re-exposure of the oesophagus causes further burns and risk of perforation;
- ingestion of **hydrocarbons** (paraffin, petrol, turpentine) — vomiting greatly increases the risk of aspiration pneumonitis;
- ingestion of **sharp objects** or button batteries;
- **haemorrhagic diathesis** or recent GI surgery;
- **infants younger than six months**, frail older persons, and patients with significant medical illness;
- prior vomiting.

### Situations in which emesis might theoretically be considered

The AACT/EAPCCT position paper states that ipecac might be considered **only in rare circumstances**, when **all** of the following apply (Höjer et al., 2013):

1. there are **no contraindications** to its use;
2. there is a **substantial risk of serious toxicity** to the patient;
3. there is **no alternative therapy available or effective** to decrease gastrointestinal absorption (for example activated charcoal is unavailable, or the toxin is not bound by charcoal, such as iron or lithium);
4. there will be a **delay of more than one hour before the patient arrives at an emergency medical facility** (for example in a very remote rural area); and
5. ipecac can be administered **within 30–90 minutes** of the ingestion.

In South African practice, these conditions are almost never all met, ipecac is not available to EMS, and the CDST directs that vomiting not be induced. **The correct examination answer is therefore that induced emesis no longer has a role in routine emergency care**, and that the rare theoretical exception (an alert patient, in a remote area more than an hour from care, with a potentially life-threatening ingestion of a non-corrosive, non-hydrocarbon substance that is not adsorbed by charcoal and does not cause rapid CNS depression or seizures, within 30–90 minutes, where no alternative exists) is of historical rather than practical relevance. Spontaneous vomiting should be managed by protecting the airway (lateral position, suction) and preventing aspiration.

### High-yield points — Outcome 61

- Ipecac/induced emesis: no proven benefit, delays charcoal and antidotes, causes aspiration — not recommended (Höjer et al., 2013).
- CDST: avoid milk/inducing vomiting.
- Contraindications: reduced consciousness, seizure risk, corrosives, hydrocarbons, sharp objects, infants <6 months.
- Theoretical use only if all five conditions are met — virtually never in practice.

# Outcome 62. Comprehensively discuss the general management principles of poisoning in the emergency care setting

## The general approach (CDST and literature)

Kaswa (2024) summarises the approach to poisoning with the mnemonic **ABCDEFG**: **A**irway, **B**reathing, **C**irculation, **D**econtamination (with PPE to protect health care workers), **E**nhance elimination, **F**ocus therapy (antidotes and specific therapy), and **G**et help from the poison control centre. Hüser et al. (2025) emphasise that "the cornerstone of treatment is rapid stabilization of vital functions — often before the causative pharmaceutical is identified", using the ABCDE approach, and that "while most patients recover with supportive care alone, a significant proportion require targeted interventions and advanced organ support". Kaswa (2024) stresses: "treat the patient first, not the laboratory results".

The CDST Poisoning tool (HPCSA PBEC, 2025, pp. 54–57) is the South African prehospital standard and is summarised below.

### CDST Poisoning tool — step by step (adult and child)

**"Please ensure the scene and own safety are prioritised."**

**1. Primary survey and stabilisation.** If convulsing, use the Seizures tool. Attach monitors and check SpO₂, HR, BP, RR, GCS, pupils and capillary refill.

- Give oxygen if SpO₂ <92%, RR >30, RR <9 or GCS ≤14.
- Suction airway secretions; if the patient is unable to maintain the airway, use the Airway tool.
- If breathing is inadequate, assist ventilation with a BVM connected to oxygen (one breath every three to five seconds).

**2. Prevent ongoing exposure.**

- Skin: protect yourself (gloves, apron, eye protection, mask); remove clothes and wash skin with soap and water.
- Eyes: flush under running water or normal saline for at least 20 minutes.
- **Activated charcoal**: "Only if fully conscious and oral overdose of harmful substance in past 1 hour: give activated charcoal **1 g/kg** mixed with water. Avoid if ingested household products, paraffin, petrol, corrosive poisons (acids/bases), iron, lithium or alcohol. Avoid milk/inducing vomiting." (Adult dose in the drug appendix: 50–100 g; child dose by weight from the formulary.)
- Bites and stings: expose the site and cover with a sterile dressing; remove constrictive clothing and jewellery.

**3. IV access and circulation.**

- Adult: if SBP <90, give 250–500 mL crystalloid IV over 5–10 minutes and reassess; repeat if SBP is still <90; stop IV fluids if the patient becomes breathless or develops new crackles.
- Child: if two or more signs of shock (altered mental status, fast respiratory rate, fast pulse, weak peripheral pulses, delayed capillary refill >2 seconds, or low BP as a late sign), give crystalloid 10 mL/kg IV over 10–30 minutes.

**4. Glucose.** If <3.5 mmol/L: smear 25 g oral glucose inside the cheek/gum, or give dextrose 50% 20 mL IV (child: dextrose 10% 3 mL/kg IV); if no IV access, glucagon 1 mg IM (child 0.5 mg IM); recheck after 10 minutes and repeat if still <3.5 mmol/L.

**5. History** from family and friends: time of exposure, amount and type of substance; ask about **all** drugs and toxins that may have been accessible — prescribed, over-the-counter, herbal, chemicals, pesticides, **traditional medications**, recreational substances; ask to see tablets, packets and containers. **Check the scene** for pill bottles, a suicide note, and fires in poorly ventilated rooms (cyanide/carbon monoxide). Check for bite, sting and injection marks.

**6. Identify the poison.** If the substance is known, give specific management according to its class (Specific poisoning pages). If unknown, note unusual smells, look for sweating, drooling and secretions, check pupils and temperature, and identify the toxidrome (62.3).

**7. Get advice.** "Wherever possible, contact **Poisons Information Helpline of the Western Cape (0861 555 777)** for specific management recommendations."

**8. Determine toxicity and disposition.** Calculate the dose ingested in mg/kg (total mg ÷ weight) and check the package insert or seek advice to determine whether the dose is toxic.

- If a toxic dose, if the patient is symptomatic with no response to treatment, or if the poisoning is due to an unknown substance: **transport to the closest emergency centre**, taking remaining tablets, packets and containers if safe to do so.
- If a non-toxic dose and the patient is well with no signs or symptoms: advise the patient to remain at home and monitor for changes. *(Note: this does not apply to deliberate self-poisoning, where the suicide risk itself requires transport — see Outcome 46 and the CDST Aggression tool obligation to transport.)*

**9. Monitoring during transport.** Monitor BP, HR, RR, SpO₂ and GCS every 10 minutes; recheck glucose if it was initially low; reassess airway, breathing, circulation and level of consciousness if BP <90/60, HR >100 or <50, RR >20 or <9, SpO₂ <92% or GCS falls; reassess for further fluids if shock was present.

### Specific poisoning management in the CDST (summary)

| Poison class | CDST adult management | CDST child management |
|---|---|---|
| **Opioid** | If respiratory depression: \*naloxone 0.4 mg IV/IM\* (0.04 mg in a known opioid abuser); reassess every 2 min; increasing doses 0.8 mg, 2 mg, 4 mg every 2 min to a maximum total of 10 mg; if RR remains low, consider other poisoning; naloxone wears off quickly — monitor and repeat | \*Naloxone 0.1 mg/kg IV/IM (up to 2 mg)\*, repeat every 2 min to a maximum cumulative 10 mg |
| **Cholinergic (organophosphate)** | ^Atropine 2 mg IV^; look for response after 5 min (clear lung sounds, dilated pupils, increased HR, dry mouth); if no response, ^double the previous dose every 3 min until improvement^; when responding, ^give 20% of the total bolus dose per hour by infusion^; intubate if unable to maintain the airway or toxicity persists; **avoid succinylcholine** for RSI | ^Atropine 0.05 mg/kg IV^, doubling every 3 min until response, then 20% of the total per hour |
| **Tricyclic antidepressant** | ECG; if QRS widened, ventricular arrhythmia, seizures or hypotension not responsive to fluids: ^sodium bicarbonate 8.4% 100 mL IV over 3 min^; reassess after 10 min; repeat if toxicity persists, then seek advice; if intubated, ^hyperventilate with 100% oxygen at 20–24 breaths/min^, aiming for ETCO₂ 20–25 mmHg | ^Sodium bicarbonate 8.4% 2 mL/kg IV over 3 min^; repeat once if needed |
| **Beta-blocker / calcium channel blocker** | Bradycardia → RCSA bradycardia algorithm; hypotension → 250–500 mL crystalloid; if still hypotensive → ^calcium chloride 10% 10 mL IV over 10 min^ or ^calcium gluconate 10% 30 mL IV over 10 min^ under ECG monitoring; if still hypotensive → ^adrenaline infusion^ (1 mg in 50 mL or 4 mg in 200 mL normal saline; start 5 mL/h = 1.6 mcg/min; target 2–10 mcg/min; titrate to SBP 90/MAP 65) | Fluids 10 mL/kg; ^calcium chloride 10% 0.2 mL/kg or calcium gluconate 10% 0.6 mL/kg at 1 mL/min^; if beta-blocker more likely: \*glucagon 100 mcg/kg IV over 1–2 min (max 1 mg/min)\*, repeat after 10 min; ^adrenaline infusion^ |
| **Sedative-hypnotic, anticholinergic, sympathomimetic** | If temperature ≥38 °C: remove clothing, water spray, fan or air-conditioning; if aggressive or agitated → Aggression tool; airway → Airway tool; stimulant chest pain: aspirin 300 mg PO, ^diazepam 5 mg or midazolam 1 mg IV slowly^, ^isosorbide dinitrate 5 mg SL or GTN 400 mcg SL^, exclude cardiac cause; widened QRS → ^sodium bicarbonate 8.4%^ | Cooling; Aggression tool; Airway tool; widened QRS (anticholinergic) → ^sodium bicarbonate 8.4%^ |

## 62.1 Know how to obtain advice regarding poisons

**Sources of advice in South Africa:**

1. **Poisons Information Helpline of the Western Cape — 0861 555 777** (24 hours, national), operated by the Tygerberg Poison Information Centre (Stellenbosch University) and the Red Cross War Memorial Children's Hospital Poisons Information Centre (University of Cape Town). This is the number specified in the CDST (HPCSA PBEC, 2025, p. 55).
2. **Afritox** — the South African poisons information database for health professionals, maintained by the poison centres.
3. **Senior medical advice / on-line medical control** — the CDST repeatedly directs the provider to "consult senior medical advisor/Poisons Information Helpline for further guidance" (HPCSA PBEC, 2025, p. 56).
4. **The receiving emergency centre** (pre-alert).
5. **Product information**: package inserts, product labels, and **Safety Data Sheets (SDS)** for industrial and agricultural chemicals; the CDST advises checking the package insert to determine whether a dose is toxic (HPCSA PBEC, 2025, p. 55).
6. **Hazardous materials resources**: the UN number and hazard placards on vehicles, the Emergency Response Guidebook, and fire and rescue HAZMAT teams.
7. **For snakebite**: the poison centres and recognised South African snakebite experts and resources (for example the African Snakebite Institute).

**Information to have ready when calling**: the patient's age, weight and sex; the substance(s) (name, strength, formulation — take the container); the amount; the time and route of exposure; whether intentional or accidental; symptoms and vital signs; treatment already given; and relevant medical history.

Hüser et al. (2025) note that "poison information centers provide 24/7 access to expert guidance on toxicant identification, risk assessment, and individualized treatment strategies. Early consultation — particularly in unclear or complex cases — can enhance clinical decision-making, avoid unnecessary interventions and improve patient safety."

## 62.2 Understand and critically appraise the use of activated charcoal

### What activated charcoal is and how it works

Activated charcoal is a fine, black, odourless powder produced by heating organic material (for example wood, coconut shells) and then "activating" it with steam or chemicals to create an extremely porous structure with a very large surface area (about 950–2,000 m² per gram). Toxins are **adsorbed** (bound to its surface) by weak intermolecular forces, preventing their absorption from the gut (Tintinalli et al., 2020).

Activated charcoal works in two ways:

1. **Single-dose activated charcoal (SDAC)** — prevents absorption of a toxin still in the gut.
2. **Multiple-dose activated charcoal (MDAC)** — enhances elimination of drugs already absorbed by interrupting **enterohepatic recirculation** and by **"gastrointestinal dialysis"** (drug diffuses from the blood back into the gut lumen and is bound by charcoal) (Kaswa, 2024; Hüser et al., 2025).

### Dosing

- **CDST**: 1 g/kg orally mixed with water (adult 50–100 g; child by weight band, for example 3.5–7 kg 5 g, 7–11 kg 10 g, 11–17 kg 15 g, 17.5–35 kg 25 g, 35–55 kg 50 g, ≥55 kg 100 g) (HPCSA PBEC, 2025, pp. 54, 68, 74).
- **Hüser et al. (2025)**: SDAC commonly 50 g in adults, aiming for a charcoal-to-drug ratio of about 10:1 (higher ratios up to 40:1 may be warranted); MDAC — an initial dose followed by 12.5 g every one, two or four hours in adults.
- **Kaswa (2024)**: 0.5–1 g/kg in children and 25–100 g in adults.
- Avoid **sorbitol-containing** preparations, particularly in children (a cathartic that causes diarrhoea and dehydration; Kaswa, 2024, describes it as "emetic").

### Indications (CDST criteria — all must be met)

1. The patient is **fully conscious** (and able to protect the airway).
2. The overdose was **oral**.
3. The substance is **harmful** (a potentially toxic dose).
4. It was taken **within the past one hour**.
5. The substance is **adsorbed by charcoal** (that is, not one of the exclusions).

### Contraindications and substances not adsorbed

- **Reduced level of consciousness or unprotected airway** (unless the patient is intubated, in which case charcoal can be given via a nasogastric tube in hospital) — the main risk is **aspiration**, which can cause severe pneumonitis.
- **Substances poorly adsorbed**: the CDST lists **household products, paraffin, petrol (hydrocarbons), corrosives (acids and alkalis), iron, lithium and alcohol** (HPCSA PBEC, 2025, p. 54). Other non-adsorbed substances include other metals and ions (potassium, lead, mercury, arsenic), cyanide (poorly), methanol and ethylene glycol, and mineral acids. A useful mnemonic for poorly adsorbed substances is "**PHAILS**": **P**esticides (some) and **P**otassium, **H**ydrocarbons and **H**eavy metals, **A**cids, alkalis and **A**lcohols, **I**ron, **L**ithium, **S**olvents.
- **Corrosives**: charcoal obscures endoscopic visualisation and does not bind the agent.
- **Hydrocarbons**: increased aspiration risk with vomiting.
- **Bowel obstruction, perforation or ileus**, and patients at risk of GI haemorrhage or perforation.
- **Patients who will need endoscopy.**
- A **non-toxic ingestion** (no benefit, only risk).
- **Uncooperative patients** — forcing charcoal (for example via nasogastric tube in an unprotected airway) is dangerous.

### Indications for MDAC (hospital)

MDAC should be considered for massive ingestions or for drugs with enterohepatic or entero-enteric recirculation, such as **carbamazepine, phenobarbital, dapsone, quinine and theophylline** (Hüser et al., 2025), and also for sustained-release preparations and some others (for example amatoxin mushrooms, 63.1).

### Complications

Aspiration pneumonitis (potentially fatal), vomiting (common, especially when given quickly or with sorbitol), constipation and bowel obstruction (especially MDAC), corneal abrasion if spilled in the eyes, black stools, and interference with oral antidotes (for example oral N-acetylcysteine).

### Critical appraisal

**Arguments for activated charcoal:**

- Volunteer studies show that charcoal reduces drug absorption, by about 50% or more if given within the first hour, with the effect declining with time.
- It is cheap, widely available, and relatively safe in an alert patient.
- It may reduce the need for other interventions and the severity of poisoning with highly toxic substances.
- The time window may be **extended** for massive overdoses, sustained-release formulations and anticholinergic drugs (which delay gastric emptying) (Hüser et al., 2025).

**Arguments against (limitations):**

- **No clinical studies have demonstrated a clear reduction in morbidity or mortality** compared with no intervention (Hüser et al., 2025).
- Most patients present after one hour, when benefit is small.
- Risk of **aspiration**, particularly in patients whose consciousness may deteriorate after charcoal is given (for example TCAs, seizure-inducing drugs).
- Poor palatability and vomiting reduce compliance.
- It is **ineffective for many common South African poisons** (paraffin, corrosives, alcohols, iron, lithium, many household products).
- It may **delay transport** if time is spent persuading a patient to drink it.
- In the prehospital setting, the one-hour limit and the requirement of full consciousness significantly restrict its use.

**Conclusion**: activated charcoal is **not a routine treatment for all poisoned patients**. It should be used **selectively**: in an alert, cooperative patient who has ingested a potentially toxic amount of a charcoal-adsorbed substance within about an hour (or later for sustained-release or massive ingestions on expert advice), with no contraindications. In the prehospital setting, the CDST criteria should be followed strictly, and charcoal must never delay airway protection or transport. Advice from the Poisons Information Helpline can help decide whether it is warranted.

## 62.3 Discuss the use of toxidromes in the context of emergency care diagnostics in patients presenting with poisoning

### What a toxidrome is

A **toxidrome** (toxic syndrome) is a group of signs and symptoms that consistently occur together with exposure to a particular class of toxins. Recognising a toxidrome allows the clinician to identify the **likely class** of toxin (even when the specific agent is unknown), anticipate complications, and start empirical treatment and specific antidotes (Hüser et al., 2025). The key examination findings that differentiate toxidromes are **level of consciousness, pupil size, heart rate, blood pressure, respiratory rate, temperature, skin (sweaty versus dry), mucous membranes (salivation versus dry mouth), bowel sounds and urinary function, and muscle tone and reflexes** (Kaswa, 2024; Tutorial 1 memo).

### The CDST toxidromes (HPCSA PBEC, 2025, p. 55)

| Toxidrome | Features | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Sympathomimetic / psychostimulant / hallucinogenic** | Agitation, hallucinations, seizures; **sweating**; hyperthermia; **dilated pupils**; tachycardia; hypertension | LSD, cocaine, amphetamines (methamphetamine "tik"), MDMA |
| **Anticholinergic** | Agitation, hallucinations, seizures, unresponsiveness; **flushed, dry, warm skin**; hyperthermia; **dilated pupils**; tachycardia; **urinary retention**; **decreased bowel sounds**; blurred vision | Antihistamines, atropine, TCAs, *Datura stramonium*, some antipsychotics |
| **Opioid** | Depressed level of consciousness; hypothermia; **pinpoint pupils**; bradycardia; hypotension; **respiratory depression** | Morphine, codeine, methadone, heroin, fentanyl, tramadol |
| **Cholinergic** | Depressed level of consciousness, seizures; **sweating**; **pinpoint pupils, watery eyes**; **bradycardia**; **drooling**; urinary incontinence; diarrhoea and vomiting; **airway secretions/crackles** | Organophosphates, carbamates (aldicarb "Two-step") |
| **Sedative-hypnotic** | Depressed level of consciousness; **slurred speech**; respiratory depression (vital signs otherwise often near normal) | Alcohol, benzodiazepines, barbiturates |

**Mnemonics:**

- **Anticholinergic**: "**Hot as a hare** (hyperthermia), **dry as a bone** (dry skin and mucosa), **red as a beet** (flushing), **blind as a bat** (mydriasis, blurred vision), **mad as a hatter** (delirium), **full as a flask** (urinary retention), and the heart runs alone" (tachycardia).
- **Cholinergic (muscarinic)**: **DUMBELS** — **D**iarrhoea/**D**iaphoresis, **U**rination, **M**iosis, **B**radycardia/**B**ronchorrhoea/**B**ronchospasm, **E**mesis, **L**acrimation, **S**alivation; the "killer Bs" (bradycardia, bronchorrhoea, bronchospasm) cause death. **Nicotinic** effects (muscle fasciculations, weakness, paralysis, tachycardia and hypertension) can be remembered by the days of the week: **M**ydriasis (occasionally), **T**achycardia, **W**eakness, **T**witching/fasciculations, **F**asciculations, **S**ugar (hyperglycaemia).

### Other important toxidromes and syndromes (beyond the CDST table)

- **Serotonin toxicity (serotonin syndrome)**: the triad of **altered mental status** (agitation, confusion), **autonomic instability** (tachycardia, hypertension, hyperthermia, sweating, diarrhoea, mydriasis) and **neuromuscular hyperactivity** (**clonus** — inducible, ocular or spontaneous — hyperreflexia and tremor, more marked in the lower limbs), with rapid onset (within hours) after serotonergic drugs (SSRIs, SNRIs, MAOIs, tramadol, MDMA, linezolid, fentanyl, combinations). The **Hunter criteria** (spontaneous clonus; inducible clonus with agitation or diaphoresis; ocular clonus with agitation or diaphoresis; tremor with hyperreflexia; or hypertonia with temperature >38 °C and ocular or inducible clonus) are the most accurate diagnostic tool (Dunkley et al., 2003; Boyer and Shannon, 2005).
- **Neuroleptic malignant syndrome (NMS)**: develops over days after starting or increasing dopamine antagonists (or abrupt withdrawal of dopaminergic drugs), with **"lead-pipe" rigidity**, **hyperthermia**, altered mental status, autonomic instability and raised creatine kinase; **bradyreflexia** and the absence of clonus distinguish it from serotonin toxicity.
- **Withdrawal syndromes** (alcohol, sedatives, opioids) (52.8).
- **Sodium channel blockade**: widened QRS (>100 ms), terminal R wave in aVR, ventricular arrhythmias, seizures, hypotension (TCAs, diphenhydramine, local anaesthetics, flecainide) (Hüser et al., 2025).
- **Metabolic acidosis with a raised anion gap** (salicylates, methanol, ethylene glycol, iron, cyanide, metformin): the mnemonic **MUDPILES** or **CAT MUDPILES** (carbon monoxide/cyanide, alcoholic ketoacidosis, toluene, methanol, uraemia, diabetic ketoacidosis, paraldehyde/phenformin, iron/isoniazid, lactic acidosis, ethylene glycol, salicylates) (Tintinalli et al., 2020); clinically suggested by Kussmaul breathing (Kaswa, 2024).
- **Asphyxiant syndrome** (CO, cyanide): headache, confusion, seizures, coma, cardiovascular collapse, with a normal SpO₂ reading.

### How toxidromes differ — key discriminators

| Finding | Sympathomimetic | Anticholinergic | Opioid | Cholinergic | Sedative-hypnotic | Serotonin |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Mental status | Agitated | Agitated/delirious | Depressed | Depressed/seizures | Depressed | Agitated |
| Pupils | Dilated | Dilated | Pinpoint | Pinpoint | Normal/small | Dilated |
| Skin | **Sweaty** | **Dry, hot, flushed** | Normal/cool | **Sweaty** | Normal | Sweaty |
| HR | ↑ | ↑ | ↓ | ↓ (may ↑ — nicotinic) | Normal/↓ | ↑ |
| Temperature | ↑ | ↑ | ↓ | Normal | Normal/↓ | ↑ |
| Bowel sounds | Normal/↑ | **↓** | ↓ | **↑ (diarrhoea)** | ↓ | **↑** |
| Secretions | — | Dry mouth | — | **Drooling, bronchorrhoea** | — | — |
| Neuromuscular | Tremor | Myoclonus possible | — | Fasciculations, weakness | — | **Clonus, hyperreflexia** |

The single most useful bedside discriminator between the sympathomimetic and anticholinergic toxidromes is the **skin**: sweaty in sympathomimetic toxicity, dry in anticholinergic toxicity (Tutorial 1 memo; Tintinalli et al., 2020).

### Value and limitations of toxidromes in emergency diagnostics

**Value:**

- Allows rapid, bedside identification of the likely class of toxin without laboratory tests, which is essential prehospitally and in resource-limited settings.
- Guides immediate, potentially life-saving treatment (naloxone for opioids, atropine for organophosphates, sodium bicarbonate for sodium channel blockade, cooling and benzodiazepines for sympathomimetic toxicity).
- Helps anticipate complications (seizures, arrhythmias, respiratory failure).
- Helps prioritise investigations and communicate with the poison centre.

**Limitations:**

- **Mixed ingestions** (about 50% of cases; Hüser et al., 2025) produce mixed or **opposing** features that obscure the toxidrome ("toxidromes may be more difficult to appraise with multiple toxicants especially if they have antagonistic effects").
- **Incomplete toxidromes** are common (for example tramadol may not cause miosis; hypoxia may dilate pupils in opioid overdose).
- Features **evolve over time** (early versus late presentation).
- Some important poisons do **not produce a recognisable toxidrome early** (paracetamol, iron, toxic alcohols, colchicine, amatoxin mushrooms) — patients may appear well initially despite lethal ingestions.
- **Comorbid conditions** (hypoglycaemia, head injury, sepsis, hypothermia) can mimic or mask toxidromes; Hüser et al. (2025) warn clinicians to "avoid attributing altered mental status solely to intoxication".
- Toxidromes identify a **class**, not a specific agent.

Toxidromes should therefore be used together with the history, the scene, the ECG, glucose and other point-of-care tests, and expert advice.

## 62.4 Comprehensively discuss the approach to the mixed drug overdose

### Why mixed overdoses are challenging

About half of poisoned patients admitted to ICU have ingested more than one substance (Hüser et al., 2025), often with **alcohol**. Mixed overdoses are challenging because:

- toxidromes overlap or cancel out (for example an opioid with a stimulant — "speedball" — or an anticholinergic with a sedative);
- **synergistic toxicity** occurs (for example benzodiazepines plus opioids or alcohol — profound respiratory depression; multiple serotonergic drugs — serotonin syndrome; multiple QT-prolonging drugs — torsade de pointes; multiple cardiodepressants);
- the clinical course is unpredictable, with **delayed and sequential toxicity** (for example a patient who appears drowsy from a benzodiazepine may later develop liver failure from paracetamol or cardiotoxicity from a sustained-release calcium channel blocker);
- treatment of one agent may worsen another (for example **flumazenil** to reverse a benzodiazepine can precipitate seizures in a co-ingestion of TCAs; naloxone may unmask stimulant-induced agitation);
- the history is often unreliable (Hüser et al., 2025).

### Approach

1. **Assume the worst**: treat as a potentially life-threatening poisoning; assume that dangerous agents (paracetamol, TCAs, cardiotoxic drugs, sustained-release preparations) may be involved until excluded.
2. **ABCDE stabilisation** (CDST): airway (anticipate deterioration; lateral position; BVM; advanced airway if needed), breathing (oxygen; ventilation), circulation (IV access, fluids, ECG monitoring), disability (GCS, pupils, **glucose**, seizures), exposure (temperature, injuries, patches, track marks).
3. **12-lead ECG and continuous cardiac monitoring** — look for QRS widening (>100 ms), QTc prolongation (≥500 ms), bradycardia, AV block and arrhythmias (Hüser et al., 2025).
4. **History and collateral information** — every substance available in the home (prescribed, over-the-counter, herbal, traditional, recreational); time of ingestion; bring all containers (HPCSA PBEC, 2025).
5. **Identify the dominant toxidrome** and treat life threats in order of priority: airway and breathing first; then cardiovascular toxicity; then seizures; then hyperthermia.
6. **Antidotes with caution**: titrate **naloxone** to adequate breathing (not full awakening) to avoid unmasking agitation and withdrawal (CDST); **avoid flumazenil** in mixed overdoses (not in the CDST; contraindicated with TCA co-ingestion and in benzodiazepine-dependent patients — Hüser et al., 2025; RCEM, 2025); give **sodium bicarbonate** for a widened QRS (ANT/ECP).
7. **Decontamination**: activated charcoal only if the CDST criteria are met (conscious, within one hour, adsorbable substances); never if consciousness is impaired.
8. **Contact the Poisons Information Helpline** (0861 555 777).
9. **Transport all mixed overdoses** to an emergency centre (by definition, toxicity is uncertain); monitor every 10 minutes.
10. **Hospital**: paracetamol and salicylate levels in **all** deliberate overdoses (because these may be clinically silent early), blood gas, electrolytes, renal and liver function, CK, and further management (NAC, enhanced elimination, ICU) (Hüser et al., 2025; Kaswa, 2024).
11. **Psychosocial assessment** before discharge for deliberate self-poisoning (Kaswa, 2024).

## 62.5 Discuss the approach to a poisoning situation where the causative agent is not known

The principle is "**treat the patient, not the poison**". The approach integrates the general management above with a systematic search for clues:

1. **Scene safety** — consider toxic gases (CO, cyanide, hydrogen sulphide), chemical contamination (organophosphates), or multiple patients with similar symptoms (suggesting an environmental, food-borne, or deliberate chemical release).
2. **ABCDE stabilisation** and empirical treatment of reversible causes: oxygen; ventilation; **glucose** (hypoglycaemia mimics many poisonings); naloxone if there is an opioid picture with respiratory depression; thiamine in malnourished or alcohol-dependent patients; benzodiazepines for seizures and agitation; cooling for hyperthermia; warming for hypothermia (HPCSA PBEC, 2025).
3. **Clues from the history and scene**: containers, pill bottles, syringes, notes, plants, chemicals, the patient's occupation (farm worker, miner, cleaner), recent prescriptions, medications belonging to other household members, traditional medicines, and the time course (HPCSA PBEC, 2025, pp. 54–55).
4. **Clues from the examination**: the toxidrome; **unusual odours** (bitter almonds — cyanide; garlic — organophosphates or arsenic; fruity/acetone — ketoacidosis or isopropanol; alcohol; petrol/paraffin — hydrocarbons; rotten eggs — hydrogen sulphide; mothballs — naphthalene); skin (cherry-red is a late and unreliable sign of CO; cyanosis unresponsive to oxygen suggests methaemoglobinaemia; bullae suggest barbiturates or CO; needle marks); pupils; temperature; breathing pattern (Kussmaul breathing suggests metabolic acidosis — salicylates, methanol, ethylene glycol); mouth (burns from corrosives, tablet residue, charcoal-like residue from previous treatment); and the ECG.
5. **Point-of-care investigations**: glucose, ECG (QRS, QTc), SpO₂ (falsely normal in CO; low and unresponsive in methaemoglobinaemia — Hüser et al., 2025), ETCO₂, and temperature.
6. **Contact the Poisons Information Helpline** for help with identification (for example identifying tablets by markings).
7. **Transport all patients poisoned with an unknown substance** to the closest emergency centre (HPCSA PBEC, 2025, p. 55), with all containers and substances found.
8. **In hospital**: blood gas, anion and osmolal gaps, electrolytes, renal and liver function, paracetamol and salicylate levels, CK; retaining a blood sample for later analysis; urine toxicology screens have limited value (false positives and negatives; inability to detect many drugs such as Z-drugs, GHB and fentanyl analogues) (Hüser et al., 2025).

## 62.6 Describe and discuss the importance of history taking in drug overdose

### Why history matters

History is the foundation of the diagnosis in poisoning: Kaswa (2024) states that "history-taking aims to identify the toxic substance that caused the overdose or poisoning", and that "identifying toxic substances in a patient is crucial to obtaining a thorough medical history". The history determines the likely toxin, the expected toxicity and time course, the need for decontamination (time since ingestion), the choice of antidote, the need for enhanced elimination, the risk of delayed toxicity, the disposition, and the psychosocial risk.

### Limitations

Hüser et al. (2025) warn that "in patients with poisoning, history is often unreliable". Reasons include altered consciousness, intoxication, intentional concealment or exaggeration (in deliberate self-harm), fear of legal consequences (illicit drugs), embarrassment, unknown contents of street drugs, uncertainty about amounts and timing, and language barriers. **Collateral history** from EMS, family, friends and bystanders is therefore essential (Hüser et al., 2025; Kaswa, 2024).

### What to ask — the "**WHAT, HOW MUCH, WHEN, HOW, WHY, WHO, WHAT ELSE**" framework

1. **What** was taken? Name, strength, formulation (immediate- versus sustained-release, enteric-coated), and whether it could have been a combination; ask to see packets and containers (HPCSA PBEC, 2025). Ask about **all** substances available: prescribed medications (the patient's and other household members'), over-the-counter medicines, herbal and **traditional medicines**, chemicals, pesticides, recreational drugs, and alcohol.
2. **How much?** Count remaining tablets and estimate the maximum possible amount (assume the worst); calculate mg/kg (CDST).
3. **When?** The time of ingestion determines whether charcoal is appropriate (within one hour), whether a drug level is interpretable (for example the paracetamol nomogram requires a level at least four hours after ingestion), and when delayed toxicity may develop. Was it a single ingestion or staggered over hours?
4. **How (route)?** Ingested, injected, inhaled, or applied to the skin.
5. **Why (intent)?** Accidental or deliberate? Suicidal intent determines the psychiatric risk and the need for transport (Outcomes 45–46). In children, consider abuse or neglect.
6. **Who?** Who found the patient, who else was present, and is anyone else affected (for example CO, food poisoning, chemical release)?
7. **What else?**
   - Symptoms since ingestion (vomiting, seizures, collapse);
   - treatment already given (by bystanders, other health care providers — for example milk, salt water, induced vomiting);
   - past medical history (renal and liver disease, epilepsy, cardiac disease, diabetes, HIV, pregnancy);
   - psychiatric history and previous overdoses;
   - allergies;
   - social circumstances;
   - weight (for dose calculations).

### The importance of the ECP's history

The ECP is often the **only** person to see the scene, to speak with family and bystanders before they disperse, and to collect containers and notes. Information not gathered on scene is often lost. A thorough prehospital history and the containers handed over at the emergency centre (HPCSA PBEC, 2025, p. 55) can be decisive for management — for example identifying a sustained-release calcium channel blocker, a TCA, or paracetamol in a patient who currently looks well.

### High-yield points — Outcome 62

- Kaswa's ABCDEFG; treat the patient first; supportive care is the cornerstone.
- CDST: O₂ if SpO₂ <92%, RR >30 or <9, GCS ≤14; skin wash, eye flush 20 min; charcoal 1 g/kg only if fully conscious, oral, harmful, within 1 hour, adsorbable; avoid household products, paraffin, petrol, corrosives, iron, lithium, alcohol; no milk, no vomiting.
- Poisons Information Helpline: **0861 555 777**.
- CDST toxidromes: sympathomimetic (sweaty, dilated), anticholinergic (dry, dilated, urinary retention), opioid (pinpoint, respiratory depression), cholinergic (pinpoint, secretions, bradycardia), sedative-hypnotic (slurred speech, depressed LOC).
- Charcoal: no proven outcome benefit; selective use only.
- Mixed overdose: assume the worst; ECG; avoid flumazenil; titrate naloxone; paracetamol level in all deliberate overdoses.
- History is vital but often unreliable — collateral history and containers.
