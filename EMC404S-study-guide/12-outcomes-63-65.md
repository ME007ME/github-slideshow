# Outcome 63. Discuss poisonous plants and mushrooms indigenous to South Africa commonly involved in serious poisoning incidents for both adults and children

## Context

South Africa has one of the richest floras in the world, and many plants are used in traditional medicine. Plant poisonings (biological toxins) account for part of the 12.6% of poisoning exposures attributed to biological toxins in the Tygerberg Poison Information Centre data (Veale, Wium and Müller, 2013). Plant poisoning occurs through accidental ingestion by children (attractive berries, seeds and flowers), deliberate recreational use (for example *Datura* for hallucinogenic effects), misidentification of edible plants and mushrooms, contamination of grain, and — importantly — the use of plants in **traditional medicines** (63.2) (Van Wyk, Van Heerden and Van Oudtshoorn, 2002).

## 63.1 Discuss common South African plants and mushrooms implicated in poisoning incidents

### Poisonous mushrooms

Mushroom poisoning usually results from misidentification during foraging, accidental ingestion by children, or deliberate use of hallucinogenic mushrooms.

### Prehospital approach to plant and mushroom poisoning

1. Identify the plant or mushroom: bring a sample (in a paper bag, not plastic), photographs, the remains, and any vomitus.
2. Establish the time of ingestion and onset of symptoms (early versus delayed onset for mushrooms).
3. ABCDE; glucose; toxidrome recognition (anticholinergic — *Datura*; cholinergic — muscarinic mushrooms, *Nicotiana*; cardiac glycoside — bradycardia and arrhythmias).
4. Activated charcoal if fully conscious and within one hour (or later for delayed-onset toxins on expert advice).
5. Contact the Poisons Information Helpline (0861 555 777), which can assist with plant identification.
6. Transport symptomatic patients, all suspected amatoxin, cardiac glycoside, *Callilepis*, colchicine or ricin ingestions, and all children who have ingested unknown plants or mushrooms with any symptoms.

## 63.2 Discuss the role of traditional medicine in poisoning incidents, with reference to pesticide involvement, hepatotoxicity and cardiovascular collapse

### Context

Traditional medicine (often called "muthi" in South Africa) is widely used, alongside or instead of Western medicine, for physical, spiritual and social problems, including protection from harm, cleansing, treating illness, and fertility. Traditional health practitioners (izangoma, izinyanga, amagqirha) are recognised under the **Traditional Health Practitioners Act 22 of 2007**. Most traditional remedies are used safely, but poisoning can result from inherently toxic plants, incorrect dosing, the wrong plant being used (misidentification), adulteration and contamination (including with pesticides, heavy metals and pharmaceuticals), and the route of administration (for example **enemas**, which allow rapid absorption and can cause bowel injury) (Stewart et al., 1999; Kaswa, 2024).

The CDST explicitly directs providers to ask about **"herbal, chemicals, pesticides, traditional medications"** in every poisoning (HPCSA PBEC, 2025, p. 54).

### Pesticide involvement

- Pesticides — especially **organophosphates and carbamates** (including illegally decanted **aldicarb**, "Two-step") — are sometimes added to, or used as, traditional remedies, or used for protective rituals, or sold by informal traders alongside traditional products (Rother, 2010).
- Accidental or deliberate ingestion causes a **cholinergic crisis** (60.8), which may not initially be recognised if the history is of "muthi" rather than "poison".
- Pesticides stored in unlabelled containers in households and traditional practitioners' premises contribute to accidental child poisoning (Davies, Hlela and Rother, 2023, cited by Kaswa, 2024).
- **Prehospital implication**: a patient who has taken "muthi" and presents with pinpoint pupils, secretions, bradycardia, sweating, vomiting, diarrhoea and fasciculations must be treated as a **cholinergic poisoning** — PPE, decontamination, ^atropine^ per CDST, airway management.

### Hepatotoxicity

- ***Callilepis laureola*** (Impila) is the best-known South African traditional remedy causing **acute hepatic and renal tubular necrosis**, with high mortality, particularly in children, often administered as an enema (Popat et al., 2001).
- **Pyrrolizidine alkaloids** (from *Senecio*, *Crotalaria* and other plants used in herbal teas and remedies) cause **hepatic veno-occlusive disease**.
- **Atractyloside**-containing plants (for example *Callilepis*) cause hepatorenal failure and hypoglycaemia.
- Traditional remedies may interact with **antiretrovirals** and **anti-TB drugs**, increasing the risk of drug-induced liver injury, or may contain hepatotoxic pharmaceuticals.
- **Clinical features**: vomiting, abdominal pain, jaundice, hypoglycaemia, altered consciousness, coagulopathy, renal failure.
- **Prehospital implication**: check glucose (hypoglycaemia is common), treat dehydration, and transport early; liver failure may develop over days.

### Cardiovascular collapse

- **Cardiac glycoside-containing plants** used in traditional medicine — *Drimia/Urginea* (slangkop), ***Bowiea volubilis*** (climbing onion), *Cotyledon*, and oleander — cause bradyarrhythmias, AV block, ventricular arrhythmias, hyperkalaemia and cardiovascular collapse.
- **Anticholinergic plants** (*Datura*) cause tachyarrhythmias, hyperthermia and collapse.
- ***Boophone disticha*** causes CNS depression, bradycardia and hypotension.
- **Enemas** and purgatives cause profuse diarrhoea, **dehydration, hypovolaemic shock and electrolyte disturbances** (hypokalaemia, hyperkalaemia), and can cause **bowel perforation** and chemical colitis; caustic or oxidising substances (for example **potassium dichromate**, sometimes found in traditional remedies) cause **acute kidney injury**, haemolysis and cardiovascular collapse (Stewart et al., 1999).
- **Prehospital implication**: ECG monitoring, IV fluids, RCSA bradycardia algorithm, CDST management of shock; transport urgently.

### Approach to suspected traditional medicine poisoning

1. **Respectful, non-judgemental inquiry**: patients and families may be reluctant to disclose traditional medicine use for fear of criticism. Ask specifically: "Have you (or your child) taken any traditional or herbal medicines, or had an enema, in the last few days?"
2. **Obtain the remedy** (the container, plant material or powder) for identification, and the name and contact of the traditional health practitioner if possible.
3. **Toxidrome-based management** (cholinergic, anticholinergic, cardiac glycoside, hepatotoxic, renal).
4. **Glucose** in every case.
5. **Contact the Poisons Information Helpline**.
6. **Transport** — toxicity may be delayed.
7. **Children**: consider whether the child is in need of care and protection only where there is wilful neglect or abuse; most families act out of love and cultural belief, and education is the appropriate response.

### High-yield points — Outcome 63

- *Datura*: anticholinergic toxidrome ("hot, dry, red, blind, mad, full"); benzodiazepines; physostigmine in hospital.
- Cardiac glycoside plants (oleander, *Drimia*, *Bowiea*, *Cotyledon*): bradycardia, AV block, hyperkalaemia; digoxin Fab in hospital.
- *Callilepis laureola* (Impila): hepatorenal failure and hypoglycaemia, especially in children.
- *Amanita phalloides*: GI symptoms >6 h after ingestion, false recovery, liver failure.
- Traditional medicine: pesticides (cholinergic crisis), hepatotoxicity (Impila, pyrrolizidine alkaloids), cardiovascular collapse (glycosides, enemas, dichromate).

# Outcome 64. Discuss envenomation by snakes, spiders, scorpions and poisonous marine animals

## 64.1 Discuss snake bite in the Southern African and global context

### Global context

The WHO estimates that each year **1.8–2.7 million people** are envenomed by snakes, with **81,000–138,000 deaths** and about **400,000** people left with permanent disabilities (amputations, contractures, blindness, chronic kidney disease); snakebite envenoming was added to the WHO list of **neglected tropical diseases** in 2017, and the WHO strategy (2019) aims to halve deaths and disabilities by 2030 (WHO, 2019; Gutiérrez et al., 2017). The burden falls mainly on rural populations in sub-Saharan Africa, South and Southeast Asia and Latin America — agricultural workers, herders and children — with limited access to antivenom.

### Southern African context

South Africa has about 170 snake species and subspecies, of which fewer than 20 are considered potentially deadly to humans; most bites occur in rural and peri-urban areas, at night or in summer, on the lower limbs, and the **puff adder** (cytotoxic) and **Mozambique spitting cobra** (cytotoxic) are responsible for a large proportion of significant bites, while **mambas** and **non-spitting cobras** cause the most deaths (Müller et al., 2012). The majority of bites are by non-venomous snakes or are "dry bites" (no venom injected), but every bite must be assessed.

### Classification of South African snake venoms by clinical syndrome (Müller et al., 2012)

| Syndrome | Snakes | Pathophysiology | Clinical features |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Cytotoxic (progressive painful swelling)** | **Puff adder** (*Bitis arietans*), Gaboon adder (*B. gabonica*), **Mozambique spitting cobra** (*Naja mossambica*), black-necked spitting cobra, rinkhals (mixed), night adders, stiletto (burrowing) snakes | Proteolytic enzymes, phospholipases and metalloproteinases destroy tissue and blood vessels; fluid shifts into the limb | Immediate severe pain; **progressive swelling** (which may involve the whole limb within hours), blistering, bruising, **tissue necrosis** (especially spitting cobras — often with relatively little pain); hypovolaemic shock from fluid loss into the limb (puff adder); compartment-like syndrome (true compartment syndrome is uncommon); mild coagulopathy (Gaboon adder) |
| **Neurotoxic (progressive weakness)** | **Black mamba** (*Dendroaspis polylepis*), **green mamba** (*D. angusticeps*), Jameson's mamba; **non-spitting cobras** — **Cape cobra** (*Naja nivea*), **snouted cobra** (*N. annulifera*), forest cobra; rinkhals (mixed); **berg adder** and other small adders (atypical neurotoxicity) | **Postsynaptic** (α-neurotoxins — cobras, block acetylcholine receptors) and **presynaptic/dendrotoxins and fasciculins** (mambas — increase acetylcholine release, then fatigue) causing neuromuscular blockade | Often minimal local signs (cobras may cause some swelling); paraesthesia; sweating, salivation, nausea; **ptosis** (an early sign), diplopia, blurred vision; **bulbar paralysis** — dysphagia, dysarthria, drooling, inability to protrude the tongue; descending flaccid paralysis; **respiratory muscle paralysis and death** — may occur within 30 minutes to a few hours with mamba bites; mamba bites may cause fasciculations; **berg adder** causes ptosis, ophthalmoplegia, loss of taste and smell, and occasionally respiratory failure, with local swelling |
| **Haemotoxic (bleeding)** | **Boomslang** (*Dispholidus typus*), **vine/twig snake** (*Thelotornis* species) | Procoagulant venom activates prothrombin → consumption coagulopathy (**venom-induced consumption coagulopathy**, VICC) → incoagulable blood | **Delayed onset** (often hours to more than 24 hours) — a minor bite may seem harmless initially; then bleeding from the bite site, gums, nose, old wounds, venepuncture sites; haematuria, haematemesis, intracranial haemorrhage; renal failure; boomslang bites are uncommon (back-fanged, shy snakes, bites mostly in snake handlers) |
| **Venom in the eyes** (spitting cobras, rinkhals) | — | Direct irritation | Intense pain, blepharospasm, conjunctivitis, corneal erosions; risk of blindness if untreated |

### Prehospital management of snakebite (CDST and Southern African guidance)

**First aid and what to do:**

1. **Scene safety**: move away from the snake; do not try to catch or kill it (a photograph from a safe distance is helpful; a dead snake can still envenomate by reflex).
2. **Reassure** the patient and **keep them still** (movement increases lymphatic spread of venom); lie the patient down.
3. **Primary survey (ABCDE)**: airway, breathing (assess for respiratory muscle weakness — ability to count aloud, cough, lift head; RR, SpO₂), circulation (shock), disability (ptosis, bulbar signs, GCS).
4. **Expose the bite site and cover it with a sterile dressing; remove any constrictive clothing or jewellery** (rings, watches, bracelets, shoes) before swelling develops (HPCSA PBEC, 2025, p. 54).
5. **Immobilise the bitten limb** with a splint and keep it at about heart level.
6. **Pressure immobilisation bandage (PIB)**: applied firmly (like a sprained-ankle bandage) from the bite site up the entire limb with splinting — **recommended only for bites by known or suspected neurotoxic snakes (mambas, non-spitting cobras)** when transport is prolonged; it slows lymphatic spread of venom. **It must not be used for cytotoxic bites** (puff adder, spitting cobras), because confining the venom worsens local tissue destruction (Müller et al., 2012). Once applied, it should not be removed until the patient is in a facility with antivenom.
7. **Mark the edge of the swelling** with a pen and the time, every 30–60 minutes, to track progression.
8. **IV access** in the **unbitten limb**; IV fluids for shock (CDST shock tool).
9. **Analgesia** (CDST Pain tool — paracetamol, opioids, ketamine for ANT/ECP; **avoid NSAIDs**, which increase bleeding risk; avoid intramuscular injections in patients with possible coagulopathy).
10. **Neurotoxic envenomation with respiratory failure**: **BVM ventilation and, for ECPs, advanced airway management (Airway tool)** — patients who are ventilated survive neurotoxic paralysis even before antivenom is given; this is the single most important prehospital intervention for mamba and cobra bites.
11. **Venom in the eyes**: **irrigate immediately and copiously** with water or saline (at least 20 minutes; CDST eye irrigation), do not rub, and transport for ophthalmological assessment.
12. **Anaphylaxis** to venom (rare) or to antivenom: RCSA anaphylaxis algorithm (adrenaline 0.5 mg IM 1:1000 in adults).
13. **Transport** rapidly to a facility that can give **antivenom** and manage complications (ICU, ventilation); pre-alert.
14. Contact the Poisons Information Helpline or a recognised snakebite expert for advice.

**What NOT to do**: do **not** cut, suck or incise the wound; do **not** apply an **arterial tourniquet** (causes ischaemia, worse necrosis and amputations); do **not** apply ice, electric shocks, chemicals or traditional remedies to the wound; do **not** give alcohol; do **not** allow the patient to walk if it can be avoided.

### In hospital

- Observation for at least 12–24 hours (longer for boomslang because of delayed coagulopathy), with repeated assessment of swelling, neurological signs and coagulation (20-minute whole blood clotting test, INR, fibrinogen, platelets).
- **Antivenom** when indicated (64.3).
- Supportive care: ventilation, fluids, analgesia, wound care (surgical debridement of necrosis; fasciotomy only if compartment syndrome is confirmed by measured pressures), tetanus prophylaxis, and antibiotics only for established infection.

## 64.2 Discuss spider bites and scorpion stings in the Southern African and global context

### Spider bites

Almost all spiders are venomous, but only a few are medically important. Globally, the most important are the **widow spiders** (*Latrodectus* — latrodectism), the **recluse spiders** (*Loxosceles* — loxoscelism), the Australian **funnel-web spiders** (*Atrax*, *Hadronyche* — severe neurotoxicity) and the Brazilian **wandering spiders** (*Phoneutria*) (Isbister and Fan, 2011). Many lesions blamed on "spider bites" are actually infections (for example community-acquired MRSA) or other conditions.

**Southern African medically important spiders:**

| Spider | Venom type | Clinical features | Management |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Button spiders** (*Latrodectus* — the **black button spiders**, for example *L. indistinctus*, are more dangerous than the **brown button spider**, *L. geometricus*) | **Neurotoxic** — **α-latrotoxin** causes massive release of neurotransmitters (acetylcholine, noradrenaline) at nerve endings → **latrodectism** | The bite may be barely noticed (a pinprick); within 30–60 minutes: **severe pain** spreading from the bite to the regional lymph nodes and then generalised; **painful muscle cramps and rigidity**, especially of the **abdomen** (board-like rigidity mimicking an acute abdomen), back, chest and legs; **sweating** (often localised), restlessness and anxiety, hypertension, tachycardia, nausea and vomiting, ptosis and facial grimacing ("facies latrodectismica"), priapism; rarely respiratory difficulty; children and older people more severely affected | ABCDE; reassure; immobilise; analgesia (opioids per CDST Pain tool); benzodiazepines for muscle spasm (within scope); transport; hospital — **button spider antivenom** (South African Vaccine Producers) for severe latrodectism |
| **Violin (recluse) spiders** (*Loxosceles*) | **Cytotoxic** — sphingomyelinase D → dermonecrosis | Painless or mildly painful bite; over hours to days, a blistering, necrotic lesion (often with a central dark eschar surrounded by a pale ischaemic halo and erythema — "red, white and blue"), which may ulcerate and take weeks to heal; rarely **systemic loxoscelism** (haemolysis, fever, renal failure) | Wound care, analgesia, tetanus, surgical review; no antivenom available in South Africa |
| **Sac spiders** (*Cheiracanthium*) | Cytotoxic | Painful bite with local swelling, blistering and small necrotic lesions, often two fang marks | Wound care; supportive |
| **Six-eyed sand spiders** (*Sicarius*) | Highly cytotoxic (rarely bites humans) | Severe necrosis and potentially systemic effects (haemorrhage) | Supportive; hospital |
| **Baboon spiders** (tarantulas — Theraphosidae) | Mildly venomous; urticating hairs | Painful bite (mechanical); local reaction; eye irritation from hairs | Analgesia; eye irrigation |

### Scorpion stings

**Global context**: scorpion stings are common in North Africa, the Middle East, India, Mexico, and South America, with more than one million stings and several thousand deaths a year, mostly in children; dangerous scorpions belong mainly to the family **Buthidae**.

**Southern African context**: dangerous scorpions belong to the genus ***Parabuthus*** (family Buthidae) — especially ***P. transvaalicus*** and ***P. granulatus*** — found in the drier western and northern parts of the country; *Parabuthus* species can also spray venom (eye exposure). A useful field rule is that scorpions with **thick tails and small, thin pincers are dangerous** (Buthidae, for example *Parabuthus*), whereas those with **thin tails and large, robust pincers are less venomous** (for example *Opistophthalmus* — burrowing scorpions, which cause painful but not dangerous stings) (Müller et al., 2012).

**Pathophysiology**: *Parabuthus* venom is **neurotoxic** — it prolongs sodium channel opening in nerve membranes, causing excessive release of neurotransmitters (both sympathetic and parasympathetic) and neuromuscular hyperactivity.

**Clinical features**:

- **Local**: burning pain at the sting site, often with **paraesthesia and hyperaesthesia** (worsened by tapping — "tap test"); little swelling.
- **Systemic**: spreading paraesthesia, restlessness, involuntary muscle jerks and **muscle pain and cramps**, **dysphagia** and difficulty swallowing saliva (drooling), **hypersalivation**, sweating, ptosis, blurred vision, slurred speech, **urinary retention**, priapism, tachycardia and hypertension (sometimes bradycardia), and, in severe cases, generalised muscle weakness, **respiratory distress and respiratory failure**. Children and older people are most at risk of severe envenomation.

**Management**:

- ABCDE; reassure; keep still.
- **Local pain**: analgesia (CDST Pain tool); local cold application; local anaesthetic infiltration in hospital.
- **Systemic envenomation**: oxygen; airway management and **ventilatory support** for respiratory failure (the most important life-saving measure); IV fluids; benzodiazepines for agitation and muscle spasm with care (respiratory depression); transport.
- **Hospital**: **SAVP scorpion antivenom** for severe systemic envenomation (Müller et al., 2012).
- Eye exposure: irrigate.

## 64.3 Discuss the use, role and complications associated with antivenom for snake and spider bites

### What antivenom is

Antivenom (antivenin) consists of **immunoglobulin fragments** (in South Africa, purified equine F(ab')₂ fragments) produced by immunising horses with venom; it **neutralises circulating (unbound) venom** but cannot reverse tissue damage that has already occurred or neurotoxins already bound to receptors (WHO, 2016). In South Africa, antivenoms are produced by the **South African Vaccine Producers (SAVP)**:

- **SAIMR polyvalent snake antivenom** — raised against the venom of ten species: **puff adder, Gaboon adder, rinkhals, green mamba, black mamba, Jameson's mamba, Cape cobra, forest cobra, snouted cobra and Mozambique spitting cobra**. It is **not effective for berg adder and other small adders**, and its effectiveness against the Mozambique spitting cobra is weaker; it is **not effective for boomslang or vine snake** bites. Cross-protection for other spitting cobras is uncertain.
- **Boomslang monovalent antivenom** — for boomslang envenomation with coagulopathy (there is **no antivenom for the vine/twig snake**).
- **Scorpion antivenom** (*Parabuthus*).
- **Spider (button spider) antivenom** (*Latrodectus*).

Supply of SAVP antivenom has been constrained at times, so it may be held at selected referral centres; the poison centres can advise on availability.

### Role and indications

Antivenom is **the only specific treatment** for systemic envenomation, but it is expensive, scarce and can cause severe reactions, so it is given **only when clearly indicated**, not for every bite. Indications for polyvalent antivenom in Southern Africa include (Müller et al., 2012; WHO, 2016):

- **progressive neurotoxicity** — ptosis progressing to bulbar or respiratory weakness (cobra, mamba);
- **progressive, rapidly spreading swelling** (for example swelling extending beyond the wrist or ankle within a few hours of a bite on the hand or foot, or involving more than half of the limb), or swelling of a digit after a bite on that digit — particularly with puff adder and Gaboon adder bites;
- **shock** or cardiovascular instability attributable to envenomation;
- **coagulopathy or spontaneous bleeding** (boomslang — monovalent antivenom);
- **bites by a positively identified dangerous snake** with evolving signs, especially in children.

**Dose and administration** (hospital-level; follow the SAVP package insert and expert advice): antivenom is given **intravenously** (never intramuscularly into the bitten site), diluted in normal saline over about 30 minutes, or as a slow IV injection, in a setting where anaphylaxis can be treated. The **dose is based on the amount of venom injected, not on body weight, so children receive the same dose as adults**. Typical initial doses of polyvalent antivenom are in the range of several vials (for example 5–10 vials, 50–100 mL), with larger initial and repeated doses (up to about 20 vials) in severe mamba envenomation, and further doses according to clinical response (Müller et al., 2012). **Antivenom should be given as soon as possible** once indicated, but can still be effective many hours (or even days, for coagulopathy) after the bite.

### Complications (adverse reactions)

1. **Early (acute) reactions** — occurring within minutes to about three hours of starting the infusion: urticaria, itching, fever, rigors, nausea, vomiting, tachycardia, bronchospasm, angioedema, hypotension and **anaphylaxis** (which can be fatal). These are mostly non-IgE-mediated (anaphylactoid, complement activation) and occur in a substantial proportion of patients receiving equine antivenoms (WHO, 2016). **Management**: stop or slow the infusion; **adrenaline 0.5 mg IM (1:1000)** in adults (0.01 mg/kg in children) per the RCSA anaphylaxis algorithm; IV fluids; antihistamines and corticosteroids as adjuncts; restart cautiously once the reaction has resolved if antivenom is still needed. **Preparation**: adrenaline drawn up, airway equipment ready, IV access, monitoring. Some guidelines suggest prophylactic low-dose subcutaneous adrenaline before antivenom in high-risk settings (WHO, 2016).
2. **Pyrogenic reactions** — fever and rigors (from endotoxin contamination), within one to two hours.
3. **Late reactions — serum sickness** — occurring 5–24 days after antivenom: fever, urticaria, arthralgia, lymphadenopathy, proteinuria, and rarely neuritis or glomerulonephritis; treated with antihistamines and a short course of corticosteroids.

**Other limitations**: species-specificity (antivenom does not cover all snakes); limited availability, cost and cold-chain requirements; it does not reverse established necrosis; and in neurotoxic envenomation it may not reverse paralysis once neurotoxins are bound — **ventilation remains essential**.

**Prehospital relevance**: antivenom is a hospital intervention in South Africa and is not part of the CDST; the ECP's role is to recognise envenomation syndromes, provide airway and ventilatory support, manage shock and pain, apply the correct first aid (immobilisation; PIB for neurotoxic bites only), treat anaphylaxis, and transport rapidly to a facility that holds antivenom.

## 64.4 Discuss poisonous marine animals in the Southern African and global context

Marine envenomations and poisonings are encountered along South Africa's long coastline, particularly in KwaZulu-Natal (warm Indian Ocean waters) and during the summer holiday season.

### Envenomation by stinging animals

| Animal | Southern African relevance | Venom / mechanism | Clinical features | Management |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Bluebottle** (*Physalia* — Portuguese man-o'-war / bluebottle) | Very common on South African beaches, especially with onshore winds | Nematocysts (stinging cells) on tentacles inject venom | Immediate intense burning pain, linear red welts; occasionally systemic symptoms (nausea, muscle cramps), rarely anaphylaxis | Remove from water; remove adhered tentacles (with gloves or by rinsing with **seawater** — not fresh water, which may trigger nematocyst discharge); **hot water immersion (about 45 °C) for 20 minutes** is more effective than ice for pain relief (Loten et al., 2006); vinegar is **not** recommended for *Physalia* (it may trigger further nematocyst discharge in this species); analgesia; treat anaphylaxis |
| **Box jellyfish** (*Chironex fleckeri*) and Irukandji jellyfish | Mainly northern Australia and the Indo-Pacific (not a significant South African hazard) | Potent cardiotoxic and neurotoxic venom | Severe pain, cardiovascular collapse and death within minutes (box jellyfish); Irukandji syndrome (delayed severe pain, hypertension, pulmonary oedema) | **Vinegar** to inactivate undischarged nematocysts (Australian practice), CPR, box jellyfish antivenom (Australia) |
| **Venomous fish** — **stonefish** (*Synanceia verrucosa*, found on KwaZulu-Natal reefs), scorpionfish, lionfish, catfish, and **stingrays** | Stepping on stonefish in shallow reef waters; stingray injuries in shallow sandy water | **Heat-labile** protein venoms injected through spines | **Excruciating pain** out of proportion to the wound, swelling, local necrosis; systemic effects (stonefish): nausea, sweating, hypotension, arrhythmias, pulmonary oedema, collapse; stingray: deep lacerations and puncture wounds with barb fragments, possible penetrating chest or abdominal injury | **Hot water immersion (as hot as tolerated, about 45 °C, test on an unaffected limb first) for 30–90 minutes** (denatures heat-labile venom and relieves pain); analgesia (opioids per CDST); remove visible spines; irrigate; tetanus; X-ray for retained fragments; antibiotics for marine-contaminated wounds in hospital; stonefish antivenom (from Australia) is rarely available in South Africa |
| **Sea urchins** | Common on rocky shores | Spines (some venomous) | Pain, embedded spines, granulomas | Hot water immersion; spine removal |
| **Cone snails** (*Conus*) | Found in KwaZulu-Natal (Indo-Pacific species) | **Conotoxins** — neuromuscular blockade | Local pain or numbness, then paralysis and **respiratory failure** | Pressure immobilisation (as for neurotoxic snakebite); **ventilatory support**; no antivenom |
| **Blue-ringed octopus** (*Hapalochlaena*) | Indo-Pacific and Australia (not established in South Africa) | **Tetrodotoxin** — sodium channel blockade | Painless bite, then rapid paralysis and respiratory failure with preserved consciousness | Pressure immobilisation; ventilation until the toxin wears off |

### High-yield points — Outcome 64

- WHO: 1.8–2.7 million snakebite envenomings and 81,000–138,000 deaths a year; NTD since 2017.
- SA syndromes: **cytotoxic** (puff adder, spitting cobras — painful progressive swelling, necrosis), **neurotoxic** (mambas, Cape and snouted cobras — ptosis, bulbar paralysis, respiratory failure), **haemotoxic** (boomslang, vine snake — delayed bleeding).
- First aid: reassure, keep still, remove rings, immobilise, mark swelling; PIB **only for neurotoxic bites**; **no** tourniquet, cutting, sucking, ice; **ventilate** neurotoxic paralysis; irrigate venom in the eyes.
- SAVP polyvalent covers 10 species (puff adder, Gaboon adder, rinkhals, 3 mambas, Cape, forest, snouted cobras, Mozambique spitting cobra); not berg adder, boomslang or vine snake; boomslang monovalent; same dose for children; give IV with adrenaline ready; early anaphylactic reactions, pyrogenic reactions, serum sickness at 5–24 days.
- Button spider (*Latrodectus*) — neurotoxic latrodectism (abdominal rigidity, sweating); violin spider — necrosis.
- *Parabuthus* scorpions (thick tail, thin pincers) — neurotoxic; respiratory failure in children and older people.
- Marine: hot water immersion for fish venoms and bluebottles; ventilation for paralysis (PSP, tetrodotoxin, cone snail); red tide on the west coast.

# Outcome 65. Discuss the management of a patient with scombroid food poisoning

## Definition and cause

**Scombroid fish poisoning** (histamine fish poisoning) is a form of food poisoning caused by eating fish that contains high levels of **histamine** (and other biogenic amines, such as cadaverine and putrescine, which enhance histamine's effects). It is named after the **Scombridae** family of fish (tuna, mackerel, bonito, skipjack), but also occurs with non-scombroid fish such as sardines, anchovies, herring, mahi-mahi (dorado), bluefish, amberjack and marlin (Hungerford, 2010). It is one of the most common forms of fish-related food poisoning worldwide, and outbreaks typically affect several people who ate the same fish.

## Pathophysiology

These fish naturally contain high levels of free **histidine** in their muscle. When fish is **not kept adequately chilled** after it is caught (inadequate refrigeration during storage, transport or handling), bacteria on and in the fish (for example *Morganella morganii*, *Klebsiella*, *Proteus*, *Hafnia*, *Photobacterium*) produce the enzyme **histidine decarboxylase**, which converts histidine to **histamine** (Hungerford, 2010). Key facts:

- **Histamine is heat-stable** — it is **not destroyed by cooking, canning, freezing or smoking** once it has formed.
- The fish usually **looks, smells and tastes normal**, although some people notice a sharp, peppery, metallic or bitter taste.
- The ingested histamine (together with the other amines, which inhibit the gut enzymes that normally break down histamine — diamine oxidase and histamine N-methyltransferase) is absorbed and acts on **H1 and H2 receptors**, producing an illness that **mimics an allergic reaction** but is **not an allergy** (it is a toxic reaction; no IgE is involved, and the person is not allergic to fish).
- Patients taking **isoniazid** (common in South Africa because of TB treatment) or monoamine oxidase inhibitors are at risk of more severe reactions, because these drugs inhibit histamine-metabolising enzymes.

## Clinical presentation

- **Onset**: rapid — typically **10–30 minutes** (range a few minutes to about two hours) after eating the fish.
- **Skin**: **flushing** of the face, neck and upper body ("sunburn-like"), **urticaria**, pruritus, sweating.
- **Oral**: burning or tingling in the mouth, **peppery or metallic taste**, perioral swelling.
- **Neurological**: **throbbing headache**, dizziness, palpitations, anxiety.
- **Gastrointestinal**: nausea, vomiting, **abdominal cramps, diarrhoea**.
- **Cardiovascular**: **tachycardia**, palpitations, **hypotension** (vasodilatation).
- **Respiratory** (less common): bronchospasm, chest tightness — more likely in people with asthma.
- Severe cases (rare): significant hypotension, arrhythmias, respiratory distress.
- **Duration**: usually self-limiting within **12–24 hours** (often a few hours), with full recovery.
- **Diagnostic clue**: **several people who ate the same fish develop similar symptoms** at about the same time.

## Differential diagnosis

- **Fish or seafood allergy (IgE-mediated anaphylaxis)** — the most important differential: allergy usually affects one person, the person often has a known allergy, angioedema and severe bronchospasm are more prominent, and the reaction recurs with every exposure to that fish. Scombroid affects several people, is linked to a particular batch of fish, and does not recur with fresh fish.
- Ciguatera, paralytic shellfish poisoning and tetrodotoxin poisoning (64.4) — prominent neurological features (paraesthesia, paralysis).
- Other food poisonings (bacterial gastroenteritis — longer incubation).
- Monosodium glutamate reaction.

## Prehospital management

1. **Scene and history**: what fish was eaten, when, how it was stored and prepared, how many people are affected; known allergies; medications (isoniazid, MAOIs); asthma.
2. **Primary survey (ABCDE)**: airway (angioedema, stridor), breathing (bronchospasm, SpO₂), circulation (BP, HR, perfusion), disability, exposure (flushing, urticaria).
3. **Oxygen** if SpO₂ <92% or respiratory distress.
4. **If features of anaphylaxis** (airway swelling, bronchospasm, hypotension) — which cannot always be distinguished from severe scombroid in the field — treat as **anaphylaxis** according to the RCSA anaphylaxis algorithm referenced in the CDST: **adrenaline 0.5 mg IM (1:1000)** in adults (child 0.01 mL/kg of 1:1000, up to the age-appropriate maximum per the CDST formulary) (HPCSA PBEC, 2025, pp. 68, 74), high-flow oxygen, IV crystalloid for hypotension, and **salbutamol 5 mg nebulised** for bronchospasm (HPCSA PBEC, 2025, p. 73). Hydrocortisone 200 mg IV (child 5 mg/kg) may be considered per the CDST Breathing difficulty tool.
5. **IV access** and **IV crystalloid** for hypotension or dehydration from vomiting and diarrhoea (CDST shock tool).
6. **Antihistamines** are the specific treatment: an **H1 antagonist** (for example promethazine, chlorphenamine or diphenhydramine) combined with an **H2 antagonist** (for example famotidine or cimetidine) relieves flushing, urticaria, headache, hypotension and GI symptoms (Hungerford, 2010). These are hospital or primary-care medications; they are not listed in the CDST drug appendix for emergency care providers, so the prehospital priority is supportive care and treatment of anaphylaxis-like features, with antihistamines given at the receiving facility (or according to your service's protocol if they are within your scope).
7. **Analgesia** for headache (paracetamol per CDST); antiemetics if within scope.
8. **Monitoring**: vital signs every 10 minutes; ECG if palpitations, arrhythmias or significant hypotension.
9. **Transport**: patients with hypotension, bronchospasm, airway involvement, significant symptoms, comorbidities (asthma, cardiac disease, isoniazid use), or when anaphylaxis cannot be excluded. Mild cases often resolve within hours, but should be assessed at a facility.
10. **Public health**: scombroid poisoning usually occurs in clusters; **food-borne illness outbreaks are notifiable medical conditions in South Africa**, so the facility should notify the local **environmental health** officials, and the remaining fish should be kept (refrigerated) for testing of histamine levels. Advise patients that they are **not allergic to fish** and can eat fresh, properly stored fish in future (unless an allergy is confirmed).

## Prevention

Rapid chilling of fish after catching (ideally to 0–4 °C) and maintenance of the cold chain through storage, transport and retail; buying fish from reputable suppliers; and discarding fish that has been left unrefrigerated. Cooking does not make contaminated fish safe.

### High-yield points — Outcome 65

- Cause: bacterial decarboxylation of histidine to **histamine** in improperly chilled fish (tuna, mackerel, sardines); **heat-stable** — cooking does not prevent it.
- Onset 10–30 minutes: flushing, urticaria, peppery/metallic taste, headache, palpitations, GI symptoms, hypotension; resolves within 12–24 hours; clusters of diners.
- Not an allergy (no IgE); isoniazid increases severity.
- Prehospital: ABCDE; treat anaphylaxis features with **adrenaline 0.5 mg IM**, oxygen, fluids, salbutamol; transport.
- Specific treatment: **H1 + H2 antihistamines** (facility level).
- Notify environmental health; keep the fish.
