# Outcome 45. Reflect on the factors the paramedic must consider when assessing a suicide risk and formulate appropriate interview questions for determining suicidal intent

## Definitions

- **Suicide**: death caused by self-directed injurious behaviour with an intent to die as a result of the behaviour.
- **Suicide attempt**: a non-fatal, self-directed, potentially injurious behaviour with an intent to die. It may or may not result in injury.
- **Suicidal ideation**: thinking about, considering or planning suicide. Tilley et al. (2026) note that callers to EMS may describe this as a person "having thoughts of death".
- **Deliberate self-harm (DSH)**: intentional self-poisoning or self-injury, irrespective of motive or degree of suicidal intent; **non-suicidal self-injury (NSSI)** is self-injury without intent to die (for example cutting to relieve emotional distress). Both are strong predictors of later suicide.
- **Parasuicide**: an older term for a suicide attempt with no intention of death; Tilley et al. (2026) used it to label repeated attempts by the same person.

## The burden of suicide — why this matters

Globally, suicide caused about 727,000 deaths in 2021, with an estimated 20 attempts for every suicide; the global age-standardised suicide rate in 2021 was 8.9 per 100,000, compared with 11.5 per 100,000 in the WHO Africa region and **23.5 per 100,000 in South Africa** — almost 14,000 deaths per year, placing South Africa third highest in Africa (WHO, 2025, cited in Tilley et al., 2026). Suicide is the third leading cause of death among people aged 15–29 years (Tilley et al., 2026). In the Garden Route District study, there were on average **2.8 deaths by suicide and 2.3 attempted suicides per month** attended by EMS, and about 6.3 patients a month with suicidal ideation (Tilley et al., 2026).

Evans, Geduld and Stassen (2018) highlight three facts that are directly relevant to prehospital decision-making:

1. "There is a deep-seated belief that people who threaten suicide are not likely to follow through with their threat… the data assert that this is incorrect."
2. About 10% of those who threaten or attempt suicide eventually kill themselves.
3. **Eighty per cent of those who die by suicide have previously verbally stated their intention to do so.**

Every suicidal statement must therefore be taken seriously.

## Factors to consider in assessing suicide risk

Suicide risk assessment is the process of identifying the factors that increase or decrease a person's risk, assessing the immediacy and seriousness of the current suicidal thinking or behaviour, and using this information to plan management. No single factor or tool reliably predicts suicide; risk assessment is a clinical judgement that integrates the following domains (WHO, 2014; Tintinalli et al., 2020).

### 1. Demographic and historical risk factors (static factors)

- **Male sex**: in the Garden Route data, the odds of males dying by suicide were **5.049 times** those of females, and the odds of males attempting suicide were 2.053 times those of females (Tilley et al., 2026). Women more often attempt suicide and more commonly use overdose or poisoning; men more commonly use lethal methods such as hanging and firearms.
- **Age**: in the Garden Route study, the risk of death by suicide increased with age to a **peak at 41 years**, with median ages of 36 years for death by suicide and 30 years for attempted suicide (Tilley et al., 2026). Globally, rates are also high in older men and in young people aged 15–29 years.
- **Previous suicide attempt** — the single strongest predictor of future suicide.
- **Previous self-harm**, including NSSI (Tilley et al., 2026).
- **Family history of suicide** or exposure to the suicide of a friend.
- **History of childhood abuse or trauma**; Tilley et al. (2026) highlight the role of past trauma and advocate trauma-informed care.

### 2. Clinical risk factors

- **Mental disorders**: depression (especially with hopelessness), bipolar disorder, schizophrenia (especially young men early in the illness and those with command hallucinations), PTSD, personality disorders (especially borderline), eating disorders, and substance use disorders. Tilley et al. (2026) note that PTSD, bipolar disorder, depression, substance abuse and suicidal ideation are associated with deaths by suicide.
- **Alcohol and drug use** — intoxication causes disinhibition and impulsivity.
- **Chronic or terminal physical illness, chronic pain, disability**, and HIV (particularly around the time of diagnosis).
- **Hopelessness** — a strong independent predictor.
- **Recent discharge from a psychiatric facility**, or discharge against medical advice (Evans, Geduld and Stassen, 2018, citing Kuo et al., 2010).

### 3. Psychosocial and situational risk factors

- Recent loss: death, divorce or relationship breakdown, job loss, financial crisis, imprisonment.
- Social isolation, living alone, lack of support.
- Unemployment and poverty (Tilley et al., 2026).
- Domestic violence, sexual violence, bullying (especially in adolescents).
- Legal problems, shame, humiliation.
- Access to lethal means (firearms, pesticides, large quantities of medication, ligature points, heights).
- Stigma and barriers to accessing health care.

### 4. Features of the current episode (dynamic factors) — the most important in the field

- **Intent**: Does the person want to die? Do they still want to die now?
- **Planning**: Was the act planned in advance or impulsive? Was there preparation (for example buying tablets, writing a will or note, giving away possessions, saying goodbye)?
- **Lethality of the method** and the person's **belief** about its lethality (a person who believed that ten paracetamol tablets would kill them had high intent even if the dose was not lethal).
- **Precautions against discovery**: was the act timed or located so that rescue was unlikely?
- **Suicide note**.
- **Reaction to survival**: regret at being alive is a very serious sign; relief and a request for help are more reassuring.
- **Ongoing suicidal ideation** and **access to means**.
- **Command hallucinations** instructing self-harm.
- **Agitation, severe anxiety, insomnia** — acute risk factors.

### 5. Protective factors

Protective factors that reduce risk include strong family and social support, responsibility for children, religious or cultural beliefs that discourage suicide, engagement with health services, problem-solving skills, future plans, and restricted access to lethal means. The absence of protective factors increases risk.

### Clinical reflection

Evans, Geduld and Stassen (2018) found that patients with "less dramatic" suicidal histories were more likely to be discharged on scene, that few providers used formal suicide evaluation tools, and that patients with minimal traditional risk factors were managed more conservatively — yet such patients "are still inherently at risk", and respondents described patients left on scene who later died. The lesson is that an apparently "low-risk" presentation does not mean "no risk", and that **risk factors guide judgement but cannot be used to exclude risk**.

## Risk assessment tools

Several tools exist, but none is sufficiently accurate to be used on its own to decide whether a patient can safely be left at home; they should support, not replace, clinical judgement.

- **SAD PERSONS** (Patterson et al., 1983): Sex (male), Age (<19 or >45), Depression, Previous attempt, Ethanol/drug use, Rational thinking loss (psychosis), Social support lacking, Organised plan, No spouse, Sickness (chronic illness). Its predictive accuracy is poor, but it is a useful memory aid for risk factors.
- **Columbia Suicide Severity Rating Scale (C-SSRS) screener** (Posner et al., 2011): a series of structured questions about wish to be dead, suicidal thoughts, methods, intent, plan and recent behaviour, widely used by first responders.

## Interview questions for determining suicidal intent

### Principles

- **Ask directly.** Asking about suicide **does not** put the idea into someone's head and does not increase risk; most people experience relief when asked.
- Use a **private, calm** setting and a non-judgemental, empathic manner.
- Move from **general to specific** ("gradual approach") — from feelings, to thoughts of death, to suicidal thoughts, to intent, to plans, to preparation and past behaviour.
- Use clear words such as "kill yourself" or "end your life"; avoid euphemisms such as "do something silly".
- Interview the patient **and** obtain collateral history.

### Formulated questions

**Opening and rapport**

1. "I'm [name], an emergency care practitioner. I'm here to help you and to keep you safe. Can you tell me what has been happening today?"
2. "It sounds like things have been very difficult for you. How have you been feeling in yourself lately?"

**Mood and hopelessness**

3. "Have you been feeling sad, down or hopeless most of the time?"
4. "Do you feel that things will ever get better?"
5. "Have you lost interest in things you used to enjoy?"

**Passive and active suicidal ideation**

6. "Have you felt that life is not worth living, or wished you could go to sleep and not wake up?"
7. "Have you had thoughts of killing yourself or ending your life?"
8. "How often do these thoughts come? How long do they last? Can you control them?"

**Intent**

9. "Did you want to die when you [took the tablets / tied the rope]?"
10. "Do you still want to die now?"
11. "How do you feel about the fact that you are still alive?"

**Plan, method and preparation**

12. "Have you thought about how you would do it?"
13. "Do you have access to [tablets, poison, a firearm, a rope]? Where are they now?"
14. "Have you made any preparations, such as writing a note, giving away belongings or saying goodbye to people?"
15. "When were you planning to do it?"

**The current act (if an attempt has occurred)**

16. "What exactly did you take or do, how much, and at what time?"
17. "Was this planned, or did it happen on the spur of the moment?"
18. "Did you think it would kill you?"
19. "Did you do anything to avoid being found or stopped?"
20. "Did you leave a note or message for anyone?"
21. "Who found you, and how did that happen?"

**History**

22. "Have you ever tried to harm or kill yourself before? What happened?"
23. "Have you been diagnosed with any mental health condition, or are you taking any medication for your nerves or mood?"
24. "Do you drink alcohol or use any drugs? Have you used any today?"

**Psychotic symptoms**

25. "Are you hearing voices that other people can't hear? Are they telling you to do anything, such as harm yourself or someone else?"

**Harm to others**

26. "Have you had thoughts of harming anyone else?"

**Protective factors and support**

27. "What has stopped you from acting on these thoughts?"
28. "Who do you have to support you? Is there someone you trust whom we can contact?"
29. "What do you have to look forward to? Are there people who depend on you, such as children?"

**Collaboration**

30. "Would you be willing to come with us to the hospital so that a doctor can help you?"

### High-yield points — Outcome 45

- South Africa's suicide rate is 23.5/100,000 (WHO 2021 data); males are 5 times more likely to die by suicide; hanging is the most common method in men; poisoning/overdose in women; peak risk age 41 in the Garden Route data (Tilley et al., 2026).
- 80% of people who die by suicide previously stated their intent; 10% of those who threaten or attempt eventually die by suicide (Evans, Geduld and Stassen, 2018).
- Strongest predictor: previous attempt. Hopelessness, intent, planning, lethality, precautions against discovery, notes, regret at survival.
- Ask directly; asking does not increase risk.
- Tools (SAD PERSONS, C-SSRS) support but never replace clinical judgement.

## 45.1 Critically discuss the legal and ethical obligations of ECPs in the management of patients and scenarios where deliberate self-harm / suicide has occurred

### Legal obligations

**1. The duty of care and the duty to treat.** Once an ECP has responded and established a practitioner–patient relationship, a duty of care exists. Section 27(3) of the Constitution provides that no one may be refused emergency medical treatment. The ECP must provide care that meets the standard of a reasonable practitioner, and leaving an at-risk patient who lacks capacity may constitute negligence.

**2. Consent and capacity.** A competent adult may refuse treatment (NHA, s 7; HPCSA, 2023, para 8.1.2). However, the HPCSA qualifies this: a refusal must be respected "unless the patient's state of mind is such that he/she is incapable of taking or participating in the decision" (HPCSA, 2023, para 8.1.2). Patients who have attempted suicide "may have limited insight into their illness and a restricted ability to cooperate with treatment", which may require measures "that, in line with local legislation, restrict their personal freedom and allow transportation to hospital against their will" (Evans, Geduld and Stassen, 2018).

**3. The Mental Health Care Act.** Section 9(1)(c) permits a health care provider to provide care without consent where, **due to mental illness**, any delay may result in (i) death or irreversible harm to the health of the user, (ii) the user inflicting serious harm to themselves or others, or (iii) the user causing serious damage to or loss of property. The provider or establishment that does so must report this in writing to the Review Board and may not continue for longer than 24 hours unless an application under Chapter V is made within that period (MHCA, s 9(2)). Section 40 empowers and obliges SAPS to apprehend a person who, due to mental illness, is likely to inflict serious harm to self or others, and to take that person to a State health establishment for assessment. The formal involuntary application (s 33) requires a written application by a relative, partner, associate or guardian (or a health care provider if they are unwilling, incapable or unavailable) and examination by two mental health care practitioners, which "is not achievable at the scene" (Evans, Geduld and Stassen, 2018). The prehospital role is therefore emergency assessment and **transport for evaluation**.

**4. The CDST obligation to transport.** The provider is "obligated to transport" if the patient is a danger to themselves or may be a suicide risk, a danger to family/community, causing serious damage to property, if SAPS request hospitalisation, or if the patient has been sedated and needs monitoring (HPCSA PBEC, 2025, p. 48).

**5. Refusal of hospital treatment (RHT) forms.** An RHT form is valid only if signed by a **competent** patient who has been fully informed of the risks of refusal. A form signed by a suicidal patient who lacks capacity, or whose refusal is driven by a mental illness, does not absolve the provider from legal responsibility (Evans, Geduld and Stassen, 2018). One participant in that study commented that "more thought could be put towards actually helping the patient in need instead of only worrying about paperwork".

**6. Documentation.** The PRF must record the capacity assessment, the risk assessment, the legal basis for any action without consent, the people consulted, and all interventions (HPCSA, 2023, para 12).

**7. Confidentiality and disclosure.** Information about a suicide attempt is confidential (MHCA, s 13; NHA, s 14; POPIA). It may be shared with the receiving facility, and with family or SAPS to the extent necessary to protect the patient's life. If the patient is a child, information may be shared with parents or guardians in the child's best interests.

**8. Death by suicide.** A death by suicide is an **unnatural death** in terms of the Inquests Act 58 of 1959; it must be reported to SAPS, and the scene must be treated as a potential crime scene. The ECP should not move the body or disturb the scene unnecessarily once death has been confirmed, should preserve any note or ligature as found (cutting a ligature away from the knot if it must be removed for resuscitation), should document the position of the body and findings, and should hand over to SAPS. The body will undergo a medicolegal post-mortem in terms of the Inquests Act.

**9. Children and adolescents.** A child aged 12 or older who is of sufficient maturity may consent to medical treatment (Children's Act 38 of 2005, s 129(2)); younger children require parental consent. In an emergency, treatment necessary to preserve life may proceed (Children's Act, s 129(6); NHA, s 7(1)(e)). An involuntary MHCA application for a user under 18 must be made by a parent or guardian (MHCA, s 33(1)(a)(i)). Child abuse that precipitated the self-harm must be reported (Children's Act, s 110; SOA, s 54).

### Ethical obligations

1. **Beneficence and the duty to preserve life** — the ECP must act to prevent avoidable death. Because suicidal ideation is usually a symptom of a treatable mental disorder and is frequently transient, intervention is ethically justified.
2. **Respect for autonomy** — the patient must be involved in decisions as far as possible, informed, and treated as a person with dignity. Autonomy presupposes capacity; an impaired decision is not an autonomous one.
3. **Non-maleficence** — coercive measures (restraint, sedation, involuntary transport) must be proportionate and must not cause unnecessary harm or humiliation.
4. **Justice** — patients who self-harm must receive the same standard of care as other patients. Negative attitudes ("waste of time", "attention seeking", "chose to die") documented by Evans, Geduld and Stassen (2018) are ethically unacceptable and may breach the HPCSA requirement not to allow views about lifestyle or other irrational grounds to prejudice care (HPCSA, 2023, para 2.5).
5. **Fidelity and truth-telling** — do not deceive the patient.
6. **Care for the carer** — exposure to suicide is traumatic for providers, and services have an ethical obligation to provide debriefing and counselling (Evans, Geduld and Stassen, 2018; Tilley et al., 2026).

### The ethical tension in the HPCSA Guidelines for Withholding and Withdrawing Treatment (Booklet 7)

The Behavioural Emergencies Tutorial 1 asks you to critically assess the ethical tensions in the HPCSA guidelines when they are applied to suicidal patients. The key points are:

- **The guideline's context is terminal illness, not suicide.** It is intended to provide "an ethical framework of good practice for health practitioners in circumstances where they are faced with making a decision on whether to withhold or withdraw life-prolonging treatment" for patients "for whom there is little hope of recovery" (HPCSA, 2023, paras 2.1–2.2). It explicitly prohibits "killing, active euthanasia, and assisted suicide" (HPCSA, 2023, para 2.2), and its annexure (the WMA Declaration on Terminal Illness) states that "physicians are ethically prohibited from actively assisting patients in suicide".
- **Autonomy versus incapacity.** The guideline requires respect for a competent adult's refusal "even where this would result in serious harm to them or in their own death" (para 8.1.2), but with the explicit exception where the patient's "state of mind is such that he/she is incapable of taking or participating in the decision". In a suicidal patient, capacity is often impaired by depression, psychosis, intoxication or the acute crisis. Misapplying the autonomy principle (for example accepting a refusal because "the patient has chosen to die", as some providers in the Evans study believed) could result in avoidable death.
- **Emergencies.** Paragraph 7.1 states that, in an acute life-threatening emergency "where any delay might prejudice the outcome and where it is impossible to obtain all relevant information or hold any consultations required or where there is uncertainty about the diagnosis or the likelihood of recovery, health practitioners should start treatment which may be of some benefit to the patient until a clearer assessment can be made". This supports initiating treatment for a patient who has self-harmed, even when the patient's wishes are unclear. Paragraph 7.2 requires that the patient be informed as soon as they are mentally capable.
- **Advance directives.** The guideline gives weight to directives and living wills (paras 3.3, 9.1.4). A "directive" or note written by a person in a suicidal crisis is **not** an advance directive in this sense, because it was not made by a competent person in contemplation of terminal illness, and a directive "cannot compel the healthcare team to provide treatments that lie outside acceptable standards of care" (para 9.1.4).
- **Best interests and consultation.** The guideline emphasises consultation with colleagues, family and proxies, second opinions, and documentation (paras 3.5, 3.7, 5.2, 12). These are achievable prehospitally through on-line medical advice, consultation with family, and thorough PRF documentation — but time pressure, limited information and uncertainty make them difficult, which is a genuine tension.
- **Resource considerations.** The guideline permits limitation of treatment based on scarcity (para 10), but this can never justify withholding emergency care from a suicidal patient; at most it guides triage in mass-casualty situations.
- **Prolonging suffering versus relieving it.** In the rare case of a patient with a genuine terminal illness and a valid advance directive who has also self-harmed, the tension between preventing death and respecting a previously expressed wish is real; such cases require senior medical consultation, and the default in an emergency is to treat until a clearer assessment can be made (para 7.1).

### Critical discussion — conclusion

The law and ethics converge on a clear position: the ECP must take every suicide attempt seriously, assess and document capacity and risk, provide emergency treatment, and transport patients who are at risk or who lack capacity, using the least restrictive means and with SAPS assistance when needed. The South African evidence shows a gap between this position and practice, with many providers believing they cannot transport without consent, relying on refusal forms, holding negative attitudes, and receiving little training (Evans, Geduld and Stassen, 2018). Closing this gap requires training, clear protocols (the CDST obligation-to-transport criteria), and supportive systems.

### High-yield points — 45.1

- MHCA s 9(1)(c): care without consent due to mental illness if delay may cause death, serious harm to self/others, or serious property damage; report to Review Board; 24-hour limit.
- MHCA s 40: SAPS must apprehend and take to a State facility.
- CDST: obligated to transport if a suicide risk.
- RHT form signed by an incompetent patient has no legal value.
- HPCSA Booklet 7 is for terminal illness; it prohibits assisted suicide; para 7.1 supports emergency treatment under uncertainty.
- Death by suicide = unnatural death → SAPS, Inquests Act, preserve scene.

# Outcome 46. Assess prehospital management techniques for a patient who has attempted suicide

## Step-by-step prehospital management

### 1. Scene safety and size-up

- Look for hazards: weapons (knives, firearms), toxic gases (car exhaust — carbon monoxide; burning charcoal; fires in poorly ventilated rooms — the CDST advises checking for these and for cyanide or carbon monoxide poisoning; HPCSA PBEC, 2025, p. 54), chemicals and pesticides (organophosphates require PPE because of secondary contamination), electricity, heights, railway lines, water.
- Request SAPS (always for a firearm, an ongoing threat, or a death), and fire/rescue for heights, entrapment, or hazardous materials.
- Look for clues: empty pill containers, suicide notes, ligatures, alcohol, drug paraphernalia — take containers and tablets with you to hospital (HPCSA PBEC, 2025, p. 55).

### 2. Primary survey and resuscitation, according to the method

- **Hanging/strangulation** (the most common method in the Garden Route data, accounting for 82 of 102 deaths and 51 of 83 attempts; Tilley et al., 2026): cut the ligature away from the knot to preserve it as evidence, support the body while cutting it down, assume possible cervical spine injury (particularly with a drop), manage the airway (laryngeal and tracheal injury, airway oedema), give high-flow oxygen, look for petechiae and neurological deficit, and anticipate seizures, pulmonary oedema and hypoxic brain injury.
- **Overdose and self-poisoning** (52% of EMS mental-health incidents and the most common method in women; Tilley et al., 2026): follow the CDST Poisoning tool (Outcome 62): ABCDE, oxygen if SpO₂ <92%, RR >30 or <9, or GCS ≤14; airway management; glucose; IV access and fluids; identify the toxidrome; give specific antidotes within scope (for example naloxone, atropine, sodium bicarbonate); and give activated charcoal **only** if the patient is fully conscious and has taken an oral overdose of a harmful substance within the past hour, and the substance is suitable (HPCSA PBEC, 2025, pp. 54–57).
- **Cutting and stabbing**: haemorrhage control (direct pressure, tourniquets for life-threatening limb haemorrhage), shock management, and assessment for deeper injury (tendons, nerves, vessels, penetrating chest or abdominal injury).
- **Jumping from height or in front of a vehicle/train**: major trauma management with spinal precautions.
- **Firearm**: major trauma management; SAPS scene.
- **Drowning, burns, electrocution**: manage according to the relevant CDST tool.

### 3. Psychological care during treatment

- Talk to the patient throughout, explaining what you are doing; be calm, compassionate and non-judgemental.
- Avoid comments that blame or minimise ("Why would you do something so stupid?").
- Do not leave the patient alone at any time, including in the bathroom.
- Remove all potential means of further self-harm from the patient and the environment (sharp objects, medication, ligatures, belts, shoelaces where appropriate).

### 4. Risk assessment and capacity assessment

Using the questions in Outcome 45, assess intent, plan, lethality and ongoing risk, and assess capacity to refuse care. Document both.

### 5. Disposition and transport

- **All patients who have attempted suicide should be transported** for medical and psychiatric assessment. Even an apparently minor overdose may be life-threatening (for example paracetamol, which causes delayed liver failure after an asymptomatic period, or TCAs, which cause rapid deterioration), and the act itself indicates significant risk.
- If the patient refuses: attempt persuasion and involve family; if the patient lacks capacity or meets the MHCA s 9(1)(c) criteria, or meets the CDST obligation-to-transport criteria (danger to self or suicide risk), the patient must be transported, using the least restrictive means, with SAPS assistance and sedation per the CDST if necessary (HPCSA PBEC, 2025, pp. 46–48).
- Destination: an **emergency centre** first, because medical clearance is required before psychiatric admission.
- During transport: continuous observation, monitoring every 10 minutes (more often if unstable or sedated), the attending provider seated near the door, doors secured to prevent the patient from jumping out, and SAPS escort if high risk.
- Bring family if possible, as well as any notes, tablets and containers.

### 6. Handover

Hand over the method, time, quantity, the circumstances, the risk assessment, capacity, the legal basis for transport, interventions and response, and collateral information.

### 7. After the call

Debrief with your partner; seek support if the incident was distressing.

## Assessment (critical evaluation) of these techniques

- **Strengths**: a structured approach ensures that physical life threats are treated first, that psychiatric risk is assessed, and that transport decisions are lawful.
- **Gaps in South African practice**: few providers use formal suicide evaluation tools; transport decisions are influenced by how "dramatic" the presentation appears; decision-making is frequently shifted to family or seniors; less than half of respondents would transport a refusing patient; and patients have died after being left on scene (Evans, Geduld and Stassen, 2018).
- **Evidence for follow-up**: patients who receive active suicide prevention contact and follow-up after a suicide attempt have a reduced risk of repeat attempts over the following year (Evans, Geduld and Stassen, 2018, citing Inagaki et al., 2014). EMS is therefore a crucial link in the chain to mental health care, and "to be left alone is like saying nobody cares" (participant quoted in Evans, Geduld and Stassen, 2018).
- **Means restriction**: limiting access to methods is one of the most effective suicide prevention interventions (Tilley et al., 2026, citing Florentine and Crane, 2010). ECPs can contribute by removing means on scene and advising families (for example locking away medication and pesticides and removing firearms).

### High-yield points — Outcome 46

- Treat the physical injury first (method-specific), then assess risk and capacity.
- Never leave the patient alone; remove means.
- Transport every suicide attempt to an emergency centre; obligated to transport if at risk (CDST).
- Preserve ligatures, notes and containers; cut ligatures away from the knot.

# Outcome 47. Discuss the management of patient abscondment in the emergency care setting

## Definition

**Abscondment** is the unauthorised departure of a patient from care — for example running away from the scene, leaving the ambulance, or leaving the emergency centre — when the patient is at risk and should not leave. In the MHCA, the term is used for an **assisted or involuntary mental health care user** who leaves a health establishment without authorisation. Section 40(4) of the MHCA states that if such a user "has absconded or is deemed to have absconded", the head of the health establishment may request SAPS assistance to "locate, apprehend and return the user to the health establishment concerned"; SAPS "must comply with the request" (s 40(5)); SAPS must be informed of "the estimated level of dangerousness" of the user (s 40(6)); and SAPS may use "such constraining measures as may be necessary and proportionate in the circumstances" (s 40(8)). The prescribed form for notifying SAPS of an abscondment is Form MHCA 25 (Mental Health Care Regulations, 2004).

## 47.1 Reflect on the factors the paramedic must consider when assessing a patient's risk of abscondment in a behavioural emergency

### Patient factors

- **Psychiatric state**: psychosis (persecutory delusions that the crew intend harm), mania, agitation, delirium, intoxication or withdrawal.
- **Lack of insight** into illness and the need for care.
- **Fear** — of hospitalisation, of being "locked up", of SAPS, of stigma; previous negative experiences of psychiatric admission or police restraint (Evans, Geduld and Stassen, 2018).
- **Expressed intent to leave**, refusal of care, or repeated questions about when they can go.
- **Previous abscondment** or previous involuntary admission.
- **Active suicidal intent** — the patient may abscond to complete suicide.
- **Substance craving or withdrawal**.
- **Young age** (adolescents), and dementia with wandering behaviour in older persons.
- **Responsibilities or concerns** (children at home, pets, work, fear of losing a job).

### Environmental and situational factors

- An unsecured scene with open exits, crowds, or proximity to roads, railways or water.
- Delays on scene or long waiting times at the facility (Evans, Geduld and Stassen, 2018, noted long waits as a frustration for providers).
- Inadequate staff numbers or the absence of SAPS.
- An unlocked ambulance, or the patient seated near the door.
- Lack of family support, or family who encourage refusal.

### Provider factors

- Confrontational or dismissive communication.
- Deception that is discovered (Stander, Hodkinson and Dippenaar, 2021).
- Failure to explain what will happen.
- Leaving the patient unsupervised.

### Consequences of abscondment

The consequences are serious: suicide or self-harm (Evans, Geduld and Stassen, 2018, describe a patient jumping from a moving ambulance), injury in traffic, deterioration of an untreated medical condition (for example an overdose), harm to others, and legal and disciplinary consequences for the provider. Patients who leave against advice have poorer psychiatric and psychosocial outcomes, overuse emergency services, are readmitted sooner and have a higher risk of suicide (Evans, Geduld and Stassen, 2018, citing Brook et al., 2006, and Kuo et al., 2010).

## 47.2 Formulate prehospital management techniques for a patient in a behavioural emergency who attempts abscondment

### Prevention

1. **Assess risk early** using the factors above, and plan accordingly.
2. **Build rapport**: calm, honest, respectful communication; explain what will happen and why; listen to concerns; offer choices; involve trusted family (RCEM, 2025; HPCSA PBEC, 2025).
3. **Address practical concerns** (for example arranging care for children or pets, informing an employer).
4. **Minimise time on scene** once the decision to transport has been made.
5. **Position** yourself between the patient and the exit without making the patient feel trapped, keep the patient within sight at all times, and never leave a high-risk patient alone.
6. **Secure the environment**: close doors; in the ambulance, secure the side and rear doors from the inside where possible; the attending provider sits closest to the door; the patient is secured on the stretcher with the seatbelts.
7. **Request SAPS** early for high-risk patients, and have SAPS escort the ambulance or travel in the patient compartment.
8. **Offer voluntary sedation** for transfer where indicated (CDST).

### When the patient attempts to leave

1. **Do not physically chase the patient into danger** (for example onto a highway), and do not endanger yourself or your crew.
2. **Attempt verbal de-escalation** immediately; ask the patient to stop and talk.
3. If the patient **has capacity** and does not meet MHCA s 9(1)(c) or CDST obligation-to-transport criteria, the patient may lawfully leave; explain the risks, offer alternatives, document the refusal on an RHT form, advise the patient to call back at any time, and inform family where appropriate.
4. If the patient **lacks capacity or is a danger to self or others**: notify SAPS immediately (giving a description of the patient, clothing, direction of travel, the level of risk and dangerousness); SAPS has the authority and the duty under MHCA s 40(1) to apprehend a person who is likely to inflict serious harm to themselves or others due to mental illness; notify your control centre; if safe and practicable, keep the patient in sight from a safe distance; once the patient is apprehended, proceed with assessment, sedation per the CDST if necessary, and transport.
5. **Restraint and sedation** may be used in accordance with the CDST protocol (Outcome 43.3) if the patient is contained and remains dangerous.
6. **If the patient absconds from a health establishment**, it is the responsibility of the head of that establishment to request SAPS assistance (MHCA s 40(4); Form MHCA 25). EMS may be requested to assist with the transfer back.

### Documentation

Document the risk assessment, capacity assessment, the attempts to persuade, the time and circumstances of abscondment, the direction of travel, who was notified (SAPS, control, family, facility), and the outcome.

### High-yield points — Outcome 47

- MHCA s 40(4): head of establishment may request SAPS to locate, apprehend and return an absconded assisted or involuntary user; SAPS must comply; Form MHCA 25.
- Risk factors: psychosis, lack of insight, fear, intoxication, suicidal intent, previous abscondment, unsecured environment, long delays.
- Prevent: rapport, honesty, supervision, secure doors, SAPS, voluntary sedation.
- Do not chase into danger; notify SAPS with description and risk level; document.
