# Outcome 52. Discuss disorders listed in the DSM-5 relevant to emergency care practice

The *Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders*, fifth edition, text revision (DSM-5-TR), published by the American Psychiatric Association (APA) in 2022, is the current classification. For each disorder below you will find the core diagnostic features (APA, 2022; Boland, Verduin and Ruiz, 2021), the emergency presentations and risks, and the South African prehospital management. You are not expected to diagnose psychiatric disorders in the field, but you must recognise them, understand their risks, and manage the emergency safely.

## 52.1 Neurodevelopmental disorders

Neurodevelopmental disorders have their onset in the developmental period, typically before school entry, and produce deficits in personal, social, academic or occupational functioning (APA, 2022).

### 52.1.1 Intellectual disabilities (intellectual developmental disorder)

**Core features.** Three criteria must be met: (A) deficits in intellectual functions — reasoning, problem solving, planning, abstract thinking, judgement, academic learning and learning from experience — confirmed by clinical assessment and standardised testing; (B) deficits in **adaptive functioning** that result in failure to meet developmental and sociocultural standards for personal independence and social responsibility, in the conceptual, social and practical domains; and (C) onset during the developmental period (APA, 2022). Severity (mild, moderate, severe, profound) is based on **adaptive functioning**, not IQ alone. Causes include genetic conditions (for example Down syndrome, fragile X syndrome), **fetal alcohol spectrum disorder** (South Africa, particularly the Western Cape, has some of the highest reported rates of fetal alcohol spectrum disorder in the world), perinatal hypoxia, infections (for example congenital infections, meningitis), malnutrition and traumatic brain injury.

**Emergency relevance.**

- Difficulty communicating pain or symptoms, so that **medical illness presents as behavioural change** (constipation, dental pain, urinary tract infection, otitis, fractures, seizures).
- Higher rates of epilepsy, which may cause post-ictal behaviour.
- Vulnerability to abuse, exploitation and sexual assault (SOA s 54 duty to report applies to persons with mental disability).
- Distress from a change in routine or environment.
- The term "severe or profound intellectual disability" appears in the MHCA (for example s 40), which means that the involuntary and assisted pathways can apply.
- Capacity to consent varies and must be assessed for each decision.

**Prehospital management.** Speak to the caregiver and consult any care plan; use simple language, visual cues and a calm, unhurried approach; allow the caregiver to accompany the patient; look carefully for a medical cause and pain; minimise restraint; use CDST sedation only if necessary, remembering that paradoxical reactions to benzodiazepines are more common; the RCEM (2025) notes that "the differential diagnosis in a patient with learning difficulties is less likely to feature stimulant drugs", and recommends that "efforts should be made to have someone known to the patient communicate with them".

### 52.1.2 Communication disorders

**Core features.** Communication disorders include **language disorder** (persistent difficulties in acquiring and using language — reduced vocabulary, limited sentence structure, impaired discourse), **speech sound disorder** (difficulty producing speech sounds), **childhood-onset fluency disorder (stuttering)**, and **social (pragmatic) communication disorder** (difficulty with the social use of verbal and non-verbal communication, without the restricted and repetitive behaviours of autism) (APA, 2022).

**Emergency relevance.** Patients may be unable to explain symptoms, may become frustrated and agitated when not understood, and may be mistaken for being confused, intoxicated or uncooperative. Stuttering worsens under stress. Acquired communication problems (aphasia after stroke, dysarthria from intoxication or neurological disease) must be distinguished from developmental disorders — new-onset speech disturbance is a stroke until proven otherwise.

**Prehospital management.** Allow extra time; ask closed (yes/no) questions; use gestures, pictures, writing or communication devices; do not finish the patient's sentences; involve caregivers; do not interpret communication difficulty as lack of capacity without proper assessment; and use the FAST assessment for any new speech difficulty.

### 52.1.3 Autism spectrum disorder (ASD)

**Core features.** (A) Persistent deficits in **social communication and social interaction** across multiple contexts — deficits in social-emotional reciprocity, in non-verbal communication (eye contact, gestures, facial expressions), and in developing, maintaining and understanding relationships; and (B) **restricted, repetitive patterns of behaviour, interests or activities** — stereotyped or repetitive movements or speech (for example echolalia, hand-flapping, rocking), insistence on sameness and inflexible adherence to routines, highly restricted fixated interests, and **hyper- or hypo-reactivity to sensory input** (for example distress at noise, lights or touch, apparent indifference to pain); with onset in early development and clinically significant impairment (APA, 2022). Severity is graded in three levels (requiring support, substantial support, very substantial support), with or without accompanying intellectual or language impairment (Lord et al., 2018).

**Emergency relevance.**

- **"Meltdowns"** (involuntary responses to overwhelming sensory or emotional overload) may look like aggression or tantrums; they are not deliberate.
- Sirens, flashing lights, uniforms, touch, strangers and the confined ambulance are powerful triggers.
- Altered pain response — a serious injury may not be reported.
- Self-injurious behaviour (head-banging, biting).
- High rates of epilepsy, anxiety, ADHD and gastrointestinal problems.
- Wandering ("elopement") and drowning risk in children.
- Adults with ASD have increased rates of depression and suicidality.

**Prehospital management.** Reduce sensory stimulation (switch off sirens and lights near the patient, dim lights, speak quietly, limit the number of people); let the caregiver lead and stay with the patient; explain each step in concrete, literal language and allow processing time; avoid light touch and warn before touching; allow comfort objects, headphones or stimming behaviours; avoid restraint where possible (it may escalate fear); examine carefully for injury and pain; use CDST sedation only if necessary.

### 52.1.4 Motor disorders

**Core features.** Motor disorders include **developmental coordination disorder** (motor skills substantially below expected for age), **stereotypic movement disorder** (repetitive, seemingly driven, purposeless motor behaviour such as body-rocking, head-banging or self-biting, which may cause injury), and **tic disorders** — **Tourette's disorder** (multiple motor tics and at least one vocal tic present for more than one year, with onset before age 18), persistent (chronic) motor or vocal tic disorder, and provisional tic disorder (APA, 2022).

**Emergency relevance.** Self-injury from stereotypic movements; tics (including coprolalia — involuntary obscene utterances — in a minority with Tourette's) may be misinterpreted as intoxication, aggression or deliberate provocation; tics worsen with stress. Motor disorders must also be distinguished from **acute dystonia** (antipsychotics, metoclopramide), akathisia, chorea, seizures, and stimulant-induced movements.

**Prehospital management.** Reassure the patient and bystanders, protect from injury, do not punish or restrain tics, reduce stress, and look for an acute medical cause of any new movement disorder (for example a drug-induced dystonia, which responds to an anticholinergic such as biperiden or benztropine in hospital).

## 52.2 Psychotic disorders

**Psychosis** refers to a loss of contact with reality characterised by one or more of: **delusions, hallucinations, disorganised thinking (speech), grossly disorganised or abnormal motor behaviour (including catatonia), and negative symptoms** (diminished emotional expression and avolition) (APA, 2022).

### 52.2.1 Schizophrenia and its spectrum

**Schizophrenia — DSM-5-TR criteria.** (A) Two or more of the following, each present for a significant portion of time during a one-month period (or less if successfully treated), **at least one of which must be (1), (2) or (3)**: (1) delusions; (2) hallucinations; (3) disorganised speech; (4) grossly disorganised or catatonic behaviour; (5) negative symptoms. (B) Functioning in work, interpersonal relations or self-care is markedly below the level achieved before onset. (C) Continuous signs of disturbance persist for **at least six months**, including at least one month of active-phase symptoms (which may include prodromal or residual periods). (D) Schizoaffective disorder and depressive or bipolar disorder with psychotic features have been ruled out. (E) The disturbance is not attributable to a substance or another medical condition (APA, 2022).

**The schizophrenia spectrum** includes:

- **schizotypal personality disorder** (see 52.10);
- **delusional disorder** — one or more delusions for at least one month, without other prominent psychotic symptoms and with relatively preserved functioning;
- **brief psychotic disorder** — psychotic symptoms lasting at least one day but less than one month, with full return to premorbid functioning, often after a marked stressor or postpartum;
- **schizophreniform disorder** — as for schizophrenia, but lasting at least one month and less than six months;
- **schizoaffective disorder** — a major mood episode concurrent with schizophrenia criterion A, plus delusions or hallucinations for two weeks or more without a mood episode;
- **substance/medication-induced psychotic disorder** and **psychotic disorder due to another medical condition** (below);
- **catatonia** (52.3).

**Epidemiology and pathophysiology.** Lifetime prevalence is about 0.3–0.7%; onset is typically in late adolescence to the mid-30s, slightly earlier in men; the dopamine hypothesis (excess mesolimbic dopamine producing positive symptoms) underpins antipsychotic treatment, and cannabis use, urbanicity, migration, childhood adversity and obstetric complications are risk factors (McCutcheon, Reis Marques and Howes, 2020).

**Emergency presentations and risks.**

- Agitation and aggression driven by persecutory delusions or command hallucinations.
- **Suicide** — lifetime suicide risk is substantially elevated (about 5%), especially in young men early in the illness, after discharge, and with depression, command hallucinations or substance use.
- Self-neglect, malnutrition, exposure.
- **Medication adverse effects**: acute dystonia, akathisia, parkinsonism, **neuroleptic malignant syndrome** (hyperthermia, rigidity, altered consciousness, autonomic instability, raised CK), QT prolongation, clozapine-associated agranulocytosis (infection) and myocarditis, metabolic syndrome, and anticholinergic toxicity.
- Relapse due to non-adherence, substance use (cannabis, methamphetamine) or stress.
- Victimisation and comorbid physical illness (higher rates of cardiovascular disease, diabetes, HIV, TB).

**Prehospital management.** Scene safety; calm, honest de-escalation without challenging delusions (HPCSA PBEC, 2025, p. 46); ask about command hallucinations and suicidal and homicidal intent; exclude organic causes (glucose, SpO₂, temperature, head injury, intoxication); check for medication side effects (dystonia, NMS); CDST sedation if dangerous; MHCA s 9(1)(c) and s 40 if the patient lacks capacity and is at risk; transport to an emergency centre.

### 52.2.2 Substance/medication-induced psychotic disorder

**Core features.** Prominent delusions and/or hallucinations that develop **during or soon after** substance intoxication or withdrawal or after exposure to a medication, where the substance is capable of producing the symptoms and the disturbance is not better explained by an independent psychotic disorder (APA, 2022). Evidence of an independent disorder would include symptoms preceding substance use, persisting for a substantial period (about a month) after cessation of withdrawal or intoxication, or a history of non-substance-related episodes.

**Common causes.**

- **Stimulants**: methamphetamine ("tik" — a major problem in the Western Cape), cocaine, methcathinone ("cat").
- **Cannabis**, particularly high-potency products and synthetic cannabinoids.
- **Hallucinogens** (LSD, psilocybin mushrooms), **phencyclidine** and **ketamine**.
- **Alcohol** — alcoholic hallucinosis and delirium tremens (withdrawal).
- **Sedative-hypnotic withdrawal**.
- **Anticholinergic** agents (including *Datura stramonium*).
- **Medications**: corticosteroids, levodopa and dopamine agonists, **efavirenz** (an ARV historically widely used in South Africa, associated with vivid dreams, insomnia and occasional psychosis), isoniazid, anticholinergics, some antimalarials (mefloquine), and others.

**Emergency relevance.** Stimulant psychosis is frequently accompanied by **sympathomimetic toxicity** (tachycardia, hypertension, hyperthermia, seizures, arrhythmias, stroke, rhabdomyolysis) and may progress to **acute behavioural disturbance** with a risk of sudden death (RCEM, 2025). Tactile hallucinations (formication) and paranoia are typical.

**Prehospital management.** As for the sympathomimetic toxidrome (Outcome 60.1): de-escalation, early sedation per CDST (benzodiazepines are preferred because they treat both the agitation and the sympathomimetic toxicity), cooling if hyperthermic (CDST: remove clothing, spray water, fan or air-conditioning if temperature ≥38 °C; HPCSA PBEC, 2025, p. 57), aspirin, benzodiazepine and nitrates for stimulant chest pain within scope (HPCSA PBEC, 2025, p. 57), ECG, glucose, and transport.

### 52.2.3 Psychosis due to another medical condition

**Core features.** Prominent hallucinations or delusions, with evidence from the history, examination or investigations that the disturbance is the **direct pathophysiological consequence of another medical condition**, which is not better explained by another mental disorder and does not occur exclusively during delirium (APA, 2022).

**Causes.**

- **Neurological**: epilepsy (especially temporal lobe), brain tumours, stroke, head injury, Parkinson's disease and Lewy body dementia, Huntington's disease, multiple sclerosis.
- **Infections**: HIV (HIV-associated psychosis and opportunistic CNS infections), neurosyphilis, encephalitis (including autoimmune anti-NMDA receptor encephalitis, particularly in young women), cerebral malaria, sepsis.
- **Endocrine and metabolic**: hyperthyroidism and hypothyroidism, Cushing's syndrome, hypo- and hyperglycaemia, hypo- and hypercalcaemia, hyponatraemia, hepatic and uraemic encephalopathy, porphyria, vitamin B12 and thiamine deficiency (Wernicke–Korsakoff), pellagra (niacin deficiency).
- **Autoimmune**: systemic lupus erythematosus.
- **Postpartum** (postpartum psychosis — a psychiatric emergency with a risk of suicide and infanticide).

**Clues.** Late onset (>40 years), sudden onset, visual/tactile/olfactory hallucinations, altered consciousness, abnormal vital signs, focal neurological signs, and the absence of a psychiatric history.

**Prehospital management.** Identify and treat what you can (hypoglycaemia, hypoxia, seizures), protect the patient, sedate per CDST only if dangerous, and transport to an emergency centre for medical work-up.

## 52.3 Catatonia and its associated disorders and presentations

**Definition.** Catatonia is a marked psychomotor disturbance. DSM-5-TR requires **three or more of twelve features**: (1) **stupor** (no psychomotor activity; not actively relating to the environment); (2) **catalepsy** (passive induction of a posture held against gravity); (3) **waxy flexibility** (slight, even resistance to positioning by the examiner); (4) **mutism**; (5) **negativism** (opposition or no response to instructions or external stimuli); (6) **posturing** (spontaneous, active maintenance of a posture against gravity); (7) **mannerism** (odd caricature of normal actions); (8) **stereotypy** (repetitive, abnormally frequent, non-goal-directed movements); (9) **agitation** not influenced by external stimuli; (10) **grimacing**; (11) **echolalia**; (12) **echopraxia** (APA, 2022).

**Associated disorders.** In DSM-5-TR, catatonia may be specified as:

- **catatonia associated with another mental disorder** — most commonly **bipolar disorder and major depression** (more often than schizophrenia), schizophrenia, brief psychotic disorder, autism and post-traumatic states;
- **catatonic disorder due to another medical condition** — for example encephalitis (including anti-NMDA receptor encephalitis), non-convulsive status epilepticus, metabolic disorders (hyponatraemia, hepatic encephalopathy), head injury, and HIV-related disease;
- **unspecified catatonia**;
- **substance/medication-related catatonia**, including withdrawal from benzodiazepines.

**Malignant (lethal) catatonia** is catatonia with autonomic instability and hyperthermia, which can be fatal and closely resembles **neuroleptic malignant syndrome** (NMS is regarded by many as a drug-induced form of malignant catatonia).

**Emergency risks.** Dehydration, malnutrition, aspiration pneumonia, venous thromboembolism (pulmonary embolism), pressure injuries, contractures, rhabdomyolysis and acute kidney injury, and death in malignant catatonia. Excited catatonia may produce sudden violent agitation.

**Recognition.** The Bush–Francis Catatonia Rating Scale is a standard instrument (Bush et al., 1996). A useful bedside clue is a patient who is mute and immobile with eyes open, resisting passive movement or holding unusual postures, and who does not respond to commands, but who has no focal neurological deficit.

**Management.**

- **Prehospital**: ABCDE (protect the airway, risk of aspiration), glucose, temperature (hyperthermia suggests malignant catatonia/NMS), assess hydration, look for medical causes and recent antipsychotic exposure, IV fluids if dehydrated, pressure-area protection, transport.
- **Hospital**: a **lorazepam challenge** (for example 1–2 mg IV, with improvement within minutes to hours supporting the diagnosis) and ongoing benzodiazepines are first-line; **electroconvulsive therapy (ECT)** is highly effective for benzodiazepine-resistant and malignant catatonia; **antipsychotics should be avoided** in the acute phase because they may precipitate NMS (Boland, Verduin and Ruiz, 2021).

## 52.4 Bipolar disorders

**Definitions (DSM-5-TR).**

- **Manic episode**: a distinct period of abnormally and persistently elevated, expansive or irritable mood **and** abnormally and persistently increased activity or energy, lasting **at least one week** and present most of the day, nearly every day (or any duration if hospitalisation is necessary), with **three or more** of the following (**four** if the mood is only irritable): (1) inflated self-esteem or grandiosity; (2) decreased need for sleep; (3) more talkative than usual or pressure to keep talking; (4) flight of ideas or racing thoughts; (5) distractibility; (6) increased goal-directed activity or psychomotor agitation; (7) excessive involvement in activities with a high potential for painful consequences (spending sprees, sexual indiscretions, foolish investments). The episode causes marked impairment, necessitates hospitalisation, or has psychotic features, and is not due to a substance or medical condition (APA, 2022). The mnemonic **DIGFAST** (Distractibility, Impulsivity/Indiscretion, Grandiosity, Flight of ideas, Activity increased, Sleep decreased, Talkativeness) is useful.
- **Hypomanic episode**: the same symptoms lasting **at least four consecutive days**, with an observable change in functioning but **not** severe enough to cause marked impairment or require hospitalisation, and **without** psychotic features.
- **Major depressive episode**: see 52.5.1.

**Bipolar I disorder** requires **at least one manic episode**; hypomanic and major depressive episodes may occur but are not required. **Bipolar II disorder** requires **at least one hypomanic episode and at least one major depressive episode**, and **no manic episode ever**. **Cyclothymic disorder** involves at least two years (one year in children and adolescents) of numerous periods of hypomanic and depressive symptoms that do not meet full criteria for an episode (APA, 2022). Bipolar disorder may also be substance/medication-induced (for example corticosteroids, stimulants, antidepressant-induced switching) or due to a medical condition (for example hyperthyroidism).

**Clinical implications.** Bipolar I tends to have more severe manic phases with a higher risk of psychosis and functional impairment; bipolar II is often dominated by depressive episodes. Antidepressants used without a mood stabiliser can precipitate mania or rapid cycling. Treatment includes mood stabilisers (**lithium**, valproate, carbamazepine, lamotrigine) and atypical antipsychotics, plus psychotherapy (Vieta et al., 2018; Tutorial 1 memo).

**Emergency presentations and risks.**

- Manic agitation, aggression, disinhibition, reckless behaviour (traffic accidents, sexual risk, assault), exhaustion, dehydration.
- **Suicide** — bipolar disorder carries one of the highest suicide risks of any psychiatric disorder, especially in depressive and mixed episodes.
- **Lithium toxicity** (Outcome 60.27) — narrow therapeutic index; precipitated by dehydration, renal impairment, NSAIDs, ACE inhibitors and diuretics.
- **Valproate toxicity** (CNS depression, hyperammonaemia) and **carbamazepine toxicity** (ataxia, nystagmus, coma, seizures, cardiac conduction effects).
- Psychotic features.

**Prehospital management.** Low-stimulus environment, brief and firm communication, avoid arguing, set limits; exclude stimulant intoxication, thyrotoxicosis and medication toxicity; ask about lithium and check for toxicity signs (tremor, vomiting, diarrhoea, ataxia, confusion); CDST sedation if dangerous; assess suicide risk in depressive or mixed states; transport.

## 52.5 Mood disorders

### 52.5.1 Depressive disorders

**Major depressive disorder (MDD) — DSM-5-TR criteria.** **Five or more** of the following during the same **two-week** period, representing a change from previous functioning, with **at least one being (1) depressed mood or (2) loss of interest or pleasure (anhedonia)**: (1) depressed mood most of the day, nearly every day; (2) markedly diminished interest or pleasure in all or almost all activities; (3) significant weight loss or gain, or change in appetite; (4) insomnia or hypersomnia; (5) psychomotor agitation or retardation (observable by others); (6) fatigue or loss of energy; (7) feelings of worthlessness or excessive or inappropriate guilt; (8) diminished ability to think or concentrate, or indecisiveness; (9) recurrent thoughts of death, recurrent suicidal ideation, a suicide attempt or a specific plan (APA, 2022). Symptoms cause clinically significant distress or impairment, are not due to a substance or medical condition, and there has never been a manic or hypomanic episode. The mnemonic **SIG E CAPS** (Sleep, Interest, Guilt, Energy, Concentration, Appetite, Psychomotor, Suicidality) plus depressed mood is useful.

**Other depressive disorders.**

- **Persistent depressive disorder (dysthymia)**: depressed mood for most of the day, for more days than not, for **at least two years** (one year in children).
- **Premenstrual dysphoric disorder**.
- **Disruptive mood dysregulation disorder** (children 6–18 years with severe recurrent temper outbursts and persistently irritable mood).
- **Substance/medication-induced depressive disorder** (for example alcohol, benzodiazepine withdrawal, corticosteroids, interferon, efavirenz) and **depressive disorder due to another medical condition** (hypothyroidism, stroke, Parkinson's disease, cancer, HIV).
- Specifiers include **with psychotic features** (often mood-congruent nihilistic or guilty delusions), **with peripartum onset**, with melancholic features, with anxious distress, and with catatonia.

**Emergency relevance.** **Suicide and self-harm** (the major risk); self-neglect, dehydration and malnutrition; psychotic depression; depressive stupor; overdose of antidepressants (**tricyclic antidepressants are highly dangerous in overdose** — Outcome 60.18; SSRIs can cause serotonin syndrome); and the **hypoactive delirium/dementia mimic** in older people (pseudodementia). Depression is common in South Africa and is closely linked to poverty, trauma, HIV and substance use (Tilley et al., 2026).

**Prehospital management.** Empathic communication; **always ask about suicidal ideation, intent and plan** (Outcome 45); remove means; look for overdose and self-injury; never leave the patient alone; transport if at risk (CDST; MHCA s 9(1)(c)). Treatment in hospital and the community includes psychotherapy (CBT) and antidepressants (SSRIs first-line) (Malhi and Mann, 2018).

### 52.5.2 Anxiety disorders

*(Note: in DSM-5-TR, anxiety disorders form their own chapter rather than being "mood disorders", but they are grouped here as your study guide lists them.)*

**Core features.** Anxiety disorders share **excessive fear** (an emotional response to a real or perceived imminent threat) and **anxiety** (anticipation of a future threat), with related behavioural disturbances such as avoidance (APA, 2022; Craske and Stein, 2016).

| Disorder | Key DSM-5-TR features |
|---|---|
| **Generalised anxiety disorder (GAD)** | Excessive anxiety and worry about a number of events or activities, more days than not for **at least six months**, which the person finds difficult to control, with **three or more** of: restlessness, being easily fatigued, difficulty concentrating, irritability, muscle tension, sleep disturbance |
| **Panic disorder** | **Recurrent unexpected panic attacks**, with at least one month of persistent concern about further attacks or their consequences, or a significant maladaptive change in behaviour (for example avoidance) |
| **Panic attack** (a specifier, not a disorder) | An abrupt surge of intense fear or discomfort that **peaks within minutes**, with **four or more** of: palpitations/tachycardia; sweating; trembling; shortness of breath; feelings of choking; chest pain; nausea/abdominal distress; dizziness/light-headedness; chills or heat sensations; paraesthesias; derealisation or depersonalisation; fear of losing control or "going crazy"; fear of dying |
| **Agoraphobia** | Marked fear or anxiety about two or more of: public transport, open spaces, enclosed places, standing in line or being in a crowd, being outside the home alone — because escape might be difficult or help unavailable |
| **Specific phobia** | Marked fear or anxiety about a specific object or situation (for example needles, blood, heights, animals), which is out of proportion to the actual danger and is actively avoided, for six months or more. The **blood-injection-injury type** can cause **vasovagal syncope** |
| **Social anxiety disorder** | Marked fear of social situations in which the person may be scrutinised by others, with fear of negative evaluation |
| **Separation anxiety disorder** and **selective mutism** | Mainly in children |
| **Substance/medication-induced anxiety** and **anxiety due to another medical condition** | For example caffeine, stimulants, alcohol or benzodiazepine withdrawal; hyperthyroidism, phaeochromocytoma, hypoglycaemia, arrhythmias, asthma, PE |

Obsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD), which the Tutorial 1 memo discusses alongside anxiety disorders, is classified in DSM-5-TR in a separate chapter of obsessive-compulsive and related disorders. OCD consists of **obsessions** (recurrent, intrusive, unwanted thoughts, urges or images causing marked anxiety, which the person tries to suppress or neutralise) and/or **compulsions** (repetitive behaviours or mental acts that the person feels driven to perform in response to an obsession or according to rigid rules), which are time-consuming (more than one hour a day) or cause significant distress or impairment, with insight specified as good/fair, poor, or absent/delusional (APA, 2022).

**Emergency relevance.**

- **Panic attacks mimic life-threatening conditions** (ACS, PE, asthma, arrhythmias, hypoglycaemia, thyrotoxicosis, stimulant toxicity) — panic is a diagnosis of exclusion.
- Hyperventilation causes respiratory alkalosis with perioral and carpopedal paraesthesia and carpopedal spasm.
- Anxiety disorders are associated with suicidal ideation, particularly with comorbid depression or substance use.
- Benzodiazepine dependence and withdrawal.
- Needle phobia and claustrophobia may complicate procedures; OCD rituals may interfere with care (Tutorial 1 memo).

**Prehospital management.** Full assessment including vital signs, SpO₂, glucose and a 12-lead ECG; calm reassurance; coached slow breathing; remove from the stressor; avoid paper-bag rebreathing; explain procedures carefully for phobic patients and allow them to lie down for injections (blood-injection-injury phobia); respect OCD rituals where safe; transport if any doubt about the diagnosis or if the patient has suicidal thoughts. First-line long-term treatments for GAD and panic disorder are SSRIs/SNRIs and CBT (with exposure for panic disorder); benzodiazepines are limited to short-term use because of dependence (Craske and Stein, 2016; Tutorial 1 memo).

## 52.6 Disorders related to trauma and stressors

These disorders require **exposure to a traumatic or stressful event** as a diagnostic criterion (APA, 2022). Exposure to a traumatic event is defined as exposure to actual or threatened death, serious injury, or sexual violence through: (1) directly experiencing it; (2) witnessing it in person as it occurs to others; (3) learning that it occurred to a close family member or friend (violent or accidental); or (4) repeated or extreme exposure to aversive details of traumatic events — which includes **first responders** (for example collecting human remains, or repeated exposure to details of child abuse) (APA, 2022). South Africa's high levels of violence, rape and road trauma make these disorders very common, both in patients and in emergency care providers (Tilley et al., 2026, citing Padmanabhanunni and Pretorius, 2025).

The symptom clusters (common to both disorders below) are:

- **Intrusion**: distressing memories, nightmares, **flashbacks** (dissociative re-experiencing), and psychological or physiological distress at cues.
- **Avoidance** of memories, thoughts, feelings or external reminders.
- **Negative alterations in cognition and mood**: inability to remember aspects of the event, persistent negative beliefs ("I am bad", "the world is completely dangerous"), distorted blame, persistent negative emotions (fear, horror, anger, guilt, shame), diminished interest, detachment, inability to feel positive emotions.
- **Alterations in arousal and reactivity**: irritability and angry outbursts, reckless or self-destructive behaviour, **hypervigilance**, exaggerated startle response, poor concentration, sleep disturbance.
- **Dissociative symptoms**: depersonalisation, derealisation, a sense of being in a daze.

### 52.6.1 Acute stress disorder

**Core features.** Following exposure to a traumatic event, the presence of **nine or more** symptoms from any of the five categories (intrusion, negative mood, dissociation, avoidance, arousal), with onset after the trauma and a duration of **three days to one month** after the trauma (APA, 2022). It causes clinically significant distress or impairment and is not due to a substance or medical condition.

**Emergency relevance.** Victims of assault, rape, collisions, fires and disasters may be in an acute stress state at the scene — dazed, dissociated, numb, agitated, or unable to give a history. Acute stress reactions are normal responses to abnormal events, and most people recover.

**Prehospital management — Psychological First Aid (PFA)** (WHO principles: look, listen, link): ensure safety and treat injuries; provide calm, comforting presence; **do not force the person to talk** about the event or to give detailed accounts ("psychological debriefing" that forces recounting may be harmful); listen if they wish to talk; meet basic needs (warmth, water, information); help them connect with family and social supports; provide accurate information; screen for injuries, intoxication, suicidal ideation and the need for protection (for example in domestic violence or sexual assault); and refer for follow-up.

### 52.6.2 Post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD)

**Core features.** Exposure to a traumatic event plus symptoms from **all four clusters** — at least **one intrusion**, at least **one avoidance**, at least **two negative alterations in cognition and mood**, and at least **two alterations in arousal and reactivity** — lasting **more than one month**, causing clinically significant distress or impairment, and not attributable to a substance or medical condition (APA, 2022). Specifiers include "with dissociative symptoms" and "with delayed expression" (full criteria not met until at least six months after the event). A preschool subtype exists for children aged six years or younger.

**Emergency relevance.** Flashbacks can cause sudden agitation, fear and aggression (for example when a patient with PTSD from an assault is restrained or touched unexpectedly); comorbid depression, substance use and suicide; self-harm; and re-traumatisation through restraint, examinations (especially genital examinations after sexual assault), uniforms and sirens. PTSD is also an **occupational hazard for EMS staff**, with providers describing "lasting visions" from suicide scenes (Tilley et al., 2026).

**Prehospital management.** **Trauma-informed care**: explain every action before doing it, seek permission before touching, give the patient choice and control, avoid restraint where possible, offer a same-gender provider where possible (especially after sexual violence), use a calm and predictable approach, ground a patient experiencing a flashback ("You are safe, you are in an ambulance, today is…, my name is…"), assess suicide risk, and refer. Evidence-based long-term treatments include trauma-focused psychotherapy (prolonged exposure, cognitive processing therapy, EMDR) and SSRIs (Shalev, Liberzon and Marmar, 2017).

## 52.7 Eating disorders

### 52.7.1 Anorexia nervosa

**DSM-5-TR criteria.** (A) Restriction of energy intake relative to requirements, leading to a **significantly low body weight** in the context of age, sex, developmental trajectory and physical health; (B) **intense fear of gaining weight** or becoming fat, or persistent behaviour that interferes with weight gain, even though at a significantly low weight; (C) disturbance in the way body weight or shape is experienced, undue influence of weight or shape on self-evaluation, or persistent lack of recognition of the seriousness of the low weight (APA, 2022). Subtypes are **restricting type** and **binge-eating/purging type**. Severity in adults is based on BMI (mild ≥17; moderate 16–16.99; severe 15–15.99; extreme <15 kg/m²).

**Emergency relevance — anorexia has the highest mortality of any psychiatric disorder**, from medical complications and suicide (Treasure, Duarte and Schmidt, 2020). Life-threatening complications include:

- **bradycardia, hypotension, arrhythmias, QT prolongation** and sudden cardiac death;
- **hypokalaemia, hypophosphataemia, hypomagnesaemia, hypoglycaemia**;
- **hypothermia**;
- syncope and falls; osteoporosis and fractures;
- **refeeding syndrome** — when nutrition is restarted, insulin release drives phosphate, potassium and magnesium into cells, causing severe hypophosphataemia with cardiac failure, arrhythmias, respiratory failure, seizures and death; thiamine deficiency may precipitate Wernicke's encephalopathy;
- suicide.

**Prehospital management.** ABCDE, ECG monitoring (bradycardia, QT prolongation, arrhythmias), glucose (treat hypoglycaemia), temperature (prevent heat loss), cautious IV fluids in shock (risk of cardiac failure), thiamine 100 mg IV/IM for malnourished patients within CDST scope (HPCSA PBEC, 2025, p. 73), suicide risk assessment, and a non-judgemental approach (do not comment on weight or appearance). Transport to hospital.

### 52.7.2 Bulimia nervosa

**DSM-5-TR criteria.** (A) Recurrent episodes of **binge eating** (eating, in a discrete period, an amount of food definitely larger than most people would eat in a similar period, with a **sense of lack of control**); (B) recurrent inappropriate **compensatory behaviours** to prevent weight gain — self-induced vomiting, misuse of laxatives, diuretics or other medications, fasting or excessive exercise; (C) both occurring on average **at least once a week for three months**; (D) self-evaluation unduly influenced by body shape and weight; (E) not occurring exclusively during episodes of anorexia nervosa (APA, 2022). Body weight is usually normal or above normal.

**Emergency relevance.**

- **Electrolyte disturbances from purging**: **hypokalaemic, hypochloraemic metabolic alkalosis** from vomiting (and metabolic acidosis from laxative abuse), causing weakness, arrhythmias and sudden death.
- Dehydration.
- **Oesophageal complications**: Mallory–Weiss tear (haematemesis), and rarely **Boerhaave syndrome** (oesophageal rupture — severe chest pain, subcutaneous emphysema, shock).
- Gastric dilatation or rupture after binges.
- Ipecac abuse (cardiomyopathy).
- Physical signs: **Russell's sign** (calluses on the knuckles from self-induced vomiting), parotid gland enlargement, dental enamel erosion.
- Comorbid depression, substance use, self-harm and suicide.

**Prehospital management.** ABCDE, ECG monitoring for arrhythmias, IV fluids for dehydration, management of haematemesis and suspected oesophageal rupture (keep nil by mouth), suicide risk assessment, non-judgemental approach, and transport.

## 52.8 Substance-related and addictive disorders

DSM-5-TR classifies **substance use disorders** and **substance-induced disorders** (intoxication, withdrawal, and substance/medication-induced mental disorders) for ten classes of substances: alcohol, caffeine, cannabis, hallucinogens (phencyclidine and other hallucinogens), inhalants, opioids, sedatives/hypnotics/anxiolytics, stimulants, tobacco, and other/unknown substances. **Gambling disorder** is the only behavioural addiction included (APA, 2022).

### 52.8.1 Substance intoxication and withdrawal

- **Substance intoxication**: the development of a reversible, substance-specific syndrome due to recent ingestion of a substance, with clinically significant problematic behavioural or psychological changes (for example belligerence, mood lability, impaired judgement) attributable to the effects of the substance on the CNS, developing during or shortly after use, and not attributable to another medical condition or mental disorder (APA, 2022).
- **Substance withdrawal**: a substance-specific problematic behavioural change, with physiological and cognitive concomitants, due to the cessation of, or reduction in, heavy and prolonged substance use. Withdrawal syndromes are generally the **opposite** of the intoxication effects (for example sedative intoxication causes sedation; sedative withdrawal causes agitation, tremor, seizures).

**Prehospital approach to intoxication and withdrawal**: ABCDE; airway protection; glucose; thiamine for alcohol users (CDST: thiamine 100 mg IV/IM when there is concern about vitamin deficiency, for example malnourishment or chronic alcohol use; HPCSA PBEC, 2025, p. 73); identify the toxidrome (Outcome 62.3); treat specific toxicity (CDST Poisoning tool); manage seizures (CDST Seizures tool); look for head injury and co-ingestion; do not assume intoxication explains everything.

### 52.8.2 Substance use disorders

**DSM-5-TR criteria.** A problematic pattern of use leading to clinically significant impairment or distress, with **at least two of eleven criteria within a 12-month period**, grouped as follows (APA, 2022):

- **Impaired control**: (1) taking larger amounts or for longer than intended; (2) persistent desire or unsuccessful efforts to cut down; (3) a great deal of time spent obtaining, using or recovering; (4) **craving**.
- **Social impairment**: (5) failure to fulfil major role obligations; (6) continued use despite social or interpersonal problems; (7) giving up important activities.
- **Risky use**: (8) recurrent use in physically hazardous situations; (9) continued use despite knowledge of physical or psychological problems caused or worsened by the substance.
- **Pharmacological criteria**: (10) **tolerance**; (11) **withdrawal**.

Severity: **mild** = 2–3 criteria; **moderate** = 4–5; **severe** = 6 or more. (Tolerance and withdrawal alone do not count when the substance is taken as prescribed under medical supervision.)

**South African context.** Alcohol is the most widely used substance; cannabis ("dagga"), methamphetamine ("tik"), methaqualone ("Mandrax", often smoked with cannabis as a "white pipe"), heroin-based mixtures ("nyaope" or "whoonga"), cocaine and prescription opioids and benzodiazepines are important (data from the South African Community Epidemiology Network on Drug Use, SACENDU, of the South African Medical Research Council). Tilley et al. (2026) cite an estimate that 18.9% of the population abuse alcohol and/or drugs. The Prevention of and Treatment for Substance Abuse Act 70 of 2008 provides for voluntary and involuntary admission to treatment centres.

### 52.8.3 Alcohol-related disorders

- **Alcohol intoxication**: slurred speech, incoordination, unsteady gait, nystagmus, impaired attention or memory, stupor or coma, with behavioural changes (disinhibition, aggression, labile mood). Severe intoxication causes respiratory depression, aspiration, hypothermia and **hypoglycaemia** (especially in children and malnourished adults).
- **Alcohol withdrawal**: begins about 6–24 hours after the last drink, with tremor, sweating, tachycardia, hypertension, anxiety, nausea, insomnia and agitation; **withdrawal seizures** typically at 12–48 hours; **alcoholic hallucinosis** (usually visual, sometimes auditory or tactile, with a clear sensorium); and **delirium tremens** typically at 48–96 hours — disorientation, fluctuating consciousness, vivid visual and tactile hallucinations, severe autonomic hyperactivity and hyperthermia, with significant mortality if untreated (Schuckit, 2014). Severity can be scored in hospital with the CIWA-Ar (Sullivan et al., 1989).
- **Wernicke's encephalopathy**: acute thiamine deficiency with the classic (but often incomplete) triad of **confusion, ataxia and ophthalmoplegia/nystagmus**; give thiamine **before or with** glucose in at-risk patients where possible (CDST thiamine 100 mg IV/IM). Untreated, it progresses to **Korsakoff syndrome** (anterograde amnesia with confabulation).
- **Other complications**: head injury (subdural haematoma), GI bleeding (varices, Mallory–Weiss), pancreatitis, alcoholic ketoacidosis, hepatic encephalopathy, cardiomyopathy and arrhythmias ("holiday heart"), aspiration pneumonia, trauma, interpersonal violence and suicide.
- **Fetal alcohol spectrum disorder** (prenatal exposure).

**Prehospital management**: airway and aspiration prevention (lateral position), glucose, thiamine, temperature, look for injury, benzodiazepines for withdrawal seizures and agitation (CDST seizure and aggression tools), IV fluids.

### 52.8.4 Cannabis-related disorders

- **Intoxication**: euphoria, anxiety, sensation of slowed time, impaired judgement and coordination, **conjunctival injection**, increased appetite, dry mouth, **tachycardia**; at high doses, paranoia, panic, psychosis. Children who ingest edibles may present with drowsiness, ataxia, hypotonia, and occasionally coma or seizures.
- **Withdrawal**: irritability, anger, anxiety, insomnia, decreased appetite, restlessness, depressed mood, and physical symptoms (abdominal pain, tremor, sweating, headache) beginning within about a week of cessation of heavy use.
- **Cannabinoid hyperemesis syndrome**: cyclical severe vomiting and abdominal pain in chronic heavy users, characteristically relieved by **hot showers or baths**.
- **Synthetic cannabinoids** cause much more severe toxicity: agitation, psychosis, seizures, tachycardia, myocardial ischaemia, acute kidney injury and death.
- Cannabis use is a risk factor for psychosis and schizophrenia, especially with adolescent onset and high-potency products.

**Prehospital management**: reassurance in a calm environment, supportive care, benzodiazepines for severe agitation (CDST), treatment of dehydration from vomiting, and consideration of co-ingestion.

### 52.8.5 Hallucinogen-related disorders

Hallucinogens include LSD, psilocybin ("magic mushrooms"), mescaline, MDMA ("ecstasy", which has both stimulant and hallucinogenic effects), and the dissociatives phencyclidine (PCP) and ketamine (which DSM-5-TR classifies separately under phencyclidine-related disorders).

- **Intoxication**: perceptual changes (intensified perceptions, illusions, hallucinations, synaesthesia), depersonalisation, derealisation, anxiety or panic ("bad trip"), paranoia, impaired judgement, with sympathomimetic signs (**mydriasis**, tachycardia, sweating, palpitations, blurred vision, tremor, incoordination). PCP and ketamine cause dissociation, **vertical and horizontal nystagmus**, analgesia, hypertension, agitation and violence.
- **MDMA** specifically causes hyperthermia, **hyponatraemia** (from excessive water intake and SIADH — seizures and cerebral oedema), serotonin syndrome, rhabdomyolysis, DIC and multi-organ failure.
- **Hallucinogen persisting perception disorder** ("flashbacks").
- There is **no recognised withdrawal syndrome** for most hallucinogens.

**Prehospital management**: a calm, quiet environment and "talking down" with reassurance; prevent injury (patients may act on perceptions, for example believing they can fly); benzodiazepines for agitation (CDST); cool if hyperthermic; check glucose; consider hyponatraemia in MDMA users with seizures or altered consciousness; transport.

### 52.8.6 Opioid-related disorders

- **Intoxication**: initial euphoria followed by apathy and sedation; **the opioid toxidrome — depressed level of consciousness, pinpoint pupils (miosis), and respiratory depression**, with bradycardia, hypotension and hypothermia (HPCSA PBEC, 2025, p. 55). Note that pupils may be dilated with severe hypoxia or co-ingestants, and some opioids (for example tramadol, pethidine) may not cause miosis. Tramadol lowers the seizure threshold and may cause serotonin syndrome.
- **Withdrawal** (6–12 hours after short-acting opioids such as heroin; longer for methadone): dysphoria, craving, nausea and vomiting, muscle aches, **lacrimation, rhinorrhoea, mydriasis, piloerection ("gooseflesh")**, sweating, diarrhoea, yawning, fever and insomnia. Opioid withdrawal is extremely unpleasant but rarely life-threatening in adults (neonatal withdrawal can be severe).
- **Complications**: non-cardiogenic pulmonary oedema, aspiration, rhabdomyolysis and compartment syndrome from prolonged immobility, infections from injecting (abscesses, endocarditis, HIV, hepatitis B and C).
- South Africa has seen growing use of heroin-based "nyaope/whoonga".

**Prehospital management (CDST, HPCSA PBEC, 2025, p. 56)**: airway and BVM ventilation first; **naloxone if respiratory depression**: adult \*naloxone 0.4 mg IV/IM\*; if a **known opioid abuser**, \*naloxone 0.04 mg IV/IM\* to limit withdrawal; reassess every two minutes and give increasing doses (0.8 mg, 2 mg, 4 mg every two minutes) to a maximum total of 10 mg; child \*naloxone 0.1 mg/kg IV/IM (up to 2 mg)\*, repeated every two minutes to a maximum cumulative 10 mg. If the respiratory rate remains low, consider other poisoning. **Naloxone wears off quickly** (its duration of action is shorter than that of most opioids), so monitor the respiratory rate closely and give further doses if needed. Titrate to adequate breathing, not full awakening, to avoid precipitating acute withdrawal, agitation and vomiting.

### 52.8.7 Sedative-, hypnotic- or anxiolytic-related disorders

This class includes **benzodiazepines** (diazepam, lorazepam, alprazolam, midazolam), **Z-drugs** (zolpidem, zopiclone), **barbiturates** (phenobarbital), **methaqualone** ("Mandrax") and **GHB/GBL**.

- **Intoxication**: slurred speech, incoordination, unsteady gait, nystagmus, impaired cognition, stupor or coma, and (with co-ingestion of alcohol or opioids, or with barbiturates) respiratory depression. The CDST sedative-hypnotic toxidrome is a **depressed level of consciousness, slurred speech and respiratory depression** with relatively normal vital signs (HPCSA PBEC, 2025, p. 55). Benzodiazepines alone rarely cause death unless combined with other depressants.
- **Withdrawal**: autonomic hyperactivity, tremor, insomnia, anxiety, nausea, hallucinations, agitation, **seizures** and delirium — **potentially life-threatening**, like alcohol withdrawal.
- **Anterograde amnesia** with benzodiazepines and GHB (relevant to drug-facilitated sexual assault, 60.14).

**Prehospital management**: airway and ventilatory support; the CDST does **not** include flumazenil for prehospital use; flumazenil is a hospital-level antidote that is contraindicated in mixed overdoses (particularly with TCAs), in benzodiazepine-dependent patients and in patients with epilepsy, because it can precipitate seizures and withdrawal (Hüser et al., 2025; RCEM, 2025). Manage withdrawal seizures with benzodiazepines (CDST seizure tool).

### 52.8.8 Stimulant-related disorders

Stimulants include amphetamine-type substances (**methamphetamine "tik"**, MDMA, methcathinone "cat"), **cocaine** (including crack cocaine), and prescription stimulants (methylphenidate).

- **Intoxication**: euphoria, hypervigilance, grandiosity, anxiety, anger, impaired judgement, repetitive behaviour, paranoia, psychosis; with the **sympathomimetic toxidrome** — agitation, hallucinations, seizures, sweating, hyperthermia, **dilated pupils**, tachycardia and hypertension (HPCSA PBEC, 2025, p. 55). Complications include chest pain and myocardial infarction (cocaine-induced coronary vasospasm), arrhythmias, aortic dissection, stroke and intracranial haemorrhage, seizures, hyperthermia, rhabdomyolysis, acute kidney injury, and **acute behavioural disturbance with sudden death** (RCEM, 2025).
- **Withdrawal ("crash")**: dysphoric mood, fatigue, vivid unpleasant dreams, insomnia or hypersomnia, increased appetite, psychomotor retardation or agitation, and **suicidal ideation**.

**Prehospital management**: see Outcome 60.1.

### 52.8.9 Situations in which the substance related to the disorder is not known

DSM-5-TR provides the category **"other (or unknown) substance-related disorders"** for situations in which the substance is unknown or not covered by the other classes (APA, 2022). In emergency practice this is common: patients may be unconscious, unwilling or unable to give a history; street drugs are frequently adulterated or misrepresented (for example fentanyl in heroin, synthetic cannabinoids sold as cannabis, mixtures such as "nyaope"); and mixed ingestions occur in about half of poisoning admissions (Hüser et al., 2025). The approach is to **treat the patient, not the poison**:

1. ABCDE stabilisation (airway, breathing, circulation, disability including glucose, exposure including temperature) (Hüser et al., 2025; HPCSA PBEC, 2025).
2. History and collateral information, a scene search (containers, paraphernalia, notes), and bringing all substances to hospital (HPCSA PBEC, 2025, pp. 54–55).
3. **Toxidrome recognition** to identify the likely class (Outcome 62.3).
4. Empirical treatment: naloxone for an opioid picture with respiratory depression; benzodiazepines for agitation and seizures; cooling for hyperthermia; glucose for hypoglycaemia; sodium bicarbonate for a widened QRS (ANT/ECP).
5. Contact the **Poisons Information Helpline of the Western Cape (0861 555 777)** (HPCSA PBEC, 2025, p. 55).
6. Transport every patient poisoned with an unknown substance to the closest emergency centre (HPCSA PBEC, 2025, p. 55).

## 52.9 Neurocognitive disorders

Neurocognitive disorders (NCDs) are disorders in which the primary clinical deficit is in cognitive function and which are **acquired** rather than developmental (APA, 2022). DSM-5-TR assesses six cognitive domains: complex attention, executive function, learning and memory, language, perceptual-motor function, and social cognition. **Major NCD** (previously "dementia") involves significant decline that interferes with independence in everyday activities; **mild NCD** involves modest decline without loss of independence. The aetiological subtypes include Alzheimer's disease, frontotemporal lobar degeneration, Lewy body disease, vascular disease, traumatic brain injury, substance/medication use, HIV infection, prion disease, Parkinson's disease, Huntington's disease, another medical condition, and multiple aetiologies.

### 52.9.1 Delirium

**DSM-5-TR criteria.** (A) A disturbance in **attention** (reduced ability to direct, focus, sustain and shift attention) and **awareness** (reduced orientation to the environment); (B) the disturbance **develops over a short period** (usually hours to a few days), represents a change from baseline, and tends to **fluctuate** in severity during the day; (C) an additional disturbance in cognition (memory deficit, disorientation, language, visuospatial ability or perception); (D) the disturbances are not better explained by a pre-existing NCD and do not occur in the context of a severely reduced level of arousal such as coma; (E) there is evidence that the disturbance is a direct physiological consequence of another medical condition, substance intoxication or withdrawal, exposure to a toxin, or multiple aetiologies (APA, 2022). Subtypes are **hyperactive, hypoactive and mixed**.

**Epidemiology and importance.** Delirium is common in older inpatients and emergency patients, is often missed (especially the hypoactive form), and is associated with increased mortality, longer hospital stay, functional decline, institutionalisation and later dementia (Inouye, Westendorp and Saczynski, 2014).

**Causes** — think of the "**I WATCH DEATH**" or "**PINCH ME**" mnemonics: infection (UTI, pneumonia, sepsis, meningitis), withdrawal (alcohol, benzodiazepines), acute metabolic disturbance (glucose, sodium, calcium, uraemia, hepatic failure), trauma (head injury, burns, fractures), CNS pathology (stroke, haemorrhage, seizures), hypoxia, deficiencies (thiamine, B12), endocrine (thyroid), acute vascular (hypertensive encephalopathy, shock), toxins and drugs (anticholinergics, opioids, benzodiazepines, polypharmacy, poisoning), heavy metals; and pain, constipation, urinary retention, sleep deprivation and environmental change (Desai, Kulkarni and Verma, n.d.).

**Assessment tools.** The **Confusion Assessment Method (CAM)** (Inouye et al., 1990) requires (1) acute onset and fluctuating course **and** (2) inattention, plus **either** (3) disorganised thinking **or** (4) altered level of consciousness.

**Prehospital management.** Delirium is a **medical emergency**. ABCDE; oxygen if SpO₂ <92%; glucose; temperature; look for infection, trauma, dehydration and toxins; bring medications; non-pharmacological measures (reorientation, family presence, glasses and hearing aids, quiet environment); avoid benzodiazepines except for alcohol/benzodiazepine withdrawal or seizures; reduced CDST doses if sedation is unavoidable; transport to an emergency centre.

### 52.9.2 Alzheimer's disease (major or mild NCD due to Alzheimer's disease)

**Core features.** Insidious onset and gradual progression of impairment in one or more cognitive domains (two or more for major NCD), with prominent early impairment of **learning and memory** (particularly recent memory), later involving language (word-finding difficulty), visuospatial function (getting lost), executive function and behaviour; with evidence of a causative genetic mutation or a steadily progressive decline without extended plateaus, and no evidence of mixed aetiology (APA, 2022). Pathology involves extracellular amyloid-β plaques and intracellular tau neurofibrillary tangles; it is the most common cause of dementia; and age is the main risk factor (Scheltens et al., 2021).

**Emergency relevance.** Superimposed **delirium** (from infection, dehydration, medications); behavioural and psychological symptoms (agitation, aggression, wandering, psychosis, sundowning); falls and injuries; neglect and abuse; inability to report pain; questions of capacity and consent (Outcome 49).

**Prehospital management.** Collateral history to establish baseline; look for an acute cause of any change; non-pharmacological calming; analgesia; minimal sedation (CDST >60-year doses); capacity assessment and substitute consent (NHA s 7(1)(b)); consider elder abuse.

### 52.9.3 Traumatic brain injuries (major or mild NCD due to TBI)

**Core features.** Evidence of a traumatic brain injury (an impact to the head or rapid movement or displacement of the brain within the skull) with one or more of: loss of consciousness, post-traumatic amnesia, disorientation and confusion, or neurological signs (neuroimaging abnormality, new seizures, visual field deficits, anosmia, hemiparesis); with the NCD presenting immediately after the injury or after recovery of consciousness and persisting past the acute post-injury period (APA, 2022).

**Behavioural features of TBI.** Acutely: confusion, agitation, combativeness, disinhibition, amnesia — the agitated head-injured patient is often mistaken for being drunk or psychotic. Chronically: personality change, impulsivity, irritability and aggression (frontal lobe injury), depression, anxiety, PTSD, substance use and **increased suicide risk**, together with cognitive deficits (Maas et al., 2022). South Africa's very high rates of interpersonal violence and road traffic injuries make TBI common.

**Prehospital management.** CDST Head injury tool: manage the airway and spine, avoid hypoxia (SpO₂ >92%) and hypotension (both worsen secondary brain injury), check glucose, assess and trend GCS and pupils, look for signs of raised intracranial pressure, and transport to an appropriate facility. **Never attribute agitation to intoxication without excluding head injury** (the CDST instructs the provider to "look for and manage injury"). If sedation is required for agitation, the CDST notes ketamine 0.5–1 mg/kg IV for agitation due to hypoxia or TBI where the patient cannot be adequately pre-oxygenated for airway management (HPCSA PBEC, 2025, p. 14 — Airway management tool, drug-assisted intubation; ketamine is ANT/ECP scope).

### 52.9.4 HIV infections (major or mild NCD due to HIV infection)

**Core features.** Documented HIV infection, with an NCD not better explained by non-HIV conditions such as secondary brain diseases (for example progressive multifocal leukoencephalopathy or cryptococcal meningitis) (APA, 2022). HIV-associated neurocognitive disorders (HAND) range from asymptomatic neurocognitive impairment to mild neurocognitive disorder to HIV-associated dementia (the Frascati criteria; Antinori et al., 2007). Typical features are "subcortical": psychomotor slowing, poor concentration and memory retrieval, apathy, executive dysfunction, and motor problems (gait disturbance, tremor).

**South African relevance.** South Africa has one of the largest HIV epidemics in the world (roughly 13% of the population living with HIV). Although antiretroviral therapy has reduced severe HIV-associated dementia, milder HAND remains common, and **acute behavioural change in a person living with HIV demands a search for opportunistic infections**: cryptococcal meningitis, toxoplasmosis, tuberculous meningitis and tuberculoma, PML, CMV encephalitis, neurosyphilis, primary CNS lymphoma, as well as HIV-related psychosis, depression, and **ARV neuropsychiatric side effects** (efavirenz: vivid dreams, insomnia, depression, psychosis; dolutegravir: insomnia, headache, occasionally depression).

**Prehospital management.** Treat as an organic presentation: ABCDE, glucose, temperature, neck stiffness, focal signs, seizures; ask about CD4 count, viral load, ARV adherence and recent changes in ARV regimen; universal precautions; transport to an emergency centre.

### 52.9.5 Parkinson's disease (major or mild NCD due to Parkinson's disease)

**Core features.** The NCD occurs in the setting of established Parkinson's disease, with insidious onset and gradual progression, and is not attributable to another condition (APA, 2022). Parkinson's disease is a neurodegenerative disorder caused by loss of dopaminergic neurons in the substantia nigra, with the cardinal motor features of **bradykinesia, rest tremor, rigidity and postural instability**, and non-motor features including depression, anxiety, apathy, sleep disorders, autonomic dysfunction (orthostatic hypotension, constipation, urinary problems), hallucinations and dementia (Bloem, Okun and Klein, 2021). Parkinson's disease dementia and **dementia with Lewy bodies** share fluctuating cognition, visual hallucinations and extreme sensitivity to antipsychotics.

**Emergency relevance.**

- **Psychosis and visual hallucinations**, often precipitated by dopaminergic medications or infection.
- Delirium.
- **Falls and fractures**; aspiration pneumonia (dysphagia).
- **Parkinsonism-hyperpyrexia syndrome** — an NMS-like syndrome (rigidity, hyperthermia, altered consciousness) caused by **abrupt withdrawal** of levodopa or dopamine agonists (for example when the patient cannot swallow or is kept nil by mouth).
- **Dopamine antagonists (haloperidol, droperidol, metoclopramide) are contraindicated** — they severely worsen parkinsonism and may precipitate NMS (RCEM, 2025).

**Prehospital management.** Look for infection, dehydration and medication changes; avoid dopamine antagonists; use non-pharmacological calming; CDST benzodiazepine sedation at reduced doses only if necessary; protect the airway (aspiration risk); bring the medication list and report the time of the last levodopa dose (missed doses must be avoided); transport.

## 52.10 Briefly discuss cluster A, B and C categories of personality disorders

A **personality disorder** is "an enduring pattern of inner experience and behaviour that deviates markedly from the expectations of the individual's culture, is pervasive and inflexible, has an onset in adolescence or early adulthood, is stable over time, and leads to distress or impairment" (APA, 2022). The pattern is manifested in two or more of: cognition, affectivity, interpersonal functioning and impulse control. DSM-5-TR groups ten personality disorders into three clusters.

### Cluster A — "odd or eccentric"

- **Paranoid personality disorder**: pervasive distrust and suspiciousness; others' motives are interpreted as malevolent; reluctance to confide; bears grudges; perceives attacks on character; recurrent unjustified suspicion of partner's fidelity.
- **Schizoid personality disorder**: detachment from social relationships and a restricted range of emotional expression; prefers solitary activities; appears indifferent to praise or criticism; emotional coldness.
- **Schizotypal personality disorder**: acute discomfort in close relationships, cognitive or perceptual distortions, and eccentric behaviour — ideas of reference, odd beliefs or magical thinking, unusual perceptual experiences, odd speech, suspiciousness, inappropriate affect. Genetically related to schizophrenia.

**Emergency relevance**: suspicion and mistrust of the crew; transient psychotic episodes under stress; refusal of care. **Approach**: be transparent, respect personal space, explain everything, avoid excessive familiarity.

### Cluster B — "dramatic, emotional or erratic"

- **Antisocial personality disorder**: pervasive disregard for and violation of the rights of others since age 15, with evidence of conduct disorder before age 15, in a person at least 18 years old — failure to conform to laws, deceitfulness, impulsivity, irritability and aggressiveness (repeated fights or assaults), reckless disregard for safety, irresponsibility, and **lack of remorse**.
- **Borderline personality disorder**: pervasive instability of interpersonal relationships, self-image and affect, with marked impulsivity — frantic efforts to avoid abandonment; unstable, intense relationships alternating between idealisation and devaluation; identity disturbance; impulsivity in potentially self-damaging areas (spending, sex, substance use, reckless driving, binge eating); **recurrent suicidal behaviour, gestures, threats or self-mutilating behaviour**; affective instability; chronic feelings of emptiness; inappropriate, intense anger; transient stress-related paranoid ideation or dissociation. Up to about 10% of people with borderline personality disorder die by suicide.
- **Histrionic personality disorder**: excessive emotionality and attention-seeking — discomfort when not the centre of attention, inappropriately seductive or provocative behaviour, rapidly shifting and shallow emotions, use of physical appearance to draw attention, impressionistic speech, theatricality, suggestibility, considering relationships more intimate than they are.
- **Narcissistic personality disorder**: grandiosity, need for admiration and lack of empathy — sense of entitlement, interpersonal exploitation, envy, arrogance, fragile self-esteem with rage when criticised.

**Emergency relevance**: Cluster B disorders are the most frequently encountered in emergency care. They are associated with **self-harm and suicide** (especially borderline), **violence** (especially antisocial), substance use, interpersonal crises, domestic violence (as perpetrators or victims), and frequent EMS contact. Staff may experience strong negative emotions (frustration, anger), and patients may be labelled "attention seeking" — this label is dangerous, because the suicide risk is real (Evans, Geduld and Stassen, 2018). **Approach**: calm, consistent, non-judgemental, set clear boundaries; take every threat of self-harm seriously; assess and treat injuries; maintain safety; avoid being drawn into arguments or "splitting" between team members.

### Cluster C — "anxious or fearful"

- **Avoidant personality disorder**: social inhibition, feelings of inadequacy and hypersensitivity to negative evaluation; avoids interpersonal contact for fear of criticism or rejection; restraint in intimate relationships; views self as socially inept.
- **Dependent personality disorder**: a pervasive and excessive need to be taken care of, leading to submissive and clinging behaviour and fears of separation — difficulty making everyday decisions without excessive advice; needs others to assume responsibility; difficulty expressing disagreement; urgently seeks another relationship when one ends. Dependent individuals may remain in abusive relationships.
- **Obsessive-compulsive personality disorder (OCPD)**: preoccupation with orderliness, perfectionism and mental and interpersonal control at the expense of flexibility, openness and efficiency — preoccupation with details, rules and lists; perfectionism that interferes with task completion; excessive devotion to work; inflexibility about morality; inability to discard worthless objects; reluctance to delegate; miserliness; rigidity and stubbornness. Unlike OCD, OCPD does not involve true obsessions and compulsions, and the traits are usually experienced as consistent with the self (ego-syntonic).

**Emergency relevance**: anxiety, comorbid depression and anxiety disorders, difficulty making decisions about care (dependent), and potential vulnerability to domestic violence (dependent). **Approach**: reassurance, clear information, encouragement to participate in decisions, and screening for abuse.

### General principles for personality disorders in emergency care

Personality disorders are diagnosed only after thorough assessment over time — **never on the basis of a single emergency encounter**. The ECP should describe behaviour objectively on the PRF rather than labelling the patient, treat medical and psychiatric emergencies on their merits, and be aware of their own emotional reactions.

### High-yield points — Outcome 52

- Schizophrenia: two or more of delusions, hallucinations, disorganised speech, disorganised/catatonic behaviour, negative symptoms (at least one of the first three), for ≥6 months including ≥1 month active.
- Mania ≥7 days (or hospitalisation), three or more DIGFAST symptoms (four if only irritable); hypomania ≥4 days; Bipolar I = ≥1 mania; Bipolar II = hypomania + major depression, never mania.
- MDD: five or more of nine symptoms for two weeks, including depressed mood or anhedonia.
- Catatonia: three or more of twelve features; treat with lorazepam/ECT; avoid antipsychotics.
- Acute stress disorder 3 days–1 month; PTSD >1 month; first responders count as exposed.
- Anorexia: highest psychiatric mortality; refeeding syndrome; bulimia: hypokalaemic metabolic alkalosis, Russell's sign.
- Substance use disorder: two or more of eleven criteria in 12 months (mild 2–3, moderate 4–5, severe ≥6).
- CDST naloxone: 0.4 mg (0.04 mg in known abusers), increase 0.8, 2, 4 mg to 10 mg total; child 0.1 mg/kg (max 2 mg per dose).
- Delirium: acute, fluctuating, inattention; CAM.
- Parkinson's/Lewy body: no haloperidol, droperidol or metoclopramide.
- Personality clusters: A odd (paranoid, schizoid, schizotypal); B dramatic (antisocial, borderline, histrionic, narcissistic); C anxious (avoidant, dependent, obsessive-compulsive).
