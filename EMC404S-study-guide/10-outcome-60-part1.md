# Outcome 60. Comprehensively discuss poisoning in both adults and children in relation to pathophysiology, clinical presentation and management

For each poisoning, the **prehospital management** follows the CDST Poisoning tool (Outcome 62) unless otherwise stated: scene safety, ABCDE, oxygen if SpO₂ <92% (or RR >30 or <9, or GCS ≤14), glucose, decontamination, toxidrome identification, specific treatment within scope, the Poisons Information Helpline (0861 555 777), and transport with 10-minute monitoring (HPCSA PBEC, 2025). Hospital-level treatments are included so that you understand the full pathway, and are labelled as such.

## 60.1 Stimulant overdose

**Agents**: amphetamine-type stimulants (methamphetamine "tik", amphetamine, MDMA "ecstasy", methcathinone "cat" and synthetic cathinones), cocaine (powder and crack), prescription stimulants (methylphenidate), and in some definitions caffeine and theophylline.

**Pathophysiology**: amphetamines cause release (and block reuptake) of noradrenaline, dopamine and serotonin from presynaptic neurons; cocaine blocks the reuptake of noradrenaline, dopamine and serotonin and also **blocks fast sodium channels** (a local anaesthetic effect causing QRS widening and arrhythmias). The result is excessive central and peripheral sympathetic stimulation: CNS excitation, vasoconstriction (including coronary vasospasm), tachycardia, hypertension, increased myocardial oxygen demand, hyperthermia (increased muscle activity and heat production with impaired heat loss due to vasoconstriction), and, in severe cases, rhabdomyolysis, metabolic acidosis, DIC and multi-organ failure. MDMA's serotonergic effects cause serotonin toxicity and hyponatraemia (SIADH plus excessive water intake).

**Clinical presentation (sympathomimetic toxidrome)**: agitation, anxiety, paranoia, hallucinations, psychosis, seizures, **sweating, hyperthermia, dilated pupils, tachycardia and hypertension** (HPCSA PBEC, 2025, p. 55); bruxism (jaw clenching); chest pain (myocardial ischaemia or infarction, aortic dissection, pneumothorax from smoking); palpitations and arrhythmias; headache, stroke and intracranial haemorrhage; abdominal pain (mesenteric ischaemia); **acute behavioural disturbance** with risk of sudden death (RCEM, 2025). Body packers and stuffers may present with sudden severe toxicity when packets rupture.

**Children**: may ingest adult methamphetamine or prescription stimulants, presenting with agitation, tachycardia, hypertension, hyperthermia and seizures; younger children may present with seizures as the first sign.

**Prehospital management (CDST)**:

- Scene safety; SAPS if violent.
- ABCDE; oxygen if SpO₂ <92%; glucose.
- **Agitation**: CDST Aggression tool — de-escalation, then benzodiazepine sedation (\*midazolam 0.2 mg/kg IM, max 15 mg\*), or ^ketamine 4 mg/kg IM^ if midazolam unavailable (HPCSA PBEC, 2025, pp. 46–47). **Benzodiazepines are the treatment of choice** for stimulant toxicity because they reduce CNS excitation, sympathetic outflow, heart rate, blood pressure, seizures and hyperthermia. The RCEM (2025) notes that midazolam may be preferred where transient increases in tachycardia or hypertension would be particularly dangerous (for example possible cocaine-induced psychosis or ischaemic heart disease).
- **Hyperthermia** (temperature ≥38 °C): remove clothing, water spray and fan, or ambulance air-conditioning (HPCSA PBEC, 2025, p. 57); minimise physical struggle and restraint (which increases heat production).
- **Chest pain from suspected stimulant use**: aspirin 300 mg orally; ^diazepam 5 mg or midazolam 1 mg IV slowly^; ^isosorbide dinitrate 5 mg SL or GTN 400 mcg SL^; exclude a cardiac cause (Chest pain tool) (HPCSA PBEC, 2025, p. 57).
- **Widened QRS** (cocaine sodium-channel blockade): ^sodium bicarbonate 8.4% 100 mL IV over 3 min^ (child 2 mL/kg) (HPCSA PBEC, 2025, pp. 56–57).
- **Seizures**: CDST Seizures tool (midazolam 10 mg IM/buccal/IN or 10 mg IV in adults).
- IV fluids for dehydration and to protect the kidneys from rhabdomyolysis.
- Continuous ECG; monitor every 10 minutes; transport.

**Hospital**: further benzodiazepines; active cooling; avoid beta-blockers alone in cocaine toxicity (unopposed alpha stimulation is a traditional concern); phentolamine or nitrates for hypertension; management of rhabdomyolysis, hyponatraemia (MDMA), ACS and arrhythmias; chlorpromazine for serotonin toxicity with severe hyperthermia (RCEM, 2025); intubation and paralysis for refractory hyperthermia.

## 60.2 Unknown / mixed drug overdose

The general approach to the unknown and mixed overdose is described in detail in Outcomes 62.4 and 62.5. In summary:

**Pathophysiology**: the effects reflect the combined, sometimes synergistic (for example opioids plus benzodiazepines plus alcohol) and sometimes opposing (for example opioid plus stimulant) actions of multiple agents, with unpredictable and delayed toxicity (for example paracetamol hepatotoxicity, sustained-release cardiotoxicity).

**Presentation**: mixed or incomplete toxidromes; altered consciousness; unexplained arrhythmias, seizures, acidosis or hypotension. About half of ICU poisoning admissions involve more than one substance (Hüser et al., 2025).

**Children**: consider any medicine in the household, including grandparents' medicines (antihypertensives, oral hypoglycaemics, opioids); "one pill can kill" agents in toddlers include TCAs, calcium channel blockers, beta-blockers, sulphonylureas, opioids (including methadone), clonidine and antimalarials.

**Management**: ABCDE; glucose; empirical naloxone if an opioid picture with respiratory depression (titrated); benzodiazepines for seizures and agitation; sodium bicarbonate for widened QRS (ANT/ECP); no flumazenil; activated charcoal only if CDST criteria are met; ECG; Poisons Information Helpline; transport all unknown or mixed overdoses (HPCSA PBEC, 2025, p. 55); hospital measurement of paracetamol and salicylate levels in all deliberate overdoses.

## 60.3 Marijuana (cannabis)

**Agents**: herbal cannabis ("dagga", "weed"), resin (hashish), oils, edibles (cookies, sweets, cakes), vaping products, and **synthetic cannabinoids** ("spice", "K2", "Mojo"), which are much more potent full agonists. Cannabis was legalised for private adult use in South Africa following the Constitutional Court's 2018 judgment in *Minister of Justice and Constitutional Development v Prince*, and the Cannabis for Private Purposes Act 7 of 2024; the availability of edibles increases the risk of accidental ingestion by children.

**Pathophysiology**: Δ⁹-tetrahydrocannabinol (THC) is a partial agonist at cannabinoid CB1 receptors (CNS — mood, perception, memory, coordination, appetite) and CB2 receptors (immune). Inhaled THC acts within minutes and lasts two to four hours; ingested THC has a delayed onset (30 minutes to two hours) and prolonged effects (up to 12 hours or longer), so people may consume more while waiting for effects. Synthetic cannabinoids are full CB1 agonists with much greater toxicity.

**Clinical presentation**:

- **Adults**: euphoria, relaxation, altered time perception, impaired attention, memory and coordination, conjunctival injection, dry mouth, increased appetite, **tachycardia**, orthostatic hypotension; in higher doses or in naïve users, anxiety, panic, paranoia, psychosis and, rarely, chest pain and arrhythmias (myocardial infarction has been reported in young users). **Cannabinoid hyperemesis syndrome**: cyclical severe nausea, vomiting and abdominal pain in chronic heavy users, relieved by hot showers, which can cause dehydration and electrolyte disturbance.
- **Children** (usually edibles): drowsiness, ataxia, hypotonia, lethargy, mydriasis, tachycardia, and in larger ingestions coma, respiratory depression, apnoea and seizures.
- **Synthetic cannabinoids**: severe agitation, psychosis, seizures, tachycardia, hypertension, chest pain and myocardial ischaemia, hyperthermia, acute kidney injury and death.

**Prehospital management**: reassurance in a calm, quiet environment for anxiety and panic; ABCDE; glucose; airway support in children with depressed consciousness (lateral position, BVM if needed); benzodiazepines for severe agitation or seizures (CDST); IV fluids for dehydration from hyperemesis; consider co-ingestants (alcohol, methaqualone "white pipe"); transport children and anyone with significant symptoms. There is no antidote.

## 60.4 Hallucinogens

**Agents**: lysergic acid diethylamide (LSD), psilocybin ("magic mushrooms"), mescaline (peyote), dimethyltryptamine (DMT), MDMA (which has both stimulant and entactogenic effects), and dissociative agents (phencyclidine (PCP), ketamine, dextromethorphan in high doses). Plant-derived anticholinergic hallucinogens (*Datura stramonium*) are covered in 63.1.

**Pathophysiology**: classic hallucinogens (LSD, psilocybin, mescaline, DMT) are **serotonin 5-HT2A receptor agonists**, altering perception and cognition; dissociatives (PCP, ketamine) are **NMDA receptor antagonists**, producing a dissociative state, analgesia and sympathetic stimulation.

**Clinical presentation**:

- **Classic hallucinogens**: visual hallucinations and illusions, synaesthesia ("seeing sounds"), distorted perception of time and self, euphoria or intense fear ("bad trip"), panic, paranoia, impaired judgement (risk of trauma — falls, jumping from heights, walking into traffic); mild sympathomimetic signs (**mydriasis**, tachycardia, hypertension, hyperthermia, tremor, sweating). Life-threatening physical toxicity is uncommon with LSD and psilocybin alone.
- **PCP/ketamine**: dissociation, agitation, unpredictable violence, analgesia (patients may injure themselves without feeling pain), **horizontal, vertical and rotatory nystagmus**, hypertension, tachycardia, hyperthermia, rhabdomyolysis, seizures; with large doses, coma.
- **MDMA**: euphoria, bruxism, hyperthermia, serotonin toxicity, **hyponatraemia** (headache, vomiting, confusion, seizures, cerebral oedema), rhabdomyolysis, DIC.
- **Children**: accidental ingestions are rare but may cause agitation, hallucinations, seizures and hyperthermia.

**Prehospital management**:

- A calm, dim, quiet environment; "talking down" with reassurance; one provider; prevent injury.
- ABCDE; glucose; temperature.
- Benzodiazepine sedation per CDST for severe agitation or seizures.
- Cooling for hyperthermia.
- Consider hyponatraemia in MDMA users with seizures or confusion (avoid giving large volumes of hypotonic fluids).
- Look for trauma.
- Transport.

## 60.5 Sedatives and hypnotics

**Agents**: benzodiazepines (diazepam, lorazepam, alprazolam, clonazepam, midazolam, flunitrazepam), Z-drugs (zolpidem, zopiclone), barbiturates (phenobarbital), methaqualone ("Mandrax"), GHB/GBL, sedating antihistamines, and ethanol (60.29).

**Pathophysiology**: benzodiazepines and Z-drugs enhance the effect of **GABA at the GABA-A receptor** by increasing the **frequency** of chloride channel opening, causing neuronal hyperpolarisation and CNS depression; barbiturates increase the **duration** of channel opening and, at high doses, open the channel directly, which is why barbiturates cause profound respiratory and cardiovascular depression and have a much narrower safety margin. Alcohol, opioids and other CNS depressants act synergistically.

**Clinical presentation (sedative-hypnotic toxidrome)**: depressed level of consciousness, **slurred speech**, ataxia, nystagmus, and respiratory depression (HPCSA PBEC, 2025, p. 55), with vital signs often near normal in pure benzodiazepine overdose. Pupils are usually normal or small. Hypotension, hypothermia and coma occur with large doses, barbiturates or co-ingestants. Complications include aspiration pneumonia, rhabdomyolysis and pressure injuries from prolonged immobility, and **"coma blisters"** (bullae, classically with barbiturates). Pure benzodiazepine overdose is rarely fatal; deaths usually involve co-ingestion of alcohol or opioids. GHB causes abrupt coma with abrupt awakening, bradycardia, vomiting and myoclonus.

**Children**: more sensitive to respiratory depression; ataxia and drowsiness may be the first signs; paradoxical agitation can occur.

**Prehospital management**:

- ABCDE; airway positioning (lateral), suction, BVM ventilation if inadequate breathing; airway tool if unable to maintain the airway.
- Oxygen if SpO₂ <92%; glucose.
- Temperature (treat hypothermia; CDST cooling if ≥38 °C).
- If agitated (paradoxical reaction or withdrawal) — Aggression tool.
- **Flumazenil is not in the CDST** and is not recommended prehospitally. In hospital, flumazenil (titrated 0.1 mg/min to a maximum of 1 mg) may reverse CNS depression in selected pure benzodiazepine or Z-drug overdoses, but it is **contraindicated** in concomitant antidepressant (TCA) overdose, in patients with epilepsy or on antiepileptic drugs, and in benzodiazepine-dependent patients, because it can precipitate seizures and withdrawal (Hüser et al., 2025).
- Activated charcoal only if fully conscious within one hour (rarely appropriate, because consciousness may deteriorate).
- Hospital: supportive care; intubation if needed (Hüser et al., 2025, note that a conservative approach to intubation in comatose poisoned patients without respiratory failure, shock or vomiting reduced pneumonia and ICU admissions); MDAC and urinary alkalinisation may enhance phenobarbital elimination.
- Transport and monitor every 10 minutes.

## 60.6 Narcotics, opiates and opioids

**Definitions**: **opiates** are naturally occurring alkaloids derived from the opium poppy (morphine, codeine); **opioids** are all substances (natural, semi-synthetic and synthetic) that act on opioid receptors (heroin, oxycodone, fentanyl, methadone, tramadol, pethidine); **narcotic** is a legal rather than pharmacological term for controlled drugs.

**Pathophysiology**: agonism at **mu (μ) opioid receptors** in the CNS causes analgesia, euphoria, sedation, **respiratory depression** (reduced brainstem responsiveness to CO₂ and hypoxia — the cause of death), miosis (via the Edinger–Westphal nucleus), nausea, reduced gut motility, and histamine release (morphine). Death results from hypoventilation, hypoxia and aspiration. Some opioids have additional toxicity: **tramadol** (serotonergic and noradrenergic reuptake inhibition — seizures and serotonin syndrome; less miosis), **methadone** (very long half-life, QT prolongation and torsade de pointes), **pethidine** (serotonin syndrome, seizures from the norpethidine metabolite), **codeine** (variable conversion to morphine — ultra-rapid metabolisers are at risk), **loperamide** in massive doses (cardiotoxicity), and **fentanyl** (high potency, chest wall rigidity with rapid IV injection).

**Clinical presentation (opioid toxidrome)**: depressed level of consciousness, **pinpoint pupils**, **respiratory depression** (RR <12, shallow breathing, apnoea), bradycardia, hypotension and hypothermia (HPCSA PBEC, 2025, p. 55); cyanosis; non-cardiogenic pulmonary oedema; seizures (tramadol); signs of injection (track marks) or patches. Note that the pupils may be dilated if there is severe hypoxia or a co-ingested anticholinergic or stimulant.

**Children**: highly sensitive — a single tablet of methadone, buprenorphine or a fentanyl patch can be fatal for a toddler; codeine-containing cough syrups; children may present with lethargy, miosis, apnoea, and seizures; delayed and prolonged toxicity with methadone.

**Prehospital management (CDST)**:

- **Airway and ventilation first**: open the airway, BVM ventilation with oxygen (one breath every three to five seconds) — ventilation corrects hypoxia while naloxone is prepared and reduces the risk of pulmonary oedema and agitation on reversal.
- **Naloxone** if respiratory depression (HPCSA PBEC, 2025, p. 56):
  - adult: \*naloxone 0.4 mg IV/IM\*; if a known opioid abuser, \*0.04 mg IV/IM\* to limit withdrawal symptoms; reassess every two minutes; if the respiratory rate is still low, give **increasing doses of 0.8 mg, 2 mg, 4 mg** every two minutes up to a **maximum total of 10 mg**; if the respiratory rate remains low, consider other poisoning;
  - child: \*naloxone 0.1 mg/kg IV/IM, up to 2 mg\*, repeated every two minutes to a maximum cumulative dose of 10 mg;
  - the drug appendix also lists the intranasal route (HPCSA PBEC, 2025, p. 72).
- **Titrate to adequate breathing, not full consciousness**, to avoid precipitating acute withdrawal (agitation, vomiting with aspiration risk, combativeness, and rarely pulmonary oedema or arrhythmias). Hüser et al. (2025) suggest 0.05–0.1 mg IV every two minutes in hospital, with higher initial doses (0.2–0.4 mg) in pre-arrest patients.
- **Naloxone wears off quickly** (duration 30–90 minutes, shorter than most opioids, especially methadone and sustained-release preparations): monitor the respiratory rate closely and give further doses later if needed (HPCSA PBEC, 2025, p. 56). Patients who refuse transport after naloxone reversal are at risk of re-sedation; strongly encourage transport.
- Glucose; temperature; look for co-ingestion and trauma.
- In cardiac arrest, follow standard resuscitation; naloxone is not a priority over CPR and ventilation (Lavonas et al., 2023).
- Transport and monitor every 10 minutes.

## 60.7 Cardiac medications

The most important cardiac medications in overdose are **beta-blockers**, **calcium channel blockers**, **digoxin** (cardiac glycosides), and **other antihypertensives** (clonidine, ACE inhibitors, angiotensin receptor blockers, diuretics). Antiarrhythmics (flecainide — a sodium-channel blocker; amiodarone) are less common.

### Beta-blockers (propranolol, atenolol, metoprolol, carvedilol, sotalol)

- **Pathophysiology**: competitive antagonism of β1 receptors (reduced heart rate, AV conduction and contractility) and β2 receptors (bronchospasm, impaired glycogenolysis — hypoglycaemia, especially in children). **Propranolol** is lipophilic and has **sodium-channel-blocking** (membrane-stabilising) effects — causing QRS widening, seizures and coma; **sotalol** has potassium-channel-blocking effects (QT prolongation, torsade de pointes).
- **Presentation**: bradycardia, AV block, hypotension, cardiogenic shock, cardiac arrest; altered consciousness and seizures (propranolol); bronchospasm; **hypoglycaemia** (particularly children).

### Calcium channel blockers (verapamil, diltiazem — non-dihydropyridines; amlodipine, nifedipine — dihydropyridines)

- **Pathophysiology**: blockade of L-type calcium channels in the myocardium (reduced contractility and conduction — mainly verapamil and diltiazem), vascular smooth muscle (vasodilatation — mainly dihydropyridines), and pancreatic β-cells (reduced insulin release — **hyperglycaemia**). Verapamil and diltiazem are the most dangerous; sustained-release preparations cause delayed and prolonged toxicity.
- **Presentation**: hypotension, bradycardia (or reflex tachycardia with dihydropyridines), AV block, cardiogenic and vasodilatory shock, non-cardiogenic pulmonary oedema; mental status is often **preserved** until late (unlike beta-blocker toxicity); **hyperglycaemia** is a marker of severity.

### Digoxin and cardiac glycosides

- **Pathophysiology**: inhibition of the **Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase pump**, increasing intracellular calcium (increased contractility) and vagal tone (slowed AV conduction), with increased automaticity. In **acute** overdose, inhibition of the pump causes **hyperkalaemia** (a marker of severity and mortality). Plant cardiac glycosides (oleander, *Urginea*/*Drimia*, *Cotyledon*) cause the same syndrome (63.1).
- **Presentation**: nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain, confusion, **visual disturbances** (blurred vision, yellow-green halos — xanthopsia), fatigue; virtually **any arrhythmia** — bradycardia, AV block, ventricular ectopy, bigeminy, **paroxysmal atrial tachycardia with block**, bidirectional ventricular tachycardia, VF (Hüser et al., 2025); hyperkalaemia in acute toxicity. **Chronic toxicity** (more common in older people) is precipitated by renal impairment, hypokalaemia, dehydration and interacting drugs.

### Clonidine and other antihypertensives

- **Clonidine** (central α2 agonist) causes a picture resembling opioid toxicity: depressed consciousness, miosis, bradycardia, hypotension (sometimes transient initial hypertension), respiratory depression — particularly dangerous in children; it may partially respond to naloxone. Abrupt clonidine withdrawal causes rebound hypertension.
- **ACE inhibitors/ARBs**: hypotension, usually mild and responsive to fluids.
- **Diuretics**: dehydration and electrolyte disturbances.

### Children

Small numbers of tablets of calcium channel blockers, beta-blockers, clonidine or digoxin can be life-threatening in toddlers; children are particularly prone to hypoglycaemia with beta-blockers.

### Prehospital management (CDST)

- ABCDE; IV access; ECG monitoring and 12-lead ECG; glucose (hypoglycaemia in beta-blocker toxicity; hyperglycaemia in CCB toxicity).
- **Bradycardia**: follow the RCSA bradycardia algorithm (atropine, adrenaline infusion, pacing — though pacing often fails to improve blood pressure because the problem is contractility) (HPCSA PBEC, 2025, p. 57; Hüser et al., 2025).
- **Hypotension** (HPCSA PBEC, 2025, p. 57):
  1. crystalloid 250–500 mL IV over 5–10 min (child 10 mL/kg);
  2. if still hypotensive: ^calcium chloride 10% 10 mL IV over 10 min^ **or** ^calcium gluconate 10% 30 mL IV over 10 min^ under ECG monitoring (child: calcium chloride 10% 0.2 mL/kg or calcium gluconate 10% 0.6 mL/kg at 1 mL/min);
  3. in a child where beta-blocker poisoning is more likely: \*glucagon 100 mcg/kg IV over 1–2 min (not exceeding 1 mg/min)\*, repeated after 10 min if inadequate (the drug appendix lists glucagon IV for poisoning as within ECT, ANT and ECP scope; HPCSA PBEC, 2025, p. 69);
  4. if still hypotensive: ^adrenaline infusion^ — adult 1 mg in 50 mL (or 4 mg in 200 mL) normal saline, starting at 5 mL/h (1.6 mcg/min), target 6–30 mL/h (2–10 mcg/min), titrated by 1 mL/h every five minutes to SBP 90/MAP 65; child 0.06 mg/kg in 50 mL, starting at 1 mL/h (target 0.02–0.1 mcg/kg/min).
- **Digoxin toxicity**: the CDST has no specific prehospital antidote; treat arrhythmias per RCSA algorithms, avoid calcium (traditional concern about "stone heart" in digoxin toxicity, although recent evidence questions this), correct hypoxia, and transport urgently for **digoxin-specific antibody fragments (Fab)** in hospital.
- Contact the Poisons Information Helpline; transport all cardiac drug overdoses, even if asymptomatic (delayed toxicity with sustained-release preparations).

### Hospital management (Hüser et al., 2025)

- **High-dose insulin euglycaemia therapy (HIET)** for CCB and BB cardiogenic shock: insulin bolus 1 U/kg IV, then infusion 1 U/kg/h, titrated up to 10 U/kg/h, with dextrose (starting 0.5 g/kg) to maintain glucose >5.5 mmol/L and close potassium monitoring; onset may take 15–30 minutes, with the full effect taking hours; combine with vasopressors.
- **Glucagon** for beta-blocker toxicity: 50–150 mcg/kg IV bolus (typically 3–5 mg), then 2–5 mg/h infusion if responsive; evidence is weaker and vomiting is common.
- **Digoxin-specific Fab fragments**: for life-threatening arrhythmias or hyperkalaemia; with unknown amounts, two vials initially in acute poisoning or one vial in chronic poisoning, repeated as needed.
- **Sodium bicarbonate** for QRS widening (propranolol, flecainide).
- **Lipid emulsion** as a last resort for refractory toxicity from lipophilic drugs; **VA-ECMO** for refractory cardiogenic shock or arrest.
- Whole bowel irrigation for sustained-release preparations.

## 60.8 Organophosphates

**Agents**: organophosphate insecticides (for example chlorpyrifos, dimethoate, malathion, parathion), organophosphate nerve agents (sarin, VX — 60.32), and the closely related **carbamates** (aldicarb — "Two-step", methomyl, carbaryl). In South Africa, cholinesterase inhibitors are the most commonly ingested non-drug chemicals reported to the Tygerberg Poison Information Centre (8.8%), and aldicarb is a toxicovigilance target (Veale, Wium and Müller, 2013); street-sold aldicarb is an important cause of child deaths (Rother, 2010).

**Pathophysiology**: organophosphates **irreversibly** phosphorylate (inhibit) **acetylcholinesterase**, the enzyme that breaks down acetylcholine at synapses; over time the phosphorylated enzyme undergoes **"ageing"**, after which it cannot be reactivated by oximes. Carbamates inhibit acetylcholinesterase **reversibly**, so their toxicity is usually shorter, and they penetrate the CNS less. Acetylcholine accumulates at:

- **muscarinic receptors** (parasympathetic end-organs, sweat glands) → DUMBELS;
- **nicotinic receptors** at the neuromuscular junction and autonomic ganglia → fasciculations, muscle weakness and paralysis (including respiratory muscles), tachycardia, hypertension;
- **CNS receptors** → agitation, confusion, seizures, coma, central respiratory depression.

Death is usually from **respiratory failure** due to the combination of bronchorrhoea, bronchospasm, respiratory muscle weakness and central respiratory depression (Eddleston et al., 2008).

**Clinical presentation (cholinergic toxidrome)** (HPCSA PBEC, 2025, p. 55): depressed level of consciousness and seizures; **sweating**; **pinpoint pupils, watery eyes**; **bradycardia**; **drooling**; urinary incontinence; diarrhoea and vomiting; **airway secretions and crackles** on auscultation; plus fasciculations and weakness. Note that tachycardia and mydriasis may occur from nicotinic effects. A **garlic or petroleum odour** is common (from the solvent). **Intermediate syndrome** (24–96 hours after poisoning) causes proximal muscle, neck flexor, cranial nerve and respiratory muscle weakness, and can cause respiratory arrest after initial improvement; **organophosphate-induced delayed polyneuropathy** occurs weeks later.

**Children**: may present atypically with predominantly CNS signs (lethargy, coma, seizures, hypotonia) and fewer classic muscarinic signs; pinpoint pupils and secretions are important clues.

**Prehospital management (CDST)**:

1. **Scene safety and PPE** — gloves, apron, eye protection and mask; organophosphates are absorbed through skin, and vomitus and clothing are contaminated (HPCSA PBEC, 2025, p. 54; Kaswa, 2024). Ventilate the ambulance.
2. **Decontamination**: remove all clothing (bag it), wash the skin with soap and water, flush eyes.
3. **ABCDE**: suction secretions; oxygen; BVM ventilation if needed; airway tool if unable to maintain the airway — **avoid succinylcholine** for RSI (it is metabolised by plasma cholinesterase, which is also inhibited, causing prolonged paralysis) (HPCSA PBEC, 2025, p. 56).
4. **Atropine (ANT/ECP)** (HPCSA PBEC, 2025, p. 56):
   - adult: ^atropine 2 mg IV^; child: ^0.05 mg/kg IV^;
   - look for a response after five minutes: **clear lung sounds, dilated pupils, increased heart rate, dry mouth**;
   - if no response: ^double the previous atropine dose every three minutes until clinical improvement^;
   - when the patient responds: calculate the total amount of atropine given as boluses, and ^give 20% of this dose per hour via IV infusion^.
   - The goal of atropinisation is to dry secretions and reverse bronchorrhoea and bradycardia, **not** to normalise the pupils alone; the endpoints commonly used in the literature are a clear chest on auscultation, heart rate above 80/min, systolic BP above 80 mmHg, dry axillae and pupils no longer pinpoint (Eddleston et al., 2008). Very large doses (tens to hundreds of milligrams) may be needed. Tachycardia is **not** a contraindication to atropine when bronchorrhoea is present. Watch for atropine toxicity (agitation, hyperthermia, absent bowel sounds, urinary retention).
5. **Seizures**: benzodiazepines (CDST Seizures tool).
6. **Glucose**; IV fluids for hypotension.
7. Contact the Poisons Information Helpline; transport to an emergency centre (ICU likely).

**Hospital**: continued atropine titration; **oximes** (pralidoxime or obidoxime) to reactivate acetylcholinesterase before ageing — their benefit in organophosphate pesticide poisoning remains uncertain and availability varies (Eddleston et al., 2008); intubation and ventilation (avoiding succinylcholine); benzodiazepines; monitoring for intermediate syndrome.

## 60.9 Carbon monoxide

**Sources**: incomplete combustion of carbon-containing fuels — indoor coal, wood and charcoal braziers ("imbawulas"), paraffin heaters and stoves, gas geysers and heaters, generators, vehicle exhausts in enclosed spaces (including deliberate self-harm), and house and shack fires (often combined with cyanide). Methylene chloride (paint stripper) is metabolised to CO.

**Pathophysiology** (Rose et al., 2017):

- CO binds haemoglobin with about **200–250 times the affinity of oxygen**, forming **carboxyhaemoglobin (COHb)**, which reduces oxygen-carrying capacity;
- it **shifts the oxyhaemoglobin dissociation curve to the left**, impairing oxygen release to tissues;
- it binds **myoglobin** (cardiac and skeletal muscle dysfunction) and **cytochrome c oxidase** (impaired mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation);
- it causes **lipid peroxidation, inflammation and neuronal injury**, contributing to delayed neurological sequelae;
- the fetus is especially vulnerable (fetal haemoglobin binds CO even more avidly, and fetal COHb levels rise more slowly and fall more slowly).

The **half-life of COHb** is about four to six hours breathing room air, falls to about 60–90 minutes with 100% oxygen at normal pressure, and to about 20–30 minutes with hyperbaric oxygen (Rose et al., 2017).

**Clinical presentation**: non-specific — **headache** (most common), dizziness, nausea and vomiting, fatigue, confusion, "flu-like" illness in several members of a household (a key clue, especially in winter), chest pain, palpitations, dyspnoea; severe poisoning causes syncope, seizures, coma, myocardial ischaemia, arrhythmias, metabolic acidosis, pulmonary oedema and death. The "**cherry-red**" skin colour is a late and unreliable sign, usually post-mortem. **Pulse oximetry is falsely normal**, because standard two-wavelength oximeters cannot distinguish COHb from oxyhaemoglobin (a CO-oximeter is required). **Delayed neurological sequelae** (cognitive deficits, personality change, parkinsonism, memory impairment) may appear days to weeks after apparent recovery.

**Children and pregnancy**: children have higher metabolic and respiratory rates and may become symptomatic earlier; pregnant women are at high risk because of fetal vulnerability.

**Prehospital management**:

- **Scene safety**: do not enter an enclosed space with suspected CO without appropriate breathing apparatus (fire and rescue); ventilate; remove all patients to fresh air; consider multiple casualties (the whole household).
- **100% oxygen via a non-rebreather mask at 15 L/min** (or via BVM/advanced airway if needed) for all suspected CO poisoning, regardless of SpO₂ — the CDST Burns tool lists suspected carbon monoxide or cyanide poisoning (headache, dizziness, vomiting, chest pain, confusion) and fire in an enclosed space as indications for 100% non-rebreather oxygen (HPCSA PBEC, 2025, p. 34).
- Ignore the pulse oximetry reading for decision-making; use a CO-oximeter (SpCO) if available.
- Airway management for decreased consciousness; seizures per CDST; 12-lead ECG (ischaemia, arrhythmias).
- **Consider concomitant cyanide** poisoning in fire victims (60.11).
- Transport; pre-alert the emergency centre.

**Hospital**: continued 100% oxygen; COHb measurement; **hyperbaric oxygen therapy** may be considered for severe poisoning (loss of consciousness, neurological deficits, seizures, cardiovascular dysfunction, severe acidosis, very high COHb, or pregnancy), because a randomised trial showed reduced cognitive sequelae (Weaver et al., 2002), although access in South Africa is limited.

## 60.10 Chlorine gas

**Sources**: swimming pool chemicals (calcium hypochlorite), industrial accidents and water treatment plants, transport spills, and mixing household bleach (sodium hypochlorite) with acids (for example toilet cleaners) or ammonia (producing chloramine gas); chlorine has been used as a chemical weapon (60.32).

**Pathophysiology**: chlorine is a yellow-green gas with a pungent odour, heavier than air (it accumulates in low-lying areas). It has **intermediate water solubility**, so it injures both the upper and lower airways. On contact with moist mucosa it forms **hydrochloric acid and hypochlorous acid** and generates oxygen free radicals, causing direct cellular injury, inflammation, mucosal oedema, bronchospasm, loss of surfactant, and in severe exposures **acute lung injury and non-cardiogenic pulmonary oedema** (which may be delayed for hours) (Henretig, Kirk and McKay, 2019).

**Clinical presentation**: immediate burning of the eyes, nose and throat, lacrimation, rhinorrhoea, cough, chest tightness, dyspnoea, wheezing (bronchospasm), stridor (laryngeal oedema), hoarseness, nausea and vomiting; severe exposures cause hypoxia, pulmonary oedema, ARDS and death. Skin and eye burns may occur with high concentrations or liquid chlorine. People with asthma, COPD, children and older adults are most vulnerable.

**Prehospital management**:

- **Scene safety and HAZMAT**: approach from upwind and uphill; fire and rescue in appropriate PPE to remove patients; mass-casualty triage if multiple exposures.
- Remove the patient from exposure to fresh air; remove contaminated clothing; irrigate eyes and skin with copious water (HPCSA PBEC, 2025, p. 54).
- ABCDE; **high-flow oxygen**; position upright if conscious and dyspnoeic.
- **Bronchospasm**: salbutamol 5 mg nebulised (CDST, all categories) and ipratropium bromide 0.5 mg nebulised; consider hydrocortisone for severe bronchospasm per the CDST Breathing difficulty tool.
- **Stridor/upper airway oedema**: early airway management; ^nebulised adrenaline^ may be considered for upper airway involvement (as in the CDST Burns tool for inhalational injury); RSI by ECPs (Airway tool) for impending airway obstruction; Kaswa (2024) lists chlorine inhalation among exposures that may require rapid sequence intubation and ventilatory support.
- CPAP for pulmonary oedema if available and appropriate (Airway/Breathing tools).
- Transport all symptomatic patients and observe for delayed pulmonary oedema (hospital observation for several hours).
- **Hospital**: nebulised sodium bicarbonate has been used in some centres (limited evidence); supportive care; ventilation for ARDS.

## 60.11 Cyanide

**Sources**: **smoke inhalation in house fires** (from burning plastics, polyurethane, wool, silk and synthetic materials — the most common source in practice), industrial exposures (gold mining — cyanide is used extensively in gold extraction in South Africa; electroplating; metal cleaning; photography), deliberate ingestion of cyanide salts, **sodium nitroprusside** infusion, and plant sources (amygdalin in the kernels of apricots, peaches and bitter almonds; cassava).

**Pathophysiology**: cyanide binds the ferric (Fe³⁺) iron of **cytochrome c oxidase (cytochrome a3)** in the mitochondrial electron transport chain, **blocking aerobic respiration**. Cells cannot use oxygen despite adequate delivery, so the body switches to anaerobic metabolism, producing severe **lactic acidosis**. Tissues with high oxygen demand — the brain and heart — fail first. Because oxygen is not extracted, venous blood remains highly oxygenated ("arterialisation of venous blood") (Tintinalli et al., 2020).

**Clinical presentation**: rapid onset (seconds to minutes after inhalation, minutes to an hour after ingestion of salts, delayed after amygdalin): headache, anxiety, confusion, dizziness, dyspnoea and tachypnoea (early), tachycardia and hypertension (early), followed by **seizures, coma, apnoea, bradycardia, hypotension and cardiac arrest**; severe metabolic (lactic) acidosis; the classic "**bitter almond**" smell (which only some people can detect); skin may appear pink; **cyanosis is a late sign**. In fire victims, a lactate above about 8–10 mmol/L suggests significant cyanide toxicity (Borron et al., 2007).

**Children**: more vulnerable in house fires; the same presentation.

**Prehospital management**:

- **Scene safety**: fire and rescue with breathing apparatus; avoid mouth-to-mouth ventilation (use BVM); PPE and decontamination (remove clothing, wash skin) for salt or liquid exposure.
- **100% oxygen** (non-rebreather at 15 L/min or BVM/advanced airway) (HPCSA PBEC, 2025, p. 34); oxygen works synergistically with antidotes.
- ABCDE; seizures per CDST; treat shock; CPR per resuscitation guidelines.
- **Suspect combined CO and cyanide poisoning** in enclosed-space fire victims with altered consciousness, seizures, cardiovascular instability or severe acidosis.
- Transport rapidly; pre-alert the hospital for antidote availability.

**Antidotes (hospital — not CDST prehospital scope)**:

- **Hydroxocobalamin** (Cyanokit®) — the antidote of choice, especially in smoke inhalation: **5 g IV over 15 minutes in adults (70 mg/kg in children)**, repeated once if needed; it binds cyanide to form **cyanocobalamin (vitamin B12)**, which is excreted in the urine; side effects include red discoloration of the skin, mucosa and urine, hypertension, and interference with colorimetric laboratory tests (Borron et al., 2007).
- **Sodium thiosulfate** — a sulphur donor that enhances conversion of cyanide to thiocyanate by the enzyme rhodanese (slower onset; may be given with hydroxocobalamin).
- **Nitrites** (amyl nitrite, sodium nitrite) — induce methaemoglobinaemia, which binds cyanide; **avoided in smoke inhalation** because methaemoglobinaemia further reduces oxygen-carrying capacity in patients who may already have carboxyhaemoglobinaemia.

## 60.12 Caustic agents ingestion

*Note*: "caustic" and "corrosive" are often used interchangeably. In this guide, **60.12** focuses on **alkaline caustics** and the general approach, and **60.15** focuses on **acids** and other corrosives; the principles overlap.

**Agents**: alkalis (pH >11–12) — drain and oven cleaners (**sodium hydroxide**, potassium hydroxide), dishwasher detergents, cement and lime (calcium oxide/hydroxide), ammonia, button batteries (which generate hydroxide ions locally), and concentrated bleach. Common bleach (dilute sodium hypochlorite, 3–6%) is an irritant rather than a strong caustic and usually causes only minor injury unless ingested in large amounts.

**Pathophysiology**: alkalis cause **liquefactive necrosis** — saponification of fats and denaturation of proteins — which allows the alkali to **penetrate deeply** through tissue layers until neutralised. Injury is most severe in the **oesophagus** (alkalis are often tasteless and odourless, allowing larger volumes to be swallowed). Injury progresses over minutes to hours, with oedema, ulceration and necrosis, and complications including **airway oedema**, **oesophageal and gastric perforation** (mediastinitis, peritonitis), haemorrhage, and later **strictures** (weeks to months) and an increased lifetime risk of oesophageal carcinoma (Tintinalli et al., 2020).

**Clinical presentation**: oral pain, burns and blisters on the lips, tongue and pharynx, **drooling** and inability to swallow (dysphagia, odynophagia), vomiting (including haematemesis), chest or abdominal pain, **stridor, hoarseness, dyspnoea** (airway involvement — an emergency), and signs of perforation (severe chest/abdominal pain, subcutaneous emphysema, peritonism, shock). **The absence of oral burns does not exclude significant oesophageal injury.**

**Children**: usually accidental ingestion of small amounts (often from containers without child-resistant caps, or chemicals decanted into cold-drink bottles); drooling, refusal to feed, stridor and vomiting suggest significant injury. **Button batteries** lodged in the oesophagus can cause perforation and catastrophic aortoenteric fistula within hours and are a surgical emergency.

**Prehospital management**:

- Scene safety and PPE; remove contaminated clothing; irrigate skin and eyes copiously (alkali eye injuries require prolonged irrigation — CDST at least 20 minutes) (HPCSA PBEC, 2025, p. 54).
- **Airway**: assess for stridor, hoarseness, drooling and oropharyngeal oedema; give oxygen; early airway management by the most experienced provider if airway compromise is developing (RSI with a smaller tube; avoid blind nasal intubation; be prepared for a surgical airway). Kaswa (2024) notes that corrosive ingestion can compromise the respiratory tract and may require RSI and ventilatory support.
- **Do not induce vomiting** (re-exposure) (HPCSA PBEC, 2025, p. 54).
- **Do not give activated charcoal** (it does not bind caustics and obscures endoscopy) (HPCSA PBEC, 2025, p. 54).
- **Do not attempt neutralisation** with acids or alkalis (exothermic reaction causing thermal injury).
- **Dilution**: small sips of water may be considered only in an alert patient with no airway compromise, vomiting or signs of perforation, very soon after ingestion — but avoid large volumes (which can induce vomiting); many protocols advise nil by mouth, and the CDST advises avoiding milk. Follow local protocol.
- IV access, fluids for shock; analgesia (CDST Pain tool); antiemetic if within scope.
- Keep the container; transport to a facility with endoscopy and surgical capability.

**Hospital**: early endoscopy (within 12–24 hours) to grade injury; CT for suspected perforation; surgery for perforation or extensive necrosis; nutritional support; long-term follow-up for strictures.

## 60.13 Common household items ingestion

**Agents and their risks**:

| Household item | Main toxicity | Key points |
|---|---|---|
| **Paraffin, petrol, turpentine, lamp oil** | Aspiration pneumonitis | See 60.16 and 60.30 — the most important dangerous household poison in South African children |
| **Bleach** (sodium hypochlorite 3–6%) | Mucosal irritation, vomiting; rarely burns with concentrated products or large volumes; chlorine/chloramine gas if mixed with acids/ammonia | Usually minor; assess for burns and stridor |
| **Detergents, soaps, shampoos, dishwashing liquid** | Nausea, vomiting, diarrhoea; foaming and aspiration | Usually minor |
| **Liquid laundry detergent capsules ("pods")** | Vomiting, CNS depression, aspiration, airway injury, corneal injury | More toxic than conventional detergents in children |
| **Dishwasher detergents, drain cleaners, oven cleaners** | Caustic injury (alkali) | See 60.12 |
| **Toilet cleaners, descalers** | Corrosive (acid) | See 60.15 |
| **Button batteries** | Oesophageal burns and perforation within hours; aortoenteric fistula | Emergency: transport urgently for X-ray and endoscopic removal if in the oesophagus |
| **Magnets** (multiple) | Bowel perforation and fistula | Urgent surgical assessment |
| **Methylated spirits / methanol** | Toxic alcohol: CNS depression, metabolic acidosis, blindness | See 60.19/60.29 |
| **Antifreeze (ethylene glycol)** | Metabolic acidosis, renal failure | See 60.19 |
| **Mothballs** (naphthalene or paradichlorobenzene) | Naphthalene — haemolysis (especially in G6PD deficiency), methaemoglobinaemia, seizures | |
| **Rat poison** — anticoagulants ("superwarfarins") | Delayed coagulopathy (24–72 hours) | Single small ingestions in children are usually harmless; large or deliberate ingestions need INR monitoring and vitamin K |
| **Street "rat poison" / aldicarb ("Two-step")** | Cholinergic crisis | Life-threatening; see 60.8 |
| **Iron supplements** | See 60.25 | Dangerous in children |
| **Essential oils, camphor, eucalyptus oil, methyl salicylate (oil of wintergreen)** | Seizures (camphor, eucalyptus), aspiration, salicylate toxicity (wintergreen — a teaspoon can be lethal to a child) | |
| **Cosmetics, nail polish remover (acetone), hand sanitiser (ethanol/isopropanol)** | CNS depression, hypoglycaemia in children (ethanol) | |
| **Silica gel, candles, crayons, chalk, most plants** | Generally non-toxic | Reassurance (with poison centre confirmation) |
| **Cigarettes and nicotine liquid (e-cigarettes)** | Nicotine toxicity — vomiting, tachycardia then bradycardia, seizures, paralysis; e-liquids are highly concentrated | |
| **Medications** (grandparents' or parents') | See relevant sections | Child-resistant storage |

**Pathophysiology and presentation**: as above, depending on the agent — irritation, caustic injury, aspiration, CNS depression, metabolic toxicity.

**Children vs adults**: children ingest small amounts accidentally (most are minor, but some — paraffin, button batteries, caustics, iron, methyl salicylate, laundry pods, camphor, aldicarb, nicotine liquid — can be dangerous); adults usually ingest household products deliberately, in larger amounts.

**Prehospital management**:

- Identify the product (take the container; read the label and the Safety Data Sheet if available).
- ABCDE; airway assessment for burns and stridor; oxygen.
- **Do not induce vomiting; avoid milk** (HPCSA PBEC, 2025, p. 54).
- **Do not give activated charcoal for household products** (CDST exclusion) (HPCSA PBEC, 2025, p. 54).
- Skin and eye decontamination.
- Contact the **Poisons Information Helpline (0861 555 777)**: many household exposures in children can safely be managed at home on the advice of the poison centre; the CDST allows a patient who has ingested a non-toxic dose and is well to remain at home with monitoring (HPCSA PBEC, 2025, p. 55) — but not in deliberate ingestions, suspected abuse or neglect, or dangerous products.
- Transport symptomatic patients and those who ingested dangerous products.
- **Prevention advice**: store chemicals in original, labelled containers, out of reach of children, and never in cold-drink bottles.

## 60.14 Drugs abused for sexual purposes

This outcome covers two related areas: (1) **drug-facilitated sexual assault (DFSA)** — the use of drugs or alcohol to incapacitate a victim so that sexual assault can be committed; and (2) drugs used **recreationally to enhance sexual experience** ("chemsex" and aphrodisiacs), which can cause toxicity.

### Drug-facilitated sexual assault

**Agents**: **alcohol** is by far the most common agent, either alone or with other drugs; others include **benzodiazepines** (including flunitrazepam — "roofies" — midazolam, clonazepam), **Z-drugs** (zolpidem), **GHB/GBL**, **ketamine**, sedating antihistamines, opioids, **scopolamine** ("devil's breath", an anticholinergic), and cannabis (Tintinalli et al., 2020).

**Pathophysiology**: CNS depressants (GABAergic drugs, alcohol, opioids) cause sedation, disinhibition, muscle relaxation and **anterograde amnesia**; ketamine causes dissociation; scopolamine causes confusion, compliance and amnesia. Many of these drugs are colourless, tasteless and odourless when added to drinks, act quickly, and have short half-lives, so they may be undetectable within hours (GHB within about 6–12 hours in blood and 12 hours in urine).

**Clinical presentation**: sudden, unexpected intoxication out of proportion to the alcohol consumed; confusion, drowsiness, dizziness, slurred speech, ataxia, nausea, impaired vision; loss of consciousness; **amnesia** for a period of time; waking in an unfamiliar place with disarranged clothing, genital soreness or injuries; respiratory depression and bradycardia (GHB); hallucinations (ketamine). The victim may present hours later with no memory of events.

**Legal significance**: under the SOA, a person in an "altered state of consciousness, including under the influence of any medicine, drug, alcohol or other substance" who is incapable of appreciating the nature of the sexual act cannot consent (s 1(3)(d)(iii)); see Outcome 54.

**Prehospital management**:

- ABCDE (airway protection, ventilation, glucose); naloxone if opioid toxicity is suspected (CDST); supportive care.
- Treat as a potential sexual assault: trauma-informed care, evidence preservation (53.3) — in particular, **collect the first urine passed** in a clean container if the patient needs to urinate (drug testing), and preserve vomitus and any drink containers.
- Inform SAPS (with the survivor's consent for adults; mandatory for children and vulnerable persons — SOA s 54).
- Transport to a TCC or designated facility promptly (PEP within 72 hours; early blood and urine samples for toxicology).

### Drugs used to enhance sexual activity ("chemsex" and aphrodisiacs)

| Agent | Toxicity |
|---|---|
| **Methamphetamine, cocaine, MDMA, mephedrone** | Sympathomimetic toxicity (60.1); high-risk sexual behaviour; HIV and STI transmission |
| **GHB/GBL** | Rapid coma, respiratory depression, bradycardia, vomiting and aspiration; severe withdrawal syndrome in dependent users (agitation, delirium, seizures) |
| **Alkyl nitrites ("poppers" — amyl/butyl nitrite)** | Vasodilatation, hypotension, headache, syncope; **methaemoglobinaemia** (cyanosis unresponsive to oxygen, "chocolate-brown" blood; Hüser et al., 2025, note SpO₂ around 85% despite oxygen); dangerous hypotension with PDE-5 inhibitors |
| **PDE-5 inhibitors (sildenafil, tadalafil)** | Hypotension, especially with nitrates (GTN, isosorbide dinitrate, poppers) — **never give nitrates to a patient who has taken a PDE-5 inhibitor recently** (24 hours for sildenafil, 48 hours for tadalafil); priapism |
| **Cantharidin ("Spanish fly")** | Severe mucosal blistering, GI haemorrhage, renal failure |
| **Yohimbine** and some herbal "sex enhancers" | Sympathomimetic effects; adulteration with undeclared PDE-5 inhibitors |

**Prehospital management**: supportive ABCDE care; benzodiazepines for stimulant toxicity; airway support in GHB coma (patients often wake abruptly and may become agitated); oxygen for methaemoglobinaemia (hospital antidote: methylene blue 1–2 mg/kg IV; Hüser et al., 2025); **avoid nitrates** if PDE-5 inhibitors have been used; fluids for hypotension; transport.

## 60.15 Corrosive ingestion

**Agents**: **acids** (pH <2) — toilet bowl cleaners and descalers (hydrochloric acid), battery acid (sulphuric acid), rust removers (phosphoric, oxalic and hydrofluoric acids — 60.24), swimming pool acids (hydrochloric acid, "spirits of salts"), metal-cleaning products, and some traditional or industrial chemicals; also strong alkalis (60.12) and oxidising agents.

**Pathophysiology**: acids cause **coagulation necrosis** — protein denaturation forms a protective **eschar** that limits deeper penetration. Because acids are painful and bitter on contact, smaller volumes are usually swallowed. Acids pass rapidly through the oesophagus and cause the most severe injury in the **stomach** (especially the antrum and pylorus, where pooling occurs), with risk of gastric perforation, haemorrhage and later gastric outlet obstruction. **Systemic absorption** of acids can cause metabolic acidosis, haemolysis, renal failure and DIC; hydrofluoric acid causes life-threatening hypocalcaemia and hypomagnesaemia (60.24) (Tintinalli et al., 2020).

**Comparison of acid and alkali injury**:

| Feature | Acid | Alkali |
|---|---|---|
| Type of necrosis | Coagulation (eschar limits depth) | Liquefaction (deep penetration) |
| Taste | Painful, bitter — smaller volumes | Often tasteless — larger volumes |
| Main site of injury | Stomach | Oesophagus |
| Systemic effects | Acidosis, haemolysis, renal failure | Less common |
| Long-term | Gastric outlet obstruction | Oesophageal strictures, carcinoma |

**Clinical presentation**: oral and pharyngeal burns, pain, drooling, dysphagia, odynophagia, vomiting and haematemesis, epigastric pain, stridor and dyspnoea (airway oedema or aspiration), signs of perforation (peritonitis, mediastinitis, shock), and metabolic acidosis (Kussmaul breathing) in severe cases.

**Children**: usually accidental, small volumes; airway compromise and drooling indicate significant injury.

**Prehospital management**: as for caustics (60.12):

- PPE; decontaminate skin and eyes (irrigate ≥20 min) (HPCSA PBEC, 2025, p. 54).
- Airway assessment and early definitive airway if compromise (Kaswa, 2024).
- **No induced vomiting, no activated charcoal, no neutralisation, avoid milk** (HPCSA PBEC, 2025, p. 54); nil by mouth (or minimal water per local protocol).
- IV access, fluids for shock, analgesia.
- For hydrofluoric acid, see 60.24 (calcium).
- Transport with the container to a facility with endoscopy and surgery.

## 60.16 Petroleum products ingestion/inhalation

**Agents**: **paraffin (kerosene)** — the most important in South Africa; petrol (gasoline); diesel; lamp oil; lighter fluid; turpentine and mineral spirits; paint thinners; mineral oil and lubricants; and the volatile aromatic and halogenated hydrocarbons used in inhalant abuse (toluene in glue and paint, butane, propane, trichloroethane). Hydrocarbons are discussed more broadly in 60.30.

**Pathophysiology**:

- **Aspiration pneumonitis** is the main danger after ingestion. Hydrocarbons with **low viscosity**, **low surface tension** and **high volatility** (paraffin, petrol, turpentine, lamp oil) spread readily across the airway and into the lungs, even when only a small amount is swallowed, especially if the patient coughs, gags or vomits. They dissolve and inactivate **surfactant**, causing alveolar collapse, direct inflammation and capillary injury, haemorrhagic pulmonary oedema, ventilation–perfusion mismatch and hypoxia. Pneumatoceles and pneumothorax may develop. GI absorption of most aliphatic hydrocarbons such as paraffin is limited, so systemic toxicity after ingestion is usually less important than pulmonary toxicity.
- **CNS effects**: inhaled volatile hydrocarbons are highly lipid-soluble and cause intoxication (euphoria, disinhibition, slurred speech, ataxia), then CNS depression, seizures and coma; chronic use causes leukoencephalopathy, cerebellar damage and peripheral neuropathy (n-hexane).
- **Cardiac sensitisation** (halogenated and aromatic hydrocarbons): the myocardium is sensitised to catecholamines, causing **ventricular arrhythmias and "sudden sniffing death"**, typically when the user is startled or exerts themselves after inhaling.
- **Skin**: chemical burns and dermatitis with prolonged contact.

**Clinical presentation**:

- **After ingestion/aspiration**: coughing, choking and gagging at the time of ingestion (suggesting aspiration), followed by tachypnoea, dyspnoea, wheeze, crackles, grunting, cyanosis and hypoxia; respiratory symptoms may be delayed up to six hours. Fever is common (chemical pneumonitis, not necessarily infection). The breath smells of paraffin or petrol. Vomiting; drowsiness and occasionally seizures.
- **After inhalation (volatile substance abuse)**: "huffing" (soaked rag), "bagging" (bag over the face) or "sniffing"; perioral rash or paint around the mouth and nose ("glue sniffer's rash"), chemical odour, intoxication, ataxia, hallucinations, CNS depression, seizures, arrhythmias and sudden death.

**Children**: paraffin is one of the most common causes of poisoning in South African children (24% of incidents at Red Cross War Memorial Children's Hospital; Balme et al., 2012), usually from cold-drink bottles; toddlers are at high risk of aspiration pneumonitis. Volatile substance abuse is common among adolescents and street children.

**Prehospital management**:

- Scene safety (flammable and toxic vapours); remove contaminated clothing and wash skin with soap and water (HPCSA PBEC, 2025, p. 54).
- ABCDE; **oxygen** to maintain SpO₂ >92% (or as per CDST indications); position upright if conscious and breathing is laboured.
- **Do not induce vomiting** (increases aspiration) and **do not give activated charcoal** (CDST excludes paraffin and petrol; charcoal does not bind them and increases the risk of vomiting and aspiration) (HPCSA PBEC, 2025, p. 54).
- Do not give milk or oil.
- Bronchospasm: salbutamol nebulised (CDST Breathing difficulty tool).
- Airway support and ventilation for respiratory failure (CPAP or intubation per Airway tool).
- **Volatile substance abuse**: **keep the patient calm and avoid exertion or startling** (catecholamine surge can trigger fatal arrhythmias); **avoid adrenaline** where possible except in cardiac arrest (sensitised myocardium); continuous ECG monitoring.
- Seizures per CDST; glucose.
- Transport: all symptomatic children and adults; asymptomatic children who coughed or choked after ingestion should be observed in hospital for at least six hours (chest X-ray), because pneumonitis may develop later.
- **Hospital**: supportive respiratory care; antibiotics are not routinely indicated (fever is from chemical pneumonitis); steroids are not recommended.

### High-yield points — 60.1 to 60.16

- **Stimulants**: benzodiazepines are first-line (CDST midazolam); cool; chest pain → aspirin, benzodiazepine, nitrate; widened QRS → bicarbonate.
- **Opioids**: CDST naloxone 0.4 mg (0.04 mg in known abusers), escalating 0.8/2/4 mg to 10 mg total; child 0.1 mg/kg; titrate to breathing; naloxone wears off quickly.
- **Sedatives**: supportive; no prehospital flumazenil.
- **Cardiac drugs**: fluids → calcium (chloride 10 mL or gluconate 30 mL of 10%) → glucagon (child BB) → adrenaline infusion; hospital HIET; digoxin Fab.
- **Organophosphates**: PPE, decontaminate, atropine 2 mg (child 0.05 mg/kg) doubling every 3 min until chest clear, then 20% of total per hour; no succinylcholine.
- **CO**: 100% O₂; SpO₂ falsely normal; COHb half-life 4–6 h on air, 60–90 min on 100% O₂.
- **Cyanide**: fires and mining; lactic acidosis; 100% O₂; hydroxocobalamin 5 g IV (70 mg/kg child) in hospital.
- **Chlorine**: upwind, fresh air, O₂, bronchodilators, airway, delayed pulmonary oedema.
- **Caustics/corrosives**: alkali = liquefaction, oesophagus; acid = coagulation, stomach; no vomiting, no charcoal, no neutralisation; airway.
- **Household items**: call 0861 555 777; button batteries and paraffin are emergencies; no charcoal for household products.
- **DFSA**: alcohol most common; GHB, benzodiazepines; collect first urine; SOA consent provisions.
- **Petroleum**: aspiration pneumonitis; no vomiting, no charcoal; observe ≥6 h; sudden sniffing death — avoid startling and adrenaline.
